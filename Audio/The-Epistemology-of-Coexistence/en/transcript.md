# The Epistemology of Coexistence

Someone can explain the importance of trust and still break a promise. Someone else may keep a promise through habit without understanding what makes it worth keeping. What would it mean to know a relationship well enough for that understanding to guide living?

Our study, The Epistemology of Coexistence, examines knowledge, the knower, and what can be known. Epistemology asks how knowledge is possible and how a claim to know can be assessed. Madhyasth Darshan gives these questions a practical reach: understanding should become evident in thought, relationships, work, and the ability to help another person understand.

It identifies three connected contents of complete knowledge: existence as coexistence, jeevan as the sentient self, and humane conduct. The knower is jeevan; the embodied human being expresses understanding through speech, action, and participation. What is known includes the reality of nature and the relationships in which human beings live.

Complete knowledge here concerns this fundamental understanding. It does not mean possessing every fact or predicting every future event. Its purpose is resolution: an understanding that can guide life without the contradictions that arise when aims, beliefs, and conduct pull in different directions.

The study follows a connected movement from knowing to accepting, recognising, and fulfilling. A person first examines what is proposed, comes to accept what is understood, recognises the relationships and responsibilities involved, and acts to fulfil them. Evaluation then asks whether the understanding and action were adequate. The result matters for what happens next.

Consider a family's decision about food. Taste, health, and cost all matter. Understanding also asks whether needs are being met, whether labour has been treated fairly, and whether production sustains the conditions of future life. After acting, the family examines the result. A pleasant meal may be poorly nourishing; an inexpensive purchase may conceal an unfair arrangement. Evaluation can reveal what needs correction.

Madhyasth Darshan connects this process with three forms of evidence: realisation within the knower, fulfilment in human relationships, and practical work with nature. Study, reflection, and practice are meant to join these. A teacher presents a proposal and gives living expression to it; the learner must understand and examine its meaning. Repetition of the teacher's words is insufficient.

This is also why transmission matters. Understanding is expected to be communicable. It should help others inquire, act, evaluate, and develop their own understanding. Education carries both an account of reality and the capacity to examine it in living.

The comparisons sharpen this proposal. Advaita Vedanta distinguishes ordinary knowledge from liberating knowledge of the Self as Brahman. Modern philosophy examines justification, reliable ways of forming beliefs, lived experience, and the social conditions of knowing. Science develops public procedures for testing explanations and correcting error. Each brings resources for asking when confidence is warranted.

One difficult question runs through our study: how can a community distinguish realised understanding from agreement with a respected teacher? If the teaching identifies the awakened person, and that person alone validates the teaching, correction becomes difficult. Claims must remain open to disagreement and to evidence of failure, including the experience of people affected by a person's conduct.

Evidence must also fit the claim. Just conduct can support a claim about relationships without establishing jeevan's proposed physical constitution. Successful production does not establish every claim about consciousness. The study treats this distinction as a condition for meaningful comparison.

Its central contribution is the connection between knowing and responsible participation. The full paper develops that connection while examining how its errors can be recognised and corrected. The next study asks what evaluation is directed towards: what has value, and what would make its recognition and fulfilment correct? That is the question of Axiology: Value Theory.
