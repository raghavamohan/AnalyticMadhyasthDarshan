# The Epistemology of Coexistence

Someone can explain the importance of trust and still break a promise. Someone else may keep a promise through habit without understanding what makes it worth keeping. A third person may hold firm beliefs about what relationships require and have never examined them. What would it mean to know a relationship well enough for that understanding to guide living?

Our study, The Epistemology of Coexistence, examines knowledge, the knower, and what can be known. Epistemology asks how knowledge is possible and how a claim to know can be assessed. Madhyasth Darshan gives these questions a practical reach: understanding should become evident in thought, relationships, work, and teaching.

It identifies three connected contents of complete knowledge: existence as coexistence, jeevan as the sentient self, and humane conduct. The knower is jeevan; the embodied human being expresses understanding through speech, action, and participation. Madhyasth Darshan holds that this fundamental structure can be fully understood and taught, without a remainder of mystery.

Complete knowledge here concerns this fundamental understanding, not possession of every fact or prediction of every event. Its purpose is resolution: an understanding that can guide life without the contradictions that arise when aims, beliefs, and conduct pull in different directions.

The study follows a connected movement from knowing to accepting, recognising, and fulfilling. A person examines what is proposed, accepts what is understood, recognises the relationships and responsibilities involved, and acts to fulfil them. The discipline is stated as believing what is known, and knowing what is believed. The unawakened human reverses it, accepting first and never examining. Evaluation then asks whether understanding and action were adequate.

The study names three practices through which a proposal is tested. Studying the definitions until their meaning is clear. Applying justice in family and working relationships and checking whether both sides are satisfied. And working productively with nature, where physical laws are met directly. An understanding that survives all three is different from one that survives only in conversation.

Madhyasth Darshan connects these practices with three forms of evidence: realisation within the knower, fulfilment in human relationships, and practical work with nature. Nagraj's founding question was whether proof lies in a text or in the person who lives by it. Madhyasth Darshan answers that the proof is carried by a living person, in understanding and conduct. A teacher presents a proposal and gives living expression to it; the learner must examine its meaning, not repeat the words.

Understanding is also expected to be communicable: it should help others inquire, act, evaluate, and develop their own understanding.

The comparisons sharpen this proposal. Madhyasth Darshan separates knowledge of what is, wisdom about the human goal, and science as the means of achieving it; science cut off from the other two, it holds, drifts into consumption and destruction. Advaita Vedanta distinguishes ordinary knowledge from liberating knowledge of the Self as Brahman. Modern philosophy examines justification, lived experience, and the social conditions of knowing. The sciences supply the strongest public procedures for testing explanations and correcting error.

One difficult question runs through our study: how can a community distinguish realised understanding from agreement with a respected teacher? If the teaching identifies the awakened person, and that person alone validates the teaching, correction becomes difficult. Claims must remain open to disagreement and to evidence of failure, including the experience of people affected by a person's conduct.

Evidence must also fit the claim. Just conduct can support a claim about relationships without establishing jeevan's proposed physical constitution. Successful production does not establish every claim about consciousness.

The study's central contribution is the connection between knowing and responsible participation. The full paper develops that connection while examining how its errors can be recognised and corrected. The next study asks what evaluation is directed towards: what has value, and what makes it definite rather than a matter of preference? That is the question of Axiology: Value Theory.
