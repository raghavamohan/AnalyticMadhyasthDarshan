# How Undivided Society Is Established

A neighbourhood can share a water source while its families differ in language, belief, and income. Living together requires more than proximity, and it becomes harder when each group organises trust inside itself and caution toward the others, so that responsibility ends at the group's edge. What would it take for human relationship itself to be the boundary of responsibility?

Our study, How Undivided Society Is Established, examines Madhyasth Darshan's answer. Undivided society does not mean a single state, one culture, or one community enlarged until it includes everyone. It means recognising and fulfilling human relationships across the boundaries of smaller groups, while families, cultures, and local institutions remain distinct. Can those differences become forms of participation in a shared human good?

Madhyasth Darshan grounds this possibility in coexistence. Persons and relationships are real, and human beings can understand and evaluate their participation. Yet possibility is not achievement. The darshan traces division to taking the body to be the whole self. Desire then has no criterion of enough; wealth becomes accumulation rather than assurance; insecurity justifies hoarding and control; and inherited affiliation hardens into an identity defended against others.

The proposal gives social fulfilment four connected goals. Resolution is clarity in the individual. Prosperity is the family's assurance of having or producing more than its assessed needs; non-accumulation is expected to follow from that assurance, not from praising deprivation. Fearlessness means trust in social relationships. Coexistence is responsible participation with other people and the rest of nature.

The path begins with understanding and becomes concrete in family life, where care, guidance, resources, work, and education meet. Justice requires recognising relationships, fulfilling their values, evaluating what has happened, and reaching mutual satisfaction. Law can protect the conditions for justice and settle disputes, but compliance alone does not establish mutual satisfaction. A family's prosperity depends on understanding needs and developing the competence to meet them; sharing responsibilities and examining results become part of children's education.

Families then participate in wider assemblies through shared purposes and responsibilities, while their members remain individual agents. The study asks how institutions can maintain just relationships and pass on their methods as membership changes. A group held together by fear may persist, but persistence alone does not establish humane orderliness.

Five dimensions give the proposal institutional form: education, which develops understanding and humane competence; justice and security; health and restraint; production and work; and exchange and reserves. They need to work together and attend to nature's regenerative conditions.

What do other approaches contribute? Science offers indispensable ways to investigate consequences and correct mistakes, but it does not choose the human end. Advaita Vedanta can loosen possessiveness and narrow identity, though it does not specify this institutional programme. Hindu religious traditions offer diverse resources for care and community; their different authorities cannot be assumed to bind everyone.

The hardest question is transition. Existing societies contain unequal understanding, conflicting interests, and inherited institutions. The study proposes testing limited practices in families, schools, workplaces, and local councils, with the people affected helping to evaluate them. Protective law, freedom of conscience, and safeguards against abuse remain necessary, and participation should be open across philosophical commitments.

These suggestions are work to develop and test; the study does not claim that the full programme already operates at scale. It asks how resolution, sufficiency, trust, and ecological responsibility could be assessed without relying on a leader's self-certification. Failures must be open to correction.

The whole path turns on one reversal: from acting first and learning only from consequences, to understanding, acting, and evaluating in a continuing cycle. This completes the five questions of our Start here path: the human being, existence, knowledge, value, and living together. The final invitation is practical and open: examine whether understanding becomes dependable care, useful work, just institutions, and an education others can carry forward. The full study develops that proposal and the problems still to be answered.
