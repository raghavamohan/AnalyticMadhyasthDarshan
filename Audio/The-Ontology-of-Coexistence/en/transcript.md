# The Ontology of Coexistence

What exists? Did it begin at some time? Is the world we live in finally real, or a passing appearance? And where does a sentient being stand within it? Our study, The Ontology of Coexistence, takes up these questions.

Ontology is the study of what exists. Madhyasth Darshan answers through coexistence: formless Omnipresence, called satta, and countless bounded, active units of nature are always present together. Neither is produced from the other. Existence does not begin from nothing and does not pass into nothing; what changes is the arrangement of units, not the fact of existence. The texts put it in one sentence: Brahma is truth, the world is perpetual. The world and its distinct participants retain their reality.

Satta is all-pervasive and unchanging. Units change, combine, separate, and participate in relationships. The term saturation names their inseparability from this pervasive ground. Satta is actionless; the knowing activity of a sentient being belongs to jeevan. That distinction matters when words such as consciousness or space appear in translations.

The account asks us to understand each unit through four connected aspects: its form, its properties, its essential nature, and its dharma. In this context, dharma means its inseparable innateness and fulfilment. The texts illustrate this with a tree. Its form is its bounded organisation of roots, trunk, leaves, and seed. Its properties are the effects it has in its surroundings. Its essential nature is its characteristic usefulness, which can nourish one living body and harm another. Its dharma is existence and growth. The tree illustrates the four aspects; it is not a proof of satta.

Madhyasth Darshan distinguishes four orders of nature: material, biological, animal, and human, also called the knowledge order. Material and biological compositions provide bodies and environments. Animal and human living are understood as joint forms of body and jeevan, the sentient self. The human possibility includes knowing, evaluating, and consciously fulfilling relationships.

The formation of increasingly complex bodies and the proposed development of jeevan are different processes in this philosophy. Jeevan is described as an atom that has reached constitutional completeness. Awakening concerns the further development of its understanding and conduct: the capacity to know is not understanding already achieved.

This is why the account of existence reaches into everyday life. Human beings can misunderstand their needs, pursue gain at another's expense, or learn to act through justice. Awakening is evidenced as resolution in the person, prosperity in the family, trust in society, and responsible participation with nature.

The study compares this picture with Advaita Vedanta, modern philosophy, and science. Advaita gives ultimate reality to non-dual Brahman and understands the world's plurality as dependent. Madhyasth Darshan preserves the final reality of distinct units and relationships. Physicalist philosophy locates mind in the organisation of the brain and asks what a separate jeevan would explain that the organised body cannot. The sciences agree with Madhyasth Darshan that the world exists independently of any observer, but they have no procedure for identifying a constitutionally complete atom.

The distinction between a coherent account and evidence for its claims remains central. The study finds a connected framework in coexistence, but public identification of a constitutionally complete jeevan, its relation to the brain, and its persistence after bodily death remain unresolved. A successful practice of humane living would matter; those further claims would still need evidence of their own.

The world's perpetuity also does not make every particular thing permanent. A tree can die and a body can decompose while their constituents enter other relationships. The claim about jeevan's continuing identity depends on its account of constitutional completeness.

The full study develops these distinctions and comparisons. Its invitation is to examine an existence in which individuality and mutuality belong together. That raises the next question: how could this account be known, and how would understanding be distinguished from conviction? The Epistemology of Coexistence takes up that inquiry.
