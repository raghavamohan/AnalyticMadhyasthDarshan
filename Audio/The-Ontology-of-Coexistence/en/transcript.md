# The Ontology of Coexistence

What exists? Did it begin at some time? Is the world we live in finally real? And where does a sentient being stand within it? After considering the human being, The Ontology of Coexistence widens the inquiry to existence itself.

Ontology is the study of what exists. Madhyasth Darshan answers through coexistence: formless Omnipresence, called satta, and countless bounded, active units of nature are inseparably present together. Neither is produced from the other. Existence does not begin from nothing or pass into nothing. The texts put it in one sentence: Brahma is truth, the world is perpetual. This does not make every form permanent. A tree can die and a body decompose while their constituents enter other relationships.

Satta is all-pervasive and unchanging. Units change, combine, separate, and participate in relationships. Saturation names their inseparability from this pervasive ground: it permeates every unit and surrounds it. This bond grounds their energy, activity, mutual recognition, and orderly participation. In insentient nature, recognition means definite responsiveness. Satta is actionless; knowing belongs to jeevan, the sentient self. Even in realisation, the direct knowing of coexistence, the knower remains distinct from the ground it knows.

The account asks us to understand each unit through four connected aspects. A tree makes these concrete. Its form is its bounded organisation of roots, trunk, leaves, and seed. Its properties are its powers to produce effects through interaction. Its essential nature is the usefulness of those effects: a substance may nourish one body and harm another. Dharma means what is innate to a unit and fulfilled through its activity; for the tree, existence and growth. The example illustrates the account; it does not prove satta.

Madhyasth Darshan distinguishes four orders of nature: material, biological, animal, and human, also called the knowledge order. Material and biological compositions provide bodies and environments. Animal and human living join a body with jeevan. The human body provides the medium for knowing, evaluating, and consciously fulfilling relationships.

The formation of bodies and the proposed development of jeevan are different processes. Jeevan is described as an atom that has reached constitutional completeness: it no longer gains or loses constituent particles, while sentient activity continues. This is the philosophy's basis for its claimed persistence after bodily death. Awakening develops its understanding and conduct; having the capacity to know does not mean having understood.

This is why the account of existence reaches into everyday life. Human beings seek continuing happiness, yet can misunderstand their needs or pursue gain at another's expense. Awakening brings clarity into conduct: resolution in the person, prosperity in the family, trust in society, and mutually fulfilling participation with nature.

The study compares this picture with Advaita Vedanta, modern philosophy, and science. Advaita gives ultimate reality to non-dual Brahman; the world's plurality remains valid in everyday life but has no independent absolute being. Madhyasth Darshan preserves distinct units and relationships at the deepest understanding. Physicalist accounts ground mind in physical organisation; other philosophies offer different accounts of experience and selfhood. Scientific realism shares the commitment to a mind-independent world. Science itself tests models without settling one complete philosophy of existence.

The distinction between coherence and evidence remains central. Public identification of a constitutionally complete jeevan, its relation to the brain, and its persistence after bodily death remain unresolved. Humane living matters as evidence of understanding; these further claims still need evidence of their own.

The full study develops these distinctions and comparisons. Its invitation is to examine an existence in which individuality and mutuality belong together. That raises the next question: how could this account be known, and how would understanding be distinguished from conviction? The Epistemology of Coexistence takes up that inquiry.
