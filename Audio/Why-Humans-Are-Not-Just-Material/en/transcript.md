# Why Humans Are Not Just Material

A good meal can satisfy hunger. It cannot repair a broken friendship or answer the feeling that something important is missing from life. A person may also accept discomfort to keep a promise, care for someone, or discover what is true. What do these differences tell us about a human being?

Our study, Why Humans Are Not Just Material, begins with this question. It examines whether the organised life of the body and brain can fully explain conscious experience, understanding, and our ability to judge reasons and values. It compares three answers: Madhyasth Darshan, a physicalist interpretation of science, and Advaita Vedanta.

Madhyasth Darshan, presented by Shri A. Nagraj, understands the human being as body and jeevan acting together. Jeevan names the sentient self: the one who experiences, understands, evaluates, and seeks happiness. The body supplies the senses, brain, and means of acting. Human fulfilment involves both bodily provision and understanding how to live in relationships.

On this account, hunger and rest have recurring bodily requirements. The aspiration for continuous happiness concerns the quality and coherence of living. Pleasure, health, and material provision matter, but they need guidance from an understanding of justice and truth. A gain that damages a relationship may leave the person less fulfilled, even when it increases possessions.

The study develops a deeper question about experience itself. A description of pain can explain how an injury produces signals, movement, learning, and a report. Why is that activity also felt? Why does it hurt? And how do a friend's voice, expression, and our concern for them belong together within one experience? Madhyasth Darshan proposes an individual jeevan as the bearer of that experience and understanding.

The physicalist answer deserves equal care. A physicalist can recognise love, meaning, and responsibility as real features of a person. The proposal is that these capacities belong to an evolved, embodied being and require no additional sentient unit. Evidence about brain injury and stimulation gives this view substantial support: particular changes in the brain can alter particular aspects of experience and motivation.

That evidence places demands on the jeevan account. Calling the brain a medium still leaves the task of explaining those detailed changes. At the same time, the study asks whether explaining bodily functions fully explains their experienced character. The disagreement concerns the reach of an explanation, as well as the evidence it must accommodate.

Advaita Vedanta offers another answer. It identifies the deepest Self with Brahman, non-dual consciousness. Body, thought, and the familiar sense of individuality are examined in that light. Madhyasth Darshan instead retains many real, distinct sentient selves within a real world. Their shared interest in consciousness leads to different accounts of who we are.

Our study favours taking sentience as irreducible to insentient bodily organisation. It also keeps the further claims distinct. Madhyasth Darshan describes jeevan as a constitutionally complete atom that persists beyond bodily death. Arguments about experience do not by themselves establish that constitution or persistence. Those claims need evidence of their own, alongside an account of how jeevan and the body operate together.

The practical invitation is to examine the connection between understanding and living. Can a person recognise what relationships require, reconsider what they seek, and make that understanding evident in conduct? The full study develops this affirmative proposal and its strongest objections. The next question widens the inquiry: what account of existence could give body, sentience, and relationship their place together? That leads to The Ontology of Coexistence.
