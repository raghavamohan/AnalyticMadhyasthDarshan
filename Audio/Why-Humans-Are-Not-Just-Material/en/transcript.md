# Why Humans Are Not Just Material

A person can have work, a good home, comforts, food, and health, and still feel that life lacks meaning. We experience, question, judge, and seek understanding. Are these fully explained by body and brain alone, or is there more to a human being?

Our study, Why Humans Are Not Just Material, compares three focal answers—Madhyasth Darshan, a physicalist interpretation of science, and Advaita Vedanta—alongside wider philosophical views. Just material here means that experience and thought belong entirely to the organised physical and chemical life of the person. A physicalist still recognises love and responsibility as real; the disagreement concerns what makes them possible.

Shri A. Nagraj argued that matter-centred inquiry explained bodily processes without adequately understanding the person, while mystery-centred traditions placed decisive knowledge beyond ordinary explanation. His alternative is to make existence, the sentient self, and humane conduct understandable through study and evident in living.

Madhyasth Darshan understands both animals and humans as body and jeevan acting together, where jeevan names the sentient self and the body supplies senses, brain, and action. Why, then, do humans form a distinct knowledge order? Animals recognise and fulfil within fixed species limits. Through an enriched human brain, people also live by beliefs, seek understanding, and exercise imagination and freedom of action shaped through education and sanskar. Hunger and rest recur; lasting happiness concerns a settled way of living guided by truth and justice—recognising relationships, fulfilling their values, and evaluating that fulfilment for mutual satisfaction, while keeping bodily provision in place.

A description of pain can explain how injury produces signals, movement, and a report. Why does that activity also hurt? And how do a friend's voice, expression, and our concern belong together in one experience? Madhyasth Darshan proposes an individual jeevan as the bearer of that experience.

Understanding raises a further question: what makes a belief justified or an action right? A business can increase profits while depleting water its neighbours need. Becoming better at pursuing an aim does not show that the aim is sound. Awakening means understanding our place in a shared existence and guiding aims and conduct accordingly.

The physicalist proposes that experience and agency belong to an evolved, embodied person, requiring no separate unit. Brain evidence gives this view substantial support. In one study, people with injury to a valuation region at the front of the brain were less willing than comparison groups to exert effort for others, while effort for themselves was relatively preserved. The task measured choices, not inner concern; calling the brain a medium still leaves the jeevan account to explain such selective differences.

The physicalist also argues that experiencing a state and describing it scientifically are different ways of knowing the same reality, and that embodied persons can learn wider consequences and reconsider goals. Our study takes these replies seriously while questioning whether bodily explanations exhaust felt experience.

Advaita Vedanta identifies the deepest Self with Brahman, non-dual consciousness, keeping relationships and responsibility meaningful in ordinary life while treating individuality as not ultimately real. Other traditions treat the soul as the organising form of a living body, a God-dependent or detached witness, or, in Buddhism, conditioned processes without a permanent self.

Rejecting material reduction therefore does not by itself establish an individual immortal unit. Madhyasth Darshan further describes jeevan as a constitutionally complete atom—no longer gaining or losing constituent particles and persisting beyond bodily death—whose relation to physics remains open and requires independent evidence.

The invitation is to test this understanding in relationships, work, and conduct, asking both whether it helps fulfil relationships and what evidence would count against it. What account of existence gives body, sentience, and relationship their place together? That leads to The Ontology of Coexistence.
