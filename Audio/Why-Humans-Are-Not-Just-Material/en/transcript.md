# Why Humans Are Not Just Material

A person can have work, a good home, comforts, food, and health, and still feel that life lacks meaning. We experience, question, judge, and seek understanding. Are these fully explained by the organised life of the body and brain, or is there something more to a human being?

Our study, Why Humans Are Not Just Material, begins with this question. It compares three answers: Madhyasth Darshan, a physicalist interpretation of science, and Advaita Vedanta. Just material, in this sense, does not mean caring only about food or comfort. It means that experience and thought belong entirely to the organised physical and chemical life of the person, a view on which love, reasoning, and responsibility can still be real. The disagreement concerns what makes them possible.

Shri A. Nagraj argued that two inherited approaches left the human question unresolved. Matter-centred inquiry explained bodily processes without understanding the person. Mystery-centred traditions placed the decisive knowledge beyond ordinary explanation. His alternative is to make existence, the sentient self, and humane conduct understandable through study and evident in living.

Madhyasth Darshan understands the human being as body and jeevan acting together. Jeevan names the sentient self: the one who experiences, understands, evaluates, and seeks happiness. The body supplies the senses, brain, and means of acting. Hunger and rest recur; the aspiration for happiness that continues concerns the coherence of a whole life, and it needs guidance from an understanding of justice and truth. A gain that damages a relationship may leave the person less fulfilled, even when it increases possessions.

The study develops a deeper question about experience itself. A description of pain can explain how an injury produces signals, movement, learning, and a report. Why is that activity also felt? Why does it hurt? And how do a friend's voice, expression, and our concern for them belong together within one experience? Madhyasth Darshan proposes an individual jeevan as the bearer of that experience and understanding.

The physicalist answer deserves equal care. A physicalist can recognise love, meaning, and responsibility as real features of a person. The proposal is that these capacities belong to an evolved, embodied being and require no additional sentient unit. Brain evidence gives this view substantial support. In one study, people with injury to a valuation region at the front of the brain became less willing to make an effort for another person's benefit, while effort for themselves was largely unchanged. The task measured choices, not inner concern; but a particular injury changed a particular aspect of motivation.

That evidence places demands on the jeevan account. Calling the brain a medium still leaves the task of explaining those detailed changes. At the same time, the study asks whether explaining bodily functions fully explains their experienced character. The disagreement concerns the reach of an explanation, as well as the evidence it must accommodate.

Advaita Vedanta offers another answer. It identifies the deepest Self with Brahman, non-dual consciousness. Body, thought, and the familiar sense of individuality are examined in that light. Madhyasth Darshan instead retains many real, distinct sentient selves within a real world. Their shared interest in consciousness leads to different accounts of who we are.

Our study favours taking sentience as irreducible to insentient bodily organisation. It also keeps the further claims distinct. Madhyasth Darshan describes jeevan as a constitutionally complete atom that persists beyond bodily death. Arguments about experience do not by themselves establish that constitution or persistence. Those claims need evidence of their own, alongside an account of how jeevan and the body operate together.

The practical invitation is to examine whether a person can recognise what relationships require, reconsider what they seek, and make that understanding evident in conduct. The full study develops this affirmative proposal and its strongest objections. The next question widens the inquiry: what account of existence could give body, sentience, and relationship their place together? That leads to The Ontology of Coexistence.
