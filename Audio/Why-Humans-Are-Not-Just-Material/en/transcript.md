# Why Humans Are Not Just Material

A person can have work, a good home, comforts, food, and health, and still feel that life lacks meaning. We experience, question, judge, and seek understanding. Are these fully explained by the life of body and brain, or is there something more to a human being?

Our study, Why Humans Are Not Just Material, compares three answers: Madhyasth Darshan, a physicalist interpretation of science, and Advaita Vedanta. Just material here means that experience and thought belong entirely to the organised physical and chemical life of the person. A physicalist can still recognise love and responsibility as real. The disagreement concerns what makes them possible.

Shri A. Nagraj argued that matter-centred inquiry explained bodily processes without adequately understanding the person, while mystery-centred traditions placed decisive knowledge beyond ordinary explanation. His alternative is to make existence, the sentient self, and humane conduct understandable through study and evident in living.

Madhyasth Darshan understands the human being as body and jeevan acting together. Jeevan names the sentient self: the one who experiences, understands, evaluates, and seeks happiness. The body supplies the senses, brain, and means of acting. Hunger and the need for rest recur; the aspiration for lasting happiness concerns a settled way of living, guided by understanding justice, truth, and our place in the world. Bodily provision retains its place. Justice involves recognising relationships, fulfilling their values, and evaluating that fulfilment for mutual satisfaction.

A description of pain can explain how injury produces signals, movement, and a report. Why does that activity also hurt? And how do a friend's voice, expression, and our concern belong together within one experience? Madhyasth Darshan proposes an individual jeevan as the bearer of that experience.

Understanding raises a further question: what makes a belief justified or an action right? A business can increase its profits while depleting water its neighbours need. Becoming better at pursuing an aim does not establish that the aim is sound. Awakening, in Madhyasth Darshan, means understanding our place in a shared existence and guiding aims and conduct through that understanding.

The physicalist proposes that experience and understanding belong to an evolved, embodied person, requiring no additional sentient unit. Brain evidence gives this view substantial support. In one study, people with injury to a valuation region at the front of the brain were less willing than comparison groups to exert effort for others, while effort for themselves was relatively preserved. The task measured choices, not inner concern. Calling the brain a medium still leaves the jeevan account to explain these selective differences.

The physicalist can also argue that experiencing a state and describing it scientifically are different ways of knowing the same reality. An embodied person can learn about wider consequences and reconsider goals. The study takes these replies seriously while questioning whether bodily explanations fully account for felt experience.

Advaita Vedanta identifies the deepest Self with Brahman, non-dual consciousness: the awareness in which body, thought, and individuality appear. Relationships and responsibility remain meaningful in ordinary life. Madhyasth Darshan retains many real, distinct sentient selves within a real world. The two accounts therefore differ over whether individuality is ultimately real.

Our study favours the view that felt experience cannot be fully explained by bodily organisation alone. That does not by itself establish a separate sentient unit. Madhyasth Darshan further describes jeevan as a constitutionally complete atom: a unit that no longer gains or loses constituent particles and persists beyond bodily death. Its relation to atoms in physics remains open. These claims require evidence of their own.

The practical invitation is to examine this understanding in relationships, work, and conduct. Does it help us fulfil what relationships require? What evidence would count against its account? The full study develops the proposal and its strongest objections. What account of existence could give body, sentience, and relationship their place together? That leads to The Ontology of Coexistence.
