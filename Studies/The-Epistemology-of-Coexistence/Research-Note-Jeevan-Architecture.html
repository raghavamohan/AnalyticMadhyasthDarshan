Research Note: Jeevan Architecture

Author: Raghava Mohan Madhwapathi (analyticmadhyasthdarshan.org)

Edited on: August 14, 2026, 12:02 PM IST

Status: Internal research note (not a catalog entry). Compiled to support The Epistemology of Coexistence.

Scope: This note presents the primary concepts needed to understand : what it is, its five faculties and ten paired activities, how understanding becomes conduct, how delusion differs from awakening, how is formed, and how the body participates without becoming the knower. A separate technical companion preserves the formal functional model, and the sixty-one-pair inventory preserves the detailed source analysis behind the 122 activities.

holds that a human being is the joint form of a persistent sentient jeevan and a temporary physicochemical body. Jeevan is the knower and bearer of understanding; the body supplies sensation, neural mediation, speech, skill, and action. Five inseparable faculties organise jeevan’s activity from realisation at atma to selection at . Awakening occurs when their activity becomes consonant with understanding and becomes evident in humane conduct.

That account is the darshan’s ontology, not an inference established by contemporary neuroscience. This note first states the textual account and then marks where a cross-text synthesis or functional interpretation begins. It does not reduce jeevan to computation, identify its faculties with brain regions, or treat as a commander.

1. Jeevan, Knowledge, and the human joint form

is a constitutionally complete sentient atom. Its nucleus is named atma; its first, second, third, and fourth orbital groupings are named , , , and . The five are therefore faculties of one unit rather than five agents assembled into a mind (MVD, p. 78; AVD, p. 87). Constitutional completeness releases the atom from molecular and weight bondage while the bondage of hope remains, distinguishing jeevan from physicochemical units without making it inactive (MVD, p. 91).

Existence as coexistence is what jeevan can know. Omnipresence, or , is Knowledge in the ontological sense: actionless, non-transforming, and available as the ground of law, regulation, balance, justice, , and truth. Knowledge is nevertheless called the basis and source of inspiration for all activity, with inspiration inherent simply because units exist in it (MVD, pp. 32–36, 174). Units act; satta does not.

Every unit, including jeevan, exists submerged, soaked, and surrounded in satta. This standing condition is called saturation and is what “coexistence” names at the ontological level (MVD, p. 32). Realisation belongs to atma, and knowledge becomes humanly evident through the joint form of jeevan and body. Reading inspiration as the standing availability of reality rather than a transmitted message is an interpretation of these passages, not a separately stated mechanism.

The body is indispensable without being the knower. Body, heart, and prana are described as the gross body; mun, vritti, and chitta as the subtle body; buddhi and atma as the causal body, where “body” in the latter two cases names activity rather than anatomy. Prana joins gross and subtle activity, while chitta joins subtle and causal activity (MVD, pp. 204–205). At the outer relation, sensory motion reaches medhas, and a wave bearing the effect of mun participates in bodily activity and behaviour (MVD, pp. 199–200). These passages identify stages and connections but do not explain why a particular sentient content corresponds to a particular neural process.

The distinction is consequently not between an immaterial spectator and a mechanical body. The human being knows, speaks, works, behaves, evaluates, and participates as a joint form. Jeevan bears knowing and acquired orientation; the body supplies the present conditions and means through which they can become observable conduct.

2. Five faculties and ten paired activities

Each faculty has two generic activities. One is classified as bal, an activity in state; the other as shakti, an activity in motion. State here does not mean inactivity, and motion is not restricted to bodily movement. Realisation, enlightenment, contemplation, deliberation, and taste are active ways in which a qualitative orientation is established. Authenticity, resolve, visualisation, analysis, and selection are the paired ways in which that orientation becomes effective (JV, pp. 40, 62; MVD, pp. 131, 323).

Faculty What it concerns Bal: activity in state Shakti: activity in motion
Atma Realisation Anubhav: realisation in coexistence Pramanikta: authenticity and evidence
Buddhi Definiteness Bodh: enlightenment or definite understanding Sankalp: resolve according to understanding
Chitta Meaning and form Chintan: contemplation Chitran: visualisation, image, or plan
Vritti Criteria and alternatives Tulan: deliberation Vishleshan: analysis
Mun Acceptability and choice Asvadan: taste or valuation Chayan: selection

The ten belong to one . They are neither modules that switch independently nor ten deposits of information. Every jeevan has the constitutional activities; awakening changes their effective content, coordination, criteria, and evidence rather than increasing their quantity (MVD, pp. 199, 275; JV, p. 73).

2.1 Atma: realisation and authenticity

Anubhav is realisation in coexistence rather than episodic sensation. Its paired activity, , is authenticity: realised understanding becoming evident. Atma is mediative at the centre, but it does not issue detailed instructions to the other faculties. It supplies the realised orientation with which definite understanding and conduct are to agree (MVD, pp. 211, 323).

2.2 Buddhi: enlightenment and resolve

Bodh is definite understanding, not firmness attached to an assumption. Where meaning remains incorrect or unresolved, bodh concerning that content has not become effective. Beliefs accepted from fear or temptation remain limited to visualisation in ; remains silent until study, enlightenment, and realisation awaken it (MVD, pp. 99–100). Sankalp is resolve based on definiteness. Buddhi can remain alienated from atma, expectational, or unenlightened; the success term bodh should not be confused with every belief attributed to the person (MVD, pp. 216–217, 276, 279).

2.3 Chitta: contemplation and visualisation

Chintan sustains and contemplates meaning. Chitran gives meaning a projectable form: an image, anticipated relationship, explanation, possibility, or plan. The quality of visualisation depends on the meaning it expresses; without enlightenment in truth it necessarily carries excess, deficiency, or omission (MVD, pp. 286, 323).

2.4 Vritti: deliberation and analysis

Tulan deliberates through definite perspectives, while vishleshan distinguishes alternatives, implications, and consequences. The six perspectives are pleasant–unpleasant, healthy–unhealthy, profit–loss, justice–injustice, –adharma, and truth–untruth. Each has its own referent: sensation, body, goods and services, behaviour, resolution, and realisation in existence (MVD, pp. 65–67).

Pleasure, health, and material result remain relevant in awakened living. Their limitation is that they vary with bodily and material circumstance and cannot finally authorise injustice or contradiction. Justice, dharma, and truth govern them rather than abolish them.

2.5 Mun: taste and selection

Asvadan is the felt assessment of acceptability; chayan selects according to that valuation. is nearest to bodily execution and receives interpreted bodily and sensory conditions through the human joint form. Selection therefore reflects the present organisation of understanding, imagined possibility, deliberative criteria, expectation, bodily condition, and available means (MVD, pp. 204, 323, 327).

2.6 Two uses of bal and shakti

The corpus uses the terms at two grains. At the activity level, state activities are strengths and motion activities powers. At the faculty level, mun, , chitta, buddhi, and atma themselves are called the five strengths, while hope, thought, desire, resoluteness, and evidence or authenticity are called their powers (MVD, pp. 199, 275; SB, pp. 63, 139; AVD, p. 151).

These formulations are related but not fully reconciled in the texts. The table above adopts the activity-level assignment directly tabulated in AVD, pp. 91–94. It should not be used to erase the faculty-level vocabulary or to infer that every occurrence of bal and shakti names one member of a ten-activity pair.

3. From understanding to conduct

The faculties form an ordered architecture, not a production line. Each performs its own activity while becoming consonant with an orientation available inward of it. Two outward sequences help make the account intelligible.

The realisation-based sequence runs through the activities in state:

realisation → enlightenment → contemplation → deliberation → taste

Realisation in atma is followed by enlightenment in , contemplation in , deliberation of justice, , and truth in , and taste in (MVD, pp. 276–277).

The paired activities in motion make that organisation effective:

authenticity → resolve → visualisation → analysis → selection

When selection is embodied, the sequence extends through bodily capacity, speech, work, behaviour, relationship, and material result. This is not the conversion of one activity into another. Each faculty responds through its own pair, and the body and circumstances constrain what can actually be performed.

3.1 Projection and reflection

In the directional account used here, paravartan is the outward manifestation of orientation, while pratyavartan is the movement of awakening in which an outer faculty comes to accord with the faculty inward of it. Hope in mun becomes according to thought in vritti; thought according to chitta; desire according to enlightenment in buddhi; and enlightenment according to realisation in atma (MVD, pp. 275–277, 307).

The adjoining relations are also named from the content they make effective. Awareness of realisation (pratiti) occurs between buddhi and chitta as contemplation; comprehension (aabhas) between chitta and vritti as deliberation of justice, dharma, and truth; and perception (bhas) between vritti and mun as taste of values with selection and fulfilment of relationships (MVD, pp. 99–100). These are descriptions of coherence among faculties, not three further faculties.

The combined form of reflection and projection is the cognisance–sensitivity cycle and the awakened cycle (MVD, p. 275). Reflection in this directional sense is not a bodily consequence travelling into atma, nor are the state activities simply run backwards. Other source uses are retained in the technical companion §13.

The relation between state–motion and projection–reflection remains unsettled. AVD places the bal activities under pratyavartan and the shakti activities under paravartan, while the realisation-based sequence orders the state activities outward. This note therefore does not declare the two distinctions either identical or fully independent.

The cycle also bears explanatory weight: lack of understanding of projection, reflection, and their effects is identified with mysteriousness and with movement toward decline despite the wish for awakening (MVD, p. 291). The architecture is therefore meant to make activity knowable, not merely to supply names.

3.2 Receptivity

An indication can be available without being received. The ability to receive a higher-conformance indication arises through internal regulation of jeevan’s powers, and four conditions are named (MVD, p. 284).

Faculty receiving Condition Supporting influence
Mun receiving from vritti Absence of crime Orderliness
Vritti receiving from chitta Absence of injustice Societal conduct
Chitta receiving from buddhi Absence of attachment Study and sanskar
Buddhi receiving from atma Absence of ignorance Inward regulation or dhyan

Three conditions depend on social and educative life rather than private introspection alone. Awakening is therefore inseparable from relationship, orderliness, study, and an evidence-bearing human tradition.

Reception is described as succeeding or failing in this passage, while capacity and receptivity elsewhere admit of degree (MVD, pp. 142, 248). The texts do not explain how partial reception relates to the stated absence-conditions. They also do not state that the paired shakti activity is “turned inward”; the defensible claim is that internal regulation of the powers enables reception.

The surrounding account joins practice to reception: crime-free behaviour is supported by orderliness, just thought by social conduct, dharma-oriented desire by study and good , and ignorance-free buddhi by inward regulation. Reception and immersion then become evident as happiness, peace, contentment, and bliss (MVD, pp. 282–286). The cycle is thus socially supported and conduct-bearing, not closed inside an isolated individual.

3.3 Consequence, evaluation, and inquiry

A bodily or relational result is not already labelled as just, fulfilling, or true. Mun tastes its acceptability; vritti deliberates through the relevant criteria; chitta recognises meaning and relationship; buddhi establishes definiteness where understanding succeeds. Correct intention is insufficient if bodily skill, means, timing, or recognition of the other person is inadequate.

A consequence may prompt a question, study, or correction. Curiosity is located at mun, inquiry can be borne, and teaching is organised around the learner’s need and questions (MVD, pp. 77–78, 248, 313–314, 344). The developmental sequence names curiosity in mun, enthusiasm in vritti, delight in chitta, elation and immersion in buddhi, and realisation in atma; it is an inwardly regulated orientation toward awakening, not a fixed response protocol (MVD, pp. 77–78). The stronger printed learning relation is that realisation-oriented conceptions become thought and action, while thought arising through action is instilled again as sanskar through inference (MVD, p. 218).

Consider a disagreement over family resources. The relationship is to be fulfilled justly, while material means serve assessed need, prosperity, and right use. Mun registers attraction and discomfort; vritti considers bodily, material, and relational consequences; chitta recognises the relationship and possible form of mutual fulfilment; buddhi establishes whether the proposed course is understood; resolve, visualisation, analysis, and selection organise conduct. The use of resources changes the relationship, and both persons must evaluate whether its values were fulfilled. Remaining contradiction can become material for another inquiry.

4. Restfulness, discord, and awakening

The aim is vishram, restfulness: the eradication of unhappiness and the evidence of resolution under continuing activity, not inactivity or withdrawal (MVD, pp. 68, 78–79). Each faculty is described as more developed than the one outside it. When bodily sensation and hope set the final terms, more developed faculties are asked to conform to less developed ones, producing discord, frustration of effort, and thirst for restfulness (MVD, pp. 91, 276).

In awakening the direction of accord is reversed: is immersed through , vritti through , chitta through , and buddhi through atma. Their functions attain effective capacity through satisfaction, and bodily sensation comes under cognisance (MVD, p. 277).

4.1 One immersion, four values

Realisation in Omnipresence has one effect and flow, named according to the faculty at which it is present:

Relation Value
Buddhi in concurrence with atma Bliss
Chitta in concurrence with buddhi Contentment
Vritti in concurrence with chitta Peace
Mun in concurrence with vritti Happiness

The same four are described both as one immersion reaching the four outer faculties and as the values arising from concurrence between adjoining faculties (MVD, pp. 307, 327). They are not four rewards to be maximised separately. Bodily pleasure can coexist with discord, and inward calm cannot substitute for failed relationship.

The four are the inward segment of a wider concurrence account. Just behaviour and vocation bear coexistence and prosperity; body–heart concurrence bears satisfaction; heart–prana concurrence health and growth; prana–mun concurrence strength and motivation; the four faculty relations then bear happiness through bliss; and atma is eternally in coexistence with Omnipresence, bearing ultimate bliss (MVD, p. 327). These outer and innermost terms should not be collapsed into four additional values of .

4.2 Delusion and the four and a half activities

In delusion, selection and taste operate in mun; analysis and deliberation toward pleasure, health, and profit in vritti; and visualisation in chitta. These are the four and a half effective activities (MVD, pp. 79, 89; JV, p. 73). The half is deliberation restricted to the first three perspectives. Its result does not become contemplation in chitta, so contemplation is absent rather than half-effective (AVD, p. 152). Mun’s two generic activities are therefore already effective; they occupy a sensory and bodily field. The sixty-four detailed activities of mun are not a second list that remains off until awakening (§6).

Delusion can therefore be imaginative, analytical, skilled, and socially successful. Its limitation is not lack of activity but the criteria and depth that govern it. The body is taken as the self, sensation and material result set the final terms, and buddhi and atma do not effectively orient conduct.

Awakening does not install new activities. It brings knowing and believing into relation with recognising and fulfilling, allowing all ten activities to become evident in behaviour (JV, p. 74). Intermediate statuses are specified by which inward faculty effectively orients the outer activity (MVD, pp. 278–279).

Status Effective orientation
Partially awakened Mun and vritti are driven by chitta: desire organises thought and hope
Half-awakened Mun, vritti, and chitta are driven by buddhi, but resolve is without atma-bodh
Awakened or completely awakened Resolve is with atma-bodh; atma regulates and disciplines buddhi, chitta, vritti, and mun

These are gradations of effective orientation, not different kinds of jeevan.

4.3 Capacity without additional power

Constitutional capacity and effective capacity must remain distinct. Jeevan’s provision of strength and power is complete and inexhaustible; awakening does not add a quantity of power. What develops is clarity, receptivity, coordination, criterion, skill, and evidential reach.

Capacity, ability, and receptivity—kshamata, yogyata, and patrata—are distributed in the complete development of chitta, vritti, and mun: creative visualisation according to buddhi, thought of according to chitta, and receptivity for just behaviour according to vritti (MVD, pp. 322–323). Their corresponding roles at buddhi and atma are not named in the same three-term form.

4.4 The practical fields of knowledge

The faculties and activities operate upon known content; that content is not another set of faculties. The printed sources distinguish four bodily propensities—eating, sleeping, protection, and mating—and five sensory modalities: sound, touch, visible form, taste, and smell. They group resolution with the wealth, progeny, and public-recognition motives (vitteshana, putreshana, and lokeshana) as intellectual satisfaction, and separately describe upakar as help and inspiration for another’s prosperity and awakening (MVD, pp. 58, 64–65, 73).

These fields differ by what is being fulfilled. Bodily and sensory information serves bodily maintenance and interaction; the eshanas become humane responsibilities under resolution, justice, and right use; upakar employs resolved capacity to support another’s material sufficiency and awakening while preserving mutuality. The four propensities and three eshanas have distinct points of effect in the activity architecture (MVD, pp. 203–205, 305).

An oral schema reportedly groups four bodily propensities, four sensory activities, three eshanas, and upakar as twelve practical knowledges. No located source enumerates that set or names the four sensory activities, so §7 records it as unfinished source work rather than using it to organise the account.

5. Sanskar, learning, and continuity

is acquired orientation toward completeness: understood content, acceptance, valuation, responsibility, participation, and readiness for conduct. It is shaped through environment, study, practice, prior organisation, work, behaviour, and evaluation (MVD, pp. 90, 121, 134; JV, pp. 49–50). It is not merely information remembered in , genetic inheritance, or a database located in one faculty.

The sentient aspect’s sanskar is described as attained awakening in , , chitta, and (MVD, p. 121). This supports reading it as organisation across those four faculties. Treating atma as realised orientation rather than a fifth store is an interpretive distinction, since the passages do not provide a theory of storage or explicitly settle whether awakening of the four includes atma implicitly.

5.1 Knowing, believing, recognising, and fulfilling

A more source-near description avoids the language of stored state. The entirety of human sanskar is presented as knowing, believing or accepting, recognising, and fulfilling (SB, p. 237). Knowing and believing belong to cognisance; recognising and fulfilling to sensitivity held under cognisance (KD §3.4, p. 66). Awakening joins these activities where deluded living had evidenced only recognising and fulfilling sensory desires (JV, p. 74).

These four terms explain why information alone is insufficient. What is known must be accepted through understanding, recognised in the present relationship and circumstance, fulfilled through competent embodied activity, and evaluated in its result. Repetition can increase skill without establishing knowledge if its criterion remains sensation, approval, or assumption.

5.2 Evidence and transmission

The primary evidence chain runs from realisation into conduct and human tradition:

Realisation itself is the ultimate evidence, evidence itself is the understanding or knowledge, understanding itself is manifest, the manifest itself is resolution, work and behaviour, work and behaviour itself is evidence, evidence itself is awakened tradition, awakened tradition itself is coexistence. (MVD, p. 12)

Comprehension is also evidenced in the ability to convey understanding to another (JV, p. 26). Sanskar is described as given by an awakened tradition, imparted through education-sanskar, embraced, and evidenced (MVD, pp. 121, 344; SB, p. 237). No passage describes mechanical copying between jeevans. Explanation supplies language, definition, and indication for another to examine, understand, practise, and evidence; the teacher–learner relation remains inquiry-bearing and participatory.

Evaluation remains part of evidence. Justice includes recognition of relationship, fulfilment of its values, evaluation, and mutual satisfaction (MVD, pp. 310–311, 336). A person may state a correct value yet misrecognise the relationship, lack the ability or means to fulfil it, or mistake unilateral satisfaction for mutual fulfilment.

5.3 Bodily death and later embodiment

Jeevan continues after bodily death and later associates with another body. Where understanding and satisfaction have been attained, their established condition continues because jeevan does not die (JV, pp. 20, 55, 77). Bodily death therefore ends the present gross means of expression; it does not end jeevan’s activity or create a new sentient unit. Without a gross body, subtle activities are said to occur according to the indication of buddhi (KD, p. 31).

The later bodily association is placed from about the fourth or fifth month of pregnancy (JV, p. 94). Acceptances toward completeness and knowledge, wisdom, and science moving toward resolution are said to be carried forward as prarabdh; prarabdh also names the difference between what can be known, desired, done, and undergone—what remains to be undergone (MVD, pp. 90–91). Neither formulation supplies a rule matching one jeevan to one body. The sources do not state that every analytical component of acquired organisation is preserved without change or provide a public method for separating earlier organisation from present education, bodily condition, relationship, circumstance, and choice. Explicit autobiographical recall is also not stated as the form in which continuity must appear.

6. The sixty-one pairs and 122 activities

The ten generic activities are refined into 122 detailed activities. Their distribution is stated as

Faculty locus Pairs Activities Generic pair
Atma, nucleus 1 2 Realisation–authenticity
Buddhi, first orbit 2 4 Enlightenment–resolve
Chitta, second orbit 8 16 Contemplation–visualisation
Vritti, third orbit 18 36 Deliberation–analysis
Mun, fourth orbit 32 64 Taste–selection
Total 61 122 State–motion

The counts and identity with state and motion are given at MVD, p. 323; the activities are defined at pp. 328–348; and AVD, pp. 91–94, tabulates every member in a bal or shakti column. The first member of every paired heading in the definitions agrees with the bal column and the second with the shakti column.

The enumeration supplies a source-governed assignment, not one relation repeated sixty-one times. The definitions include accepted states and readiness for expression, held conditions and their evidence, relationships with associated values or responsibilities, an activity and what it bears, acts and objects, and sensory qualities with varied correlates. No one relation covers all sixty-one, and the classification cannot be reconstructed from which definition sounds more static.

The detailed inventory, translation variants, non-uniform pair relations, missing sound pair, and unresolved derivation of the counts are kept in The Sixty-One Activity Pairs of Jeevan. The sixty-one pairs are contents of the five generic pairs: what each faculty establishes as bal, and how the paired shakti makes that establishment effective. They are not sixty-one further faculties, variables, or independently switchable functions, and they are not “dimensions” in the source’s sense—the eight dimensions of visualisation are a separate list and are not mapped onto ’s eight pairs (MVD, p. 327).

The 122 are tabulated as conducts of awakened (AVD, p. 91). In delusion, ’s taste and selection already operate, mainly over sensation and bodily use; the four and a half are sufficient for the five senses and four instincts (JV, pp. 73–74; §4.2). The thirty-two mun pairs name the awakened inventory of those two activities. They do not remain unused from lack of need, and a row is not the selection of its bal member.

7. Source status and open work

The architecture has four evidential levels.

Directly stated: the constitution of ; five faculties; ten and 122 activities; activity-level state and motion; the directional order of awakening; reception conditions; four values; four-and-a-half activities; bodily and unembodied stages; and continuity across bodily death.

Cross-text synthesis: the concise faculty descriptions in §2; the two outward sequences in §3; as organisation distributed across four faculties; intermediate awakening as increasing effective depth; the term-for-term correspondence between immersion and adjoining concurrence; and the 122 as contents of the ten rather than a second on/off list.

Technical interpretation: optional notation for relating present activity, acquired orientation, receptivity, inquiry, embodied consequence, evidence, and continuity across embodiments. These proposals are explicitly labelled in the technical companion.

Oral or presently weak: a twelvefold practical schema composed of four bodily propensities, four sensory activities, three eshanas, and upakar. The four bodily propensities, three eshanas, and upakar are attested individually, while no consulted printed passage or repository recording enumerates the twelve or names four sensory activities. The five sensory modalities are repeatedly attested. The twelvefold should therefore not organise the reader account or constrain the technical model until its source is located.

Five groups of questions remain: the relation between sentient content and bodily mediation; the several uses of bal–shakti and projection–reflection; the process joining consequence, inquiry, corrected understanding, and lasting sanskar; the public evidence for mutual satisfaction and continuity across embodiments; and the unresolved derivation, pair relations, sensory omissions, and oral schema surrounding the 122 activities. The full research agenda is in the technical companion §12.

Editorial Notes

The reader follows the activity-level bal–shakti assignment in AVD, the directional cycle in MVD, and the four-faculty placement of attained without claiming that the corresponding variants have been harmonised. The unused textual variants, overlapping terms, and excluded oral schema are documented in the technical companion §13; pair-level variants remain in the inventory §8.
