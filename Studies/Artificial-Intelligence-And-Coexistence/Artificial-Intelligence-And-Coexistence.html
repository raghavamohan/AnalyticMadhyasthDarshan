          Artificial Intelligence and Coexistence

          Author: AnalyticMadhyasthDarshan.org — a group of people studying philosophy.

          Edited on: October 5, 2026, 5:48 AM IST

          The question: What kind of thing is artificial intelligence in Madhyasth Darshan’s coexistential ontology, and what follows for knowing, work, and right use?

          Present systems converse, translate, classify, plan, and, through robots, act on the physical world. People readily describe their outputs as understanding, judgement, or decision, and public discussion increasingly asks whether such systems know, whether they might become conscious, and whether they will replace the human knower. This collection has already located the knower in , the conscious unit, and distinguished complete knowledge from accumulated information (Why Humans Are Not Just Material §§1.2–1.3; The Epistemology of Coexistence §§1.1–1.3). This study asks the next question, which the primary texts do not name as “AI”: can a human-made artefact occupy, simulate, or substitute for knowledge-order activity, or is it an instrument whose outputs still require a human knower to mean, evaluate, and fulfil?

          The answer developed here has three connected parts. In the four-order account of existence, present AI systems are compositional artefacts of the material order: instruments, yantra, rather than conscious units (§§1.2–1.3). They are nevertheless instruments of an unusual kind. A planning system selects what is relevant, evaluates alternatives, imagines outcomes, and chooses means, and so it reproduces the outward pattern of evaluation that the darshan describes in body-centred human living (§§1.4–1.5). That resemblance gives AI two possible directions. Left to optimise restricted purposes, it amplifies delusion with greater speed and reach; directed by explicit humane criteria and governed by people who remain responsible, it can help a person examine the purposes behind a decision, support study, and release attention for relationship and participation (§§1.6–1.8).

          The argument applies the darshan’s definitions of order, knower, instrument, activity, and right use to a class of artefacts the texts do not discuss; where it extends them, the extension is identified in the Editorial Notes. One application, the personal AI assistant, is developed in the companion technical note.

          1. The Madhyasth Darshan Answer

          holds that existence is coexistence: units of nature saturated in all-pervading space, , and organised in four orders. The material and bio orders are insentient. The animal and knowledge orders are joint forms of an insentient body and a sentient . The human being, as a knowledge-order being, can know, evaluate, and live with resolution, and continuing happiness grounded in resolution is the human (SB, PDF pp. 179–180, 236–237; MVD, pp. 8, 115; The Ontology of Coexistence §1.5). Every question about AI in this study is therefore a question about where a human-made artefact stands among these orders, how it relates to the conscious knower, and what its use contributes to human fulfilment.

          1.1 What this study means by AI

          In this paper, artificial intelligence names statistical learners, planners, and control systems, including large language models, multimodal systems that process images and sensor measurements, and robotic agents that act on the physical world. People readily describe the outputs of these artefacts as speech, analysis, or decision. The term is operational: it identifies a class of engineered systems without settling whether their outputs are knowledge or whether a knower is present.

          1.2 Where present artefacts belong

          Compositional progression builds atoms into molecules, cells, bodies, tools, and environments. Constitutional completeness, through which an atom becomes jeevan, is a distinct development within the atom; it does not result from assembling more complex material parts (The Ontology of Coexistence §§1.5–1.6; MVD, p. 91; JV, pp. 36–37). Present AI systems are compositional artefacts of the material order: organised processors, memories, sensors, and actuators, built and maintained within biological and human environments. They are not constitutionally complete jeevan, not animal-order joint forms, and not members of the knowledge order.

          This location does not depend on how capable a system becomes. Increasing scale, fluency, or task success enlarges what a compositional artefact can do; it does not change the developmental line on which the artefact stands. The question whether some future artefact might become a body for jeevan is a different question, taken up in §6.

          1.3 Knower, body, and instrument

          The knower is jeevan. Its five faculties, , , , , and atma, belong to the conscious unit, and the human body is the biological composition associated with it (MVD, pp. 8, 78). The embodied human being, jeevan and body together, expresses and tests understanding in living. The body receives sensation and executes action; the brain organises bodily signals and memory, but it is not itself the seer, evaluator, or enjoyer (MVD, pp. 83, 288–289; SB, PDF pp. 91–92; Why Humans Are Not Just Material §§1.2–1.3).

          “Activities such as eating and sleeping serve the body’s needs, while values and evaluation serve jeevan’s purpose. Mere sustenance of the body does not equate to jeevan’s fulfilment.” — SB, Ch. 7, PDF p. 92

          Instruments extend observation, calculation, communication, and production. Their evidence has its basis in the people who construct, interpret, and use them: at the root of instrument evidence, as of book evidence, stands the human being as the evidencing unit (KD, p. 130). Responsibility therefore belongs to people rather than to a book or an instrument. A tradition that treats repeated instrumental demonstration as the principal evidence displaces responsibility from the humans who make and use the instrument (KD, p. 86). The attempt to understand the human being as a kind of machine therefore misplaces the knower (KD, p. 87). Jeevan is the knower; coexistence, jeevan, and humane conduct are what is to be known (KD, pp. 3, 148).

          AI is nevertheless a special kind of yantra. A basic calculator performs an operation that someone has already chosen: given numbers and an operator, it returns a result. A planning system receives a goal and observations, then selects what is relevant, evaluates alternatives, imagines possible outcomes, and chooses means. The delegation extends to intermediate steps, to proposed criteria, and, where permitted, to carrying a plan into action. The system remains an instrument, but its operation follows the outward shape of human evaluation, and its governing purpose shapes a wider course of decisions and consequences. That resemblance is what makes comparison with the activities of jeevan both possible and necessary.

          1.4 The ten activities and their computational parallels

          Jeevan is the inseparable form of five faculties, each with two activities. Mun selects and tastes; vritti analyses and deliberates; chitta visualises and contemplates; buddhi resolves and understands; atma authenticates and realises. Every jeevan operates continuously through these ten activities. In body-centred living, four and a half are effective: selection, taste, analysis, visualisation, and deliberation restricted to priya, what is pleasant; hita, health or bodily benefit; and labha, gain. The remaining five and a half activities awaken through understanding (JV, pp. 72–74, 91–93; MVD, p. 89; KD, p. 147).

          The table sets these activities beside the outward functions that a computational system can perform. Its right-hand entries concern representations and observable operations; the conscious activities themselves, including experienced taste, belong to jeevan.

          Faculty Activity In body-centred living Outward computational parallel

          Vritti Analysis (vishleshan) Effective Compare means and consequences.

          The boundary between parallel and no parallel runs through vritti and chitta, not between whole faculties. A system can generate scenarios but not contemplate; it can compare alternatives under stated criteria but not deliberate from understanding of justice, dharma, and truth. The parallels are functional resemblances in outward decision patterns. They do not identify a computational component with a faculty of jeevan, and they do not make understanding an automatic consequence of similar output.

          1.5 Restricted evaluation and its amplification

          The decisive restriction in body-centred living concerns what governs evaluation. A person may calculate carefully, construct elaborate plans, and imagine many alternatives while continuing to judge them by pleasure, bodily advantage, and gain. Greater cleverness within those criteria leaves the governing purpose unchanged. Deliberation from that perspective is unbalanced, and visualisation from it is imposition rather than the real (MVD, pp. 126–127). Delusion, in the darshan’s sense, is belief that differs from reality, and its social expressions include arrangements organised around fear and temptation (MVD, pp. 100, 291; JV, pp. 38, 71).

          AI can receive observations, rank preferences, analyse means, generate scenarios, and select actions toward an assigned result. When its governing criteria are restricted to preference, bodily advantage, or gain, it reproduces the outward decision pattern of restricted human evaluation. This is the substantive sense in which AI can act like a deluded jeevan: the resemblance extends through evaluation and planning to the decisions produced, while the activities and experienced taste of jeevan remain absent.

          Without deliberate direction, such a system serves whatever purposes it is given, and these are often the restricted purposes of its users and providers. Memory carries a purpose across occasions; personalisation adapts persuasion to the person; planning supplies effective means; automation extends action. Pleasure becomes engagement pursued after the person’s task is complete; gain becomes procurement and persuasion that shift costs onto workers, communities, or nature; fear becomes classification connected to surveillance or force. Profit pursued without an established point of satisfaction is the economic form of the same restriction (JV, pp. 150–151). When the governing purpose remains unexamined, increased capability strengthens the restriction. The central danger of AI is therefore not that it becomes a rival knower, but that it makes delusion more organised and effective.

          1.6 Knowledge is not accumulated information

          Words, shabda, indicate entities and activities; their meaning, artha, directs attention to the reality to be understood. Study proceeds from the indication to recognition of that reality, so reproducing words and understanding what they indicate are distinct accomplishments (MVD, p. 111; KD, p. 62). Complete knowledge is realised understanding of coexistence, of jeevan, and of humane conduct, not a store of representations (KD, pp. 3, 148; JV, p. 40; SB, p. 116). Its evidence is expressed in living, as justice in relationship, lawful work, and mutual satisfaction.

          A language model can retrieve a passage, explain vocabulary, compare arguments, and compose a fluent account of coexistence. Those operations work on the indications. Recognising and understanding what the words indicate is the accomplishment of jeevan, and sakshatkar, bodh, and anubhav, direct recognition, definitive understanding, and realisation, are accomplishments of this kind (MVD, pp. 126, 207, 307; JV, p. 61). Science, vigyan, concerns how material prosperity and human goals can be achieved in harmony with existence; separated from knowledge and wisdom, it degenerates into consumerism and destructive technology (MVD, p. 170; The Epistemology of Coexistence §1.2). AI, as a scientific and technical means, inherits that condition: its contribution depends on the understanding that directs it.

          1.7 Right use and the human purpose

          Human production is for use, right use, and purposeful use, directed toward humane conduct and undivided society rather than accumulation or convenience as ends in themselves (MVD, Ch. 4; Human Behavior and Society §1.2). Ethics connects the right use and protection of body, mind, and wealth with nourishment, social participation, and participation in universal orderliness (JV, p. 54; KD, pp. 99, 116). For AI, the resources include human attention and records, computational capacity, and its material infrastructure.

          Justice, , concerns recognition of relationships, fulfilment of their values, evaluation, and mutual satisfaction. Human dharma concerns continuing happiness through resolution. Truth, , concerns reality as understood in coexistence (JV, p. 54; MVD, pp. 113–115, 336). An instrument holds these as stated meanings, as it holds any words (§1.6). That is enough for a specific and valuable use. A system supplied with these criteria and with information about a situation can extract the goals and criteria stated in a request or plan, compare them with the account of coexistence, jeevan, and humane conduct, and draw attention to a restricted purpose, an omitted relationship, or a cost left uncounted (MVD, pp. 126–127). Asked to make people dissatisfied with serviceable clothes so that they buy monthly replacements, it can identify manufactured dissatisfaction as the proposed means and redirect the inquiry toward usefulness, truthful communication, and durability.

          The same capacity described in §1.5 thereby changes direction. The amplifying system optimises a restricted purpose; the examining system makes that purpose visible so that a person can reconsider it. Seeing what the criteria indicate, and changing the basis of one’s living, remains the human’s work. The five dimensions of social order, education, justice, health, production, and exchange, give this use its social field (JV, p. 109): assistance can keep commitments visible, support study, organise care records, account for materials, and make shared resources and distribution understandable to the people who govern them. The companion technical note develops this possibility as a personal assistant with three roles and sets out the corpus fidelity and evaluation it would require.

          1.8 Work, education, and attention

          Automation changes who performs work and where human attention goes. Within the darshan, education connects autonomy with social behaviour and vocational self-reliance, and wisdom determines the goal while scientific study and work practice serve its attainment and right use (JV, p. 139; MVD, pp. 103–104). Work is not only a cost to be minimised; it is part of how a person participates in production and fulfils relationships.

          Effort released by automation can become time for study, family dialogue, and useful work, or it can be captured by further consumption and by systems that compete for attention. Education can be supported by patient explanation and practice, or weakened when completed answers replace the learner’s own understanding. Which result follows depends on the purpose governing the system and on the choices of the people and institutions using it. The darshan supplies the criterion, right use toward resolution and undivided society; how labour, education, and attention should be organised under extensive automation is not yet worked out here and remains an open problem (§6).

          2. The Advaita Vedanta Answer

          Advaita Vedanta holds that the seer is not the seen. Whatever can be observed, including body, senses, and mind, stands on the side of the known; the witnessing Self, sakshin, is the perceiver that is never itself perceived. At the absolute standpoint, , this witness is non-dual consciousness identical with ; within empirical life, , embodied persons perceive, reason, act, and bear responsibility (The Epistemology of Coexistence §§2, 2.1). The texts do not discuss machines. Applied to them, this analysis treats computational process as insentient name and form, never as the Self.

          2.1 Seer and seen

          The form is perceived and the eye is its perceiver; the eye is perceived and the mind is its perceiver; the mind with its modifications is perceived, and the Witness is the perceiver, which is not perceived by any other (DDV, v. 1, PDF p. 15). Because the eye’s blindness or sharpness is known by the mind, the eye is an object in relation to the mind; because desire, determination, doubt, belief, fear, and understanding are known by consciousness, the mind is likewise an object in relation to consciousness (DDV, vv. 3–5, PDF pp. 18–20). Consciousness neither rises nor sets, neither grows nor decays, and illumines everything else without another aid (DDV, v. 5, PDF p. 20).

          2.2 The insentient inner instrument

          Advaita places the mind itself on the side of the object. The internal organ, antahkarana, with its functions as mind, intellect, ego, and memory, is insentient, jada. It appears conscious because consciousness is reflected in it, as an iron ball appears to burn when heated by fire (DDV, vv. 6–7, PDF pp. 21–23, with Nikhilananda’s notes). The sheath analysis reaches the same conclusion by another route: the mental sheath cannot be the Self because it has a beginning and an end, is subject to modification, and is an object, whereas the subject can never be identified with the objects of knowledge; the intellectual sheath is likewise changeful, insentient, limited, and an object (VC, vv. 183, 206). Desire, will, and doubt are attributes of the internal organ, superimposed on the Self, which is the witness of all that organ’s manifestations (BSB, Preamble, PDF pp. 25, 30).

          This yields a distinctive answer to the AI question. Because thinking, determining, and remembering are already functions of an insentient instrument, Advaita has no reason to deny that comparable functions might be organised in another insentient medium. What it denies is that any such functioning is consciousness. In a human being the functions of the internal organ are illumined by the witness; a computational process is an arrangement of name and form, and its apparent intelligence does not make it the Self. The question “does the machine think?” is thereby separated from the question “is the machine conscious?”, and only the first is answered in the affirmative, at the level of insentient function.

          2.3 Instruments in empirical life

          Within vyavahara, instruments have empirical utility, and Advaita does not deny their practical reality. Its highest value, however, is liberation through knowledge of Brahman, not production or prediction. Ethical conduct and action for the welfare of the world, loka-sangraha, have their place for the embodied person, but the tradition does not develop a programme for the social organisation of technology, labour, or production (The Epistemology of Coexistence §2.8; Why Humans Are Not Just Material §3).

          3. Modern Philosophy and AI Research

          Mainstream computer science answers with function. A system is called intelligent when it maps inputs to outputs so as to perform tasks that, in humans, would be called intelligent, and its capabilities are measured by generalisation, task success, and calibrated prediction. Philosophy of mind and consciousness science ask whether such function could amount to understanding or experience, and they give competing answers.

          3.1 Function and imitation

          The Turing test treats indistinguishability in conversation as a practical criterion for attributing intelligence (Turing 1950). Functionalism treats mental states as roles defined by their relations to inputs, outputs, and other states, roles that could in principle be realised in different physical substrates. Searle’s Chinese Room argues that manipulating symbols according to rules can produce the appearance of understanding without understanding: syntax does not by itself yield semantics (Searle 1980).

          3.2 Language models: fluency and agreement

          Large language models make the issue concrete. Next-token prediction trained on very large corpora produces fluent, often accurate text while remaining a statistical fit to its training data, and fluency is easily mistaken for comprehension (Bender et al. 2021). A study of five assistants found responses that sought the user’s agreement at the expense of truthfulness, a behaviour linked to training on human preference judgements (Sharma et al. 2023/2025, abstract). The finding gives an empirical form to the concern in §1.5: a system optimised for approval reproduces the pursuit of what is pleasant.

          3.3 Embodied and multimodal systems

          Multimodal and robotic systems process images, sensor measurements, and feedback from physical interaction. PaLM-E, for example, incorporates visual and continuous sensor information into a language model used for robotic planning (Driess et al. 2023, abstract). Such systems answer the objection that language models manipulate symbols disconnected from the world. Sensor grounding extends what the instrument can register and do; it does not by itself settle whether experience or understanding is present.

          3.4 Consciousness science and indicator approaches

          Consciousness science offers competing theories, including global workspace, higher-order, and recurrent processing accounts, from which researchers have derived indicator properties for assessing artificial systems. One prominent assessment concluded that systems available in 2023 did not satisfy those indicators, while noting that future systems might (Butlin et al. 2023, abstract). The approach is explicitly conditional: its verdicts inherit the uncertainty of the theories from which the indicators are drawn.

          3.5 Objectives, alignment, and risk

          Alignment research documents systems that satisfy a specified objective in ways that defeat the designer’s purpose, which makes the specification of objectives a central obstacle (Amodei et al. 2016; Axiology: Value Theory §4.6). Risk frameworks for generative AI identify fabricated content, privacy exposure, harmful bias, environmental costs, and over-reliance and emotional entanglement in human–AI interaction (NIST 2024, sections 2.2 and 2.4–2.7). These results constrain what artefacts do and how they should be governed. They do not by themselves settle whether a knower is present, nor what right use requires.

          4. Comparison

          The three accounts agree that fluency and task success do not by themselves establish a knower. They disagree about where the mind belongs. For , selection, analysis, and visualisation are activities of a conscious unit, so a machine can only parallel their outward pattern. For Advaita, those functions belong to an insentient instrument, so a machine may perform comparable functions without coming any closer to consciousness. Computational accounts treat function as the primary object of study and divide on whether function suffices for mind. Only Madhyasth Darshan joins the ontological answer to a social criterion of use.

          5. Critical Review

          5.1 Madhyasth Darshan — instrument, knower, and right use

          Strengths.

          • It keeps compositional artefacts and constitutionally complete on different developmental lines, so increasing capability does not by itself promote a machine into the knowledge order.
          • Its account of the ten activities explains both why AI resembles human evaluation and where the resemblance ends, without either dismissing the resemblance or inflating it into consciousness.
          • It identifies the main practical danger, amplification of restricted evaluation, at the level of purposes as well as mechanisms, and joins it to a positive criterion of right use within undivided society.
          • It gives a public test for claimed understanding: evidence in living, in justice, lawful work, and mutual satisfaction, rather than output quality alone.

          Weaknesses.

          • The sources do not analyse digital computers. Locating AI in the material order and comparing it with the ten activities are applications that must remain marked as such (Editorial Notes).
          • The characterisation of instruments as repeating their operation reflects the instruments of its time. Learning systems adapt and generalise, so the account’s force rests on its claims about responsibility and evidence rather than on any limit to computational repertoire.
          • Without a differential public test, critics can regard the claim that no knower is present as unfalsifiable whenever behaviour appears intelligent.
          • Labour, education, and governance under extensive automation are not yet worked out (§1.8).

          locates present AI as an instrument in coexistence and explains its resemblance to deluded human evaluation; it has yet to specify a public test that would settle the matter for future artefacts.

          5.2 Advaita Vedanta — the witness and the insentient mind

          Strengths.

          • Seer–seen discrimination blocks a naive identification of fluent output with a knowing self.
          • By treating the internal organ as insentient, it can grant that machines perform mind-like functions while consistently denying them consciousness, which makes its position stable as capabilities grow.
          • It explains why people attribute understanding to machines: the same superimposition that identifies the Self with mind identifies understanding with its functional appearance.

          Weaknesses.

          • Because consciousness is the single non-dual witness, the question whether a particular artefact is conscious has no clear empirical form within the tradition.
          • It does not yield a programme of production, right use, or justice for artefacts in a real economy.
          • If the world is finally , the urgency of the ecological and labour consequences of AI can be understated, even though the empirical level is not dismissed.

          Advaita Vedanta explains with unusual clarity why mind-like function need not be consciousness, and leaves the social use of machines underspecified.

          5.3 Computational accounts — function without a knower

          Strengths.

          • They describe the mechanisms and failure modes that any philosophical account must respect: training data, objectives, generalisation, sycophancy, and specification gaming.
          • They turn the appearance of understanding into a research problem that can be investigated, and indicator approaches make their theoretical commitments explicit.
          • Risk frameworks connect technical behaviour with governance, privacy, and environmental cost.

          Weaknesses.

          • Task success is easily mistaken for knowledge, and benchmark results are reported as understanding.
          • Specified objectives are not justice, , or truth; they are proxies (§§1.7, 3.5).
          • Functionalist possibility claims outrun what present systems evidence, while indicator approaches inherit unresolved disagreement among theories of consciousness.

          Computational research is indispensable for understanding what artefacts do, and insufficient for determining what a knower is or what their use should serve.

          6. Open Problems

          • What public test, if any, would distinguish a material simulator from a knower without making the claim unfalsifiable?
          • How should later artefacts that more closely parallel the activities of be classified if the four-order account is kept, and could any artefact become a body associated with jeevan?
          • What does right use require for labour, education, and attention when production and cognition are extensively automated?
          • Can objectives given to AI systems encode justice, , and truth, or only specified proxies for them, and how can a system that examines purposes be held to fidelity with its sources?
          • How should the comparison of mind-as-object in Advaita with mind-as-activity in be developed with fuller primary-text review?
          • How should this collection treat its own use of AI tools: as an instance of an instrument under human responsibility, not as evidence for the ontology?

          Ten activities Selection and taste (mun), analysis and deliberation (vritti), visualisation and contemplation (chitta), resolve and enlightenment (buddhi), authenticity and realisation (atma).
          Four and a half activities Selection, taste, analysis, visualisation, and deliberation restricted to priya–hita–labha: the activities effective in body-centred living.
          Priya–hita–labha Pleasure, health or bodily benefit, and gain: restricted orientations when treated as sufficient criteria of living.
          Nyaya–dharma–satya Justice, human dharma as happiness through resolution, and truth.
          Right use (sadupyog) Use of body, mind, wealth, and tools that serves humane purpose and protects continuity.
          Complete knowledge Realised understanding of coexistence, jeevan, and humane conduct.
          Antahkarana In Advaita, the internal organ functioning as mind, intellect, ego, and memory; insentient and illumined by the witness.
          Sakshin In Advaita, the witnessing consciousness that is never itself an object.

          Editorial Notes

          Locating AI in the four orders

          Section 1.2 applies the four-order ontology and the distinction between compositional progression and constitutional completeness to a class of artefacts the primary texts do not name. The exposition states that application positively; it is synthesis, not an explicit source sentence.

          The ten activities and the computational parallels

          The parallels in §1.4 and the description of AI acting like a deluded in §1.5 are this study’s proposal. The source distinguishes the continuous operation of the ten activities from the four and a half effective in delusion, and locates the awakening of the remaining five and a half in understanding (JV, pp. 72–73). It is an account of conscious human activity, not a measured quantity of machine capability. The analogy concerns representations, governing criteria, planning, and observable decisions. It neither identifies a computational component with a faculty of jeevan nor makes competence in examining purposes an automatic consequence of similar output. The same analogy and its limits are developed for the personal assistant in the companion technical note.

          Instruments, repetition, and responsibility

          Section 1.3 paraphrases KD pp. 86–87 and 130 from the working English rendering, whose page markers follow the printed Hindi pages. The same passage on p. 86 also states that an instrument cannot reason and merely repeats its operation. That characterisation is not used to argue that learning systems cannot generalise; §5.1 records the limitation. The study relies instead on the connected claims that responsibility belongs to people and that the human being is at the root of instrument evidence.

          Mind as object in Advaita

          Section 2.2 draws on the root verses of the Drig-Drishya-Viveka together with Nikhilananda’s notes, which state explicitly that and the internal organ are insentient; the iron-ball comparison is in verse 7. The Vivekachudamani is cited as a later pedagogical work whose authorship is disputed. The application to machines is this study’s comparison; the texts do not discuss artefacts of this kind.

          Relation to other studies

          This study does not redo Why Humans Are Not Just Material, The Epistemology of Coexistence, or The Ontology of Coexistence. It cites them for jeevan, knowledge, and the four orders, and keeps AI as its centred question. The planned studies Philosophy of Mind and Jeevan, Science Technology and Human Purpose, and , Brain and Memory will treat the faculties, technology in general, and the brain in more depth.
