          Prosperity Economics and Right Use

          Author: AnalyticMadhyasthDarshan.org — a group of people studying philosophy. Source repository: raghavamohan/AnalyticMadhyasthDarshan.

          Edited on: October 14, 2026, 3:56 AM IST

          The question: Why do human economies, unlike every other order of nature, fail to reach balance (santulan), and what would an economy look like that gives human labour its true value, judged by the usefulness it establishes for human living and for the wider order of nature, rather than by scarcity, accumulation, and price?

          A forest, a herd, or a field left to itself settles into a definite balance with its surroundings. Soil feeds plants, plants return to soil, and animals take what their bodies need and then stop. The human body belongs to the same nature. Its requirements for food, shelter, and protection are definite and can be counted, and in principle they could be met and then held in balance like those of any other organism. Human economies nevertheless behave differently. They extract more each generation, accumulate wealth that no one can use, and treat a continuing rise in consumption as the measure of success. Mainstream economics takes this imbalance as its starting point: wants are assumed to be unlimited and means scarce, so economic life becomes the permanent allocation of what is never enough.

          Madhyasth Darshan locates the problem elsewhere. The human being is a joint form of a body and , the conscious unit. The body’s needs are definite; jeevan seeks continuity of happiness, which only understanding fulfils. In delusion, jeevan works through a fraction of its activities and evaluates by pleasure, bodily advantage, and gain. The dissatisfaction of its unexpressed faculties is then taken for a shortage that more sensation and more possession can remove. Because sensory satisfaction is transient and the need it is asked to meet is not material, demand loses its stopping point. Accumulation, above all of money, which alone escapes use and decay, becomes the organising aim of production and exchange.

          Shri A. Nagraj set out the darshan’s alternative in Avartansheel Arthshastra, cyclical economics. Body, mind, and wealth are deployed as labour on natural abundance; the objects produced nourish and protect the body and support social life; and these renew the capacity to produce. Exchange rests on the value of labour, need is assessed within the family, production exceeds that need, and the remainder flows into right use.

          At the centre of this alternative is a different valuation of human labour. In the darshan, the value (mulya) of anything is its participation in the order of the whole, what its activity fulfils for others (Axiology: Value Theory §1.1). Labour establishes usefulness and beauty in what nature provides, so the true value of labour lies in the usefulness it establishes: in nourishing and protecting the body, in sustaining social life, and, through right use, in the orderliness of family, society, and nature (Axiology §§1.5–1.6). The money economy values labour instead by the price it can command. Labour is bought and sold like any other commodity, the accumulation of currency becomes the measure of a person’s standing, and the producer of useful objects has generally remained poor (AA, pp. 7, 45, 52, 73).

          The study reconstructs this account from the treatise and the darshan’s other texts (§§1–3), compares it with economic theory, with Indian and Advaita teaching on desire, and with the sciences of satiation and natural limits (§§4–7), and asks what measurement and artificial intelligence can contribute to such an economy, and what they must leave to people (§8). Its central claim, assessed in the critical review (§9), is that the darshan explains the insatiability that mainstream economics postulates, while it still needs from economics a theory of coordination beyond the village, measurable criteria of need and balance, and an account of money and credit as they actually operate.

          1. Why human economies do not reach balance

          1.1 Balance in every order

          describes nature as four orders: material, biological (plant), animal, and knowledge, the last being the human. Each order maintains its own definite conduct. The material order keeps its conduct through the conformance of its constitution and results; plants reproduce their kind through seed conformance; animals live within the conduct of their species. Every unit of nature except the human exhibits definite conduct, and the human reaches definite conduct only through , the acceptances formed by understanding (JV, pp. 47–48; AA, pp. 54–55). All traditions other than the human are bound by their own laws and are already orderliness; for the human alone, recognising its own orderliness remains a task (AA, p. 117).

          The same account holds that each order is balanced in its existent state. The material order has the innateness of existence, the plant order of existence and growth, and the animal order of existence, growth, and the hope of living, and each is balanced in its existent state. The knowledge order is balanced in its existent state only when it evidences resolution, prosperity, fearlessness, and coexistence (MVD, p. 105). The earth itself is described in these terms. Its seasonal regularity is maintained by definite proportions of minerals and plants, and the coal and mineral oil buried in its interior had a part in making the planet’s heat balance and its later greenery possible (AA, pp. 30, 78–79; KD §3.7, pp. 73–81). Prosperity, on this view, is first a property of the earth, which became rich in itself before any human labour touched it (AA, pp. 77–78).

          The conclusion drawn from this account is stark. Whatever unit causes disorder in existence is the deluded human being alone (AA, p. 41). Nature other than human is already complementary to the human; the awakening needed for the human to be complementary to the rest of nature is still awaited (AA, p. 42).

          1.2 The definite needs of the body

          The human body is a chemical and physical formation (AA, p. 16). Its needs are of two kinds. Common aspirations are food, shelter, and adornment; special aspirations are telephony, broadcasting, and transport (AA, pp. 26, 73, 93). Both are needs for objects, and objects have definite properties. Grain can be stored for a few years and then rots; a machine left unused rusts; what is produced is for use, and hoarding beyond a point becomes a problem in itself (JV, p. 152). The accumulation of material objects is not feasible, because their use or transformation is inevitable (MVD, p. 183). Even prosperity is a temporal need: work for it is necessary while the body lives, and the need ends with the body (MVD, p. 96).

          The quantities involved can therefore be assessed. For a village of a hundred families, the quantity and quality of food, shelter, and adornment, and the need for medicine, become definite, and on that basis production can be distributed among the families (AA, p. 26). The body’s balance is not an ideal imposed from outside. It is the ordinary condition of a living body whose requirements are met.

          1.3 What an account must explain

          If the body’s needs are definite and the rest of nature is balanced, the imbalance of human economies needs an explanation. An adequate account must say why human want becomes insatiable when bodily need does not; how need is to be distinguished from want in practice; how production can be held within the balances on which it depends; how human labour can be given its true value, and exchange made just, when producers differ in skill and circumstance; what evidence would show that such an economy is working; and how a society could move towards it from the present money economy. The darshan’s answer to the first question supplies the ground for the others, so the study begins there.

          2. The joint form and the source of insatiability

          2.1 Jeevan seeks continuity of happiness

          Every human being carries out all activities in the hope and expectation of happiness, peace, contentment, and bliss. This expectation is the human’s innateness (dharana), present in every person, and whatever one recognises through the senses is recognised from the expectation of happiness at its root (AA, p. 13). Contentment is accepted by every human being and discontent by none (AA, p. 48).

          The darshan locates this expectation in . Jeevan has ten activities, five as powers and five as strengths, borne by its faculties: mind (), with selection and taste; , with analysis and deliberation; , with visualisation and contemplation; , with resolve and enlightenment; and atma, with authenticity and realisation (AA, p. 50; JV, pp. 72–73). The four jeevan values name harmony between adjacent faculties. Happiness is harmony between mind and vritti, peace between vritti and chitta, contentment between chitta and buddhi, and bliss between buddhi and atma (AA, pp. 20–21, 92; JV, p. 137). Happiness in this sense is not a sensation. It is a state of the whole conscious unit, and its continuity depends on the higher faculties having the content of understanding.

          2.2 Four and a half activities and unregulated taste

          In delusion, only four and a half of the ten activities are effective: selection, taste, analysis, visualisation, and half of deliberation. Taste and selection operate by what is pleasant to the senses; deliberation operates from the perspectives of pleasure, health, and profit (priya, hit, labh); and the person lives identified with bodily sensation. The remaining activities lack the substance of understanding, and the result is continuous internal conflict within jeevan (JV, pp. 72–73).

          The treatise draws a consequence from this that bears directly on economics. Selection and tasting form a cycle of their own: after selecting comes tasting, and after tasting the activity of selection arises again. However often the cycle runs, the arising of selection and tasting remains as it was, ready to be done again, because the powers of jeevan are inexhaustible (AA, pp. 17–18). Deliberation in terms of pleasure, health, and profit has been pushed to decisive conclusions in human tradition, while deliberation in terms of justice, , and truth is still under review, its decisive point, form, motion, and purpose not yet settled (AA, p. 18).

          The awakened alternative is not the suppression of taste. It is its regulation. In awakened jeevan, every tasting is balanced and regulated on the touchstone of deliberation, every deliberation on the touchstone of contemplation, all contemplation on the touchstone of enlightenment, all enlightenment on the touchstone of realisation, and all realisation on the touchstone of coexistence. This chain is the darshan’s formula of self-regulation and balance in every individual (AA, p. 95). Insatiability, on this account, is what selection and tasting become when that chain is missing: an inexhaustible activity with no higher activity to give it measure.

          2.3 Transience as a signal, misread as lack

          Decisions based on sensitivity have temporary validity. Eating feels good, but no one can keep eating; sleeping feels good, but no one can keep sleeping; no sensory activity can be maintained without change. The darshan reads this not as a defect but as a provision:

          “The transient nature of sensitivity is such a wonderful provision in humans, serving as an alarm, an inspiration, and a direction for their awakening.” — JV, p. 163

          Whatever process of indulgence a person carries out through the five senses cannot have continuity, and so discontent with it remains in the activity of reflection that follows (AA, p. 48). Temptation towards accumulation and indulgence brings relief for a moment, and in the moments after, fear and suspicion pervade more than relief (AA, p. 33). Pleasure, health, and profit are relative respectively to the senses, the body, and objects; they are the sources of dissatisfaction, whereas justice, dharma, and truth are the sources of satisfaction (AA, pp. 58–59).

          These passages together give the darshan’s diagnosis of insatiability. The deluded person experiences the discontent that follows every sensory satisfaction and the internal conflict of faculties without content. Lacking understanding of jeevan, the person reads both as a shortage of objects and seeks more of what has just failed to satisfy. The satiation point then moves ahead like a mirage (AA, p. 78). The fulfilment of duties is possible because duties are definite and limited, while the fulfilment of needs pursued as indulgence is impossible because such needs are indefinite and unlimited (MVD, p. 128). Insatiability is thus not a fact about bodily need. It is a category error: a continuous, qualitative need of jeevan pursued through transient, quantitative means. The construction of this synthesis from several connected passages is recorded in the Editorial Notes.

          2.4 Accumulation, symbolic value, and artificial scarcity

          Profit has always meant giving less and getting more, and prevailing economics has fixed no limit to how much less and how much more (AA, p. 5). Profit keeps sinking, through exploitation, into accumulation, indulgence, over-indulgence, and multi-indulgence (AA, p. 7). The treatise concludes that no individual can become prosperous by the method of trade, because accumulation, comfort, and profit have no satiation point; nor can society be formed, contradictions among communities removed, or universal good brought about by that method (AA, pp. 7–8).

          Accumulation settles on money because money escapes the constraint that governs real goods. Metal became the symbol of objects and paper the symbol of metal, so that economics became the study of a symbol, its accumulating, and objects valued through it (AA, pp. 5–6, 52). However much paper currency or metal reserve exists, it cannot replace grain for one person or quench one person’s thirst (AA, p. 6; JV, pp. 151–152). The accumulation is only of money, which is symbolic value (MVD, p. 182). Once objects are subject to currency, human labour becomes subject to it too, and accumulating currency becomes the principal basis for recognising a person’s standing (AA, p. 45). Changes in price over decades are changes in the symbol alone; rice, gold, and cement carry the same usefulness as before (AA, pp. 52–53).

          Two further consequences follow. Artificial scarcity becomes a principal technique for gaining profit: where currency is concentrated, objects are held back from the motion by which they would become accessible to all (AA, pp. 5–6, 52). And because accumulation has no satiation point, it cannot be accessible to all, since the earth is limited (AA, p. 52). Those who remain engaged in production are unable to accumulate, while those who accumulate become slack in production, and the producer of objects has generally remained poor (AA, pp. 7, 75).

          The darshan names the error in terms of evaluation. Delusion means taking a thing to be more than, less than, or other than what it is (AA, p. 17). Over-evaluation of symbolic currency, under-evaluation of usefulness value, mis-evaluation of the human being as a kind of animal, and under-evaluation of naturalness, the forests, minerals, and earth on which all provision depends, are its economic forms (AA, p. 53).

          2.5 The consequence in the earth

          The same evaluation produces the ecological crisis. Coal and mineral oil needed to remain in the earth for its balance; extracting and burning them produced pollution, rising heat, a damaged protective layer in the upper atmosphere, and rising seas (AA, pp. 30–31). Chemical fertilisers increased quantity while the concentration of nutrition in grain declined and soils were driven towards excess acidity or alkalinity (AA, p. 70). Breeding produced larger grains and flowers whose basic nutritive content stayed the same (AA, pp. 53, 55–56). Mechanised and input-dependent agriculture replaced autonomy with dependence in seed, manure, ploughing, and threshing (AA, p. 71). Pollution policy, in the treatise’s assessment, does not ask how to stop pollution but how much of it people can live in (AA, p. 74).

          The whole form of exploitation is the exploitation of the earth, and the human being is both exploited and exploiter (AA, p. 82). The only production the treatise names as opposed in itself to a coexistential economy is the manufacture of war materials; machine production as such is not contrary to economics (AA, p. 47). The objection is to the purpose and the evaluation that direct production, not to technique.

          3. Cyclical economics

          3.1 Resources as body, mind, and wealth

          Cyclical economics is the right use, security, and prosperity of resources (artha) in the form of body, mind, and wealth (AA, p. 25). The mind is an inseparable activity of . When hope, thought, desire, and resolve act by a realisation-based method, the mind works through the body, applying labour with skill and proficiency to natural abundance. The objects produced carry usefulness value and aesthetic value, and these objects are wealth (dhan); wealth is only the produced object, not money (AA, pp. 25, 105). The right use of the body is evidenced when the mind uses it consistently with realisation, enlightenment, contemplation, and deliberation (AA, p. 25).

          This changes what counts as capital. All production is the outcome of human labour applied to natural abundance, so the primary wealth is the labour power inherent in human beings, and this primary capital is always the human being’s own (AA, p. 73). Economics that treats money as the primary resource has studied the symbol rather than the thing (JV, pp. 151–152). Resources must include body, mind, and wealth together; economics cannot exist if any one of these is left out (JV, p. 151).

          3.2 What cyclicality means

          The treatise gives cyclicality a wide sense before it gives it an economic one. Complementarity itself is the meaning of cyclicality, and cyclicality is mutual sustenance: particles sustain one another as atoms, atoms as molecules, molecules as formations, and the orders of nature as one another’s support (AA, p. 42). It can be seen at many scales. Seed becomes tree and tree becomes seed; bodies form according to species and species traditions continue through bodies; knowing and believing ground recognising and fulfilling, and these return to knowing and believing; relationship grounds value, value grounds evaluation, and evaluation returns to relationship. The individual’s complementarity is linked into the family and the family’s into the individual, and the same holds between family and village. Production through the utilisation of labour, the valuation of products by the value of labour, and exchange on that basis together form a cycle, and so do the four orders, each complementary to the others (AA, p. 15). Water evaporates, gathers as cloud, falls as rain, and runs back to the sea; plants decompose and make the earth fertile for further plants (AA, pp. 34–35; MVD, p. 152).

          The common structure is a movement that starts from any point, passes through complementary activity towards the fulfilment of a purpose, and returns to its starting point with that purpose fulfilled, so that the movement can continue (AA, p. 15). The treatise’s own definition of this structure is a compressed sentence whose Hindi admits more than one parsing; the reading adopted here is recorded in the Editorial Notes. Two features of it matter for economics. A cycle returns: whatever is drawn out is restored to the conditions of the next round. And a cycle is purposive: it is linked in the sense of prosperity, so its return is the fulfilment of a goal, not mere repetition.

          3.3 The provision cycle

          Applied to provision, the cycle has a definite sequence. A need for production arises; a mentality fitted to that need, endowed with skill, proficiency, and scholarliness, forms; through a healthy body, labour establishes usefulness value and aesthetic value in objects drawn from natural abundance; the objects are rightly used according to their usefulness; the need is supplied; and the remainder is exchanged (AA, p. 106). The Jeevan Vidya lectures state the same cycle as a renewal of capacity:

          “We deploy our resources in the form of body, mind, and wealth on natural abundance for production, and the produced goods become the basis of nourishment and protection of our body and social participation. We again become capable of producing on the basis of this social participation, nourishment, and protection. In this way, it is cyclical.” — JV, p. 154

          The cycle runs through the joint form itself. The mind operates the body; mind and body together manifest object values; and object values are useful to the body and satisfying to jeevan (AA, p. 26). Earth, air, water, forest, and minerals are the sites where labour is utilised (AA, p. 26). Tools and other means made by one generation serve the next, so that means accumulate across generations in a form that remains in use: each generation can offer the next means in excess of need, the means of production increase, and the time required decreases (AA, pp. 106, 118). The cycle grows richer without growing larger in consumption.

          3.4 Need, the family, and the satiation point

          The cycle closes only where need is definite, and the darshan locates that definiteness in the family. A family is a limited number of autonomous people, at least ten, living together with justice: recognising their relationships, fulfilling the values in them, evaluating, and reaching mutual satisfaction, and complementary to one another in the family’s production (AA, pp. 14, 26, 65, 72). Needs are ascertained at the level of the family, not the individual (JV, p. 153; AA, p. 9). In a humane family, needs become limited because use, right use, and purposeful use become definite, and production in excess of need is therefore possible in every family (AA, p. 26). For a person who understands jeevan, all needs are useful only in the nourishment and protection of the body and in the motion of society, so needs appear limited, while the labour power for meeting them, being the inexhaustible power of jeevan, gives confidence in production in excess of need (AA, p. 114).

          Prosperity follows directly. The lack of the sentiment of lacking is prosperity, and production in excess of needs is prosperity (MVD, p. 71). Its basis is the sum of objects produced in excess of need, together with their right use and security (AA, p. 113). Prosperity cannot be private. For one family to be prosperous, more than one family must be; both the imagining of prosperity alone and the imagining of prosperity through accumulation prove to be delusion (AA, pp. 62–63). The person who can sustain this is the autonomous human: trusting in oneself, respecting excellence, balanced in talent and personality, social in behaviour, and self-reliant in occupation (AA, pp. 65, 71–72).

          The treatise also identifies a satiation point within exchange. Production is the remuneration of labour, and after exchange the remuneration remains just as much as was produced. This is the satiation point of exchange: in the utilisation of labour there is neither profit nor loss, only more or less production as a matter of work practice (AA, p. 76). The treatise extends the point to existence as a whole. Neither development in the atom, nor the transition to jeevan, nor the formation and decomposition of physical and chemical substances shows any sign of profit and loss; what is seen throughout is coexistence and prosperity (AA, pp. 77–78).

          3.5 Value, labour, and exchange

          Value in is not price. The value of a unit is its essentiality, its participation in the orderliness of the whole: what its activity fulfils in relation to others. For produced things this participation is usefulness, what food, shelter, a tool, or a vehicle fulfils in nourishing and protecting the body and in the motion of society, and aesthetic value adds convenience to usefulness. Values are further ordered by the purposes they serve: usefulness serves need, and need serves relationships, humane character, and finally the harmonies of jeevan (Axiology: Value Theory §§1.1, 1.5–1.6; AA, p. 92). Human labour is the activity that establishes usefulness and beauty upon natural abundance, and the primary wealth of any economy is this labour power, which is always the human being’s own (AA, p. 73). To give labour its true value is to value it by the usefulness it establishes in this larger sense, not by the currency it can command.

          Production is the usefulness value and aesthetic value established through labour on natural abundance, in the sense of prosperity (AA, p. 68). It is also described as the human establishment of usefulness and beauty upon nature other than human, and as a qualitative change in body, mind, and natural abundance in the direction of usefulness (AA, p. 116). The value of an object is determined in a chain: labour-based object, object-based usefulness, usefulness-based valuation (AA, p. 49). Evaluation can be made only of labour, not of symbolic objects (AA, p. 112).

          The treatise proposes a practical method of valuing labour. Wheat sown in a village under various circumstances yields various quantities; the midpoint of those quantities gives a common value for the labour of producing wheat. Once one object of common aspiration has been valued in this way, every product of the village can be valued similarly, and the valuation can be consolidated within the village (AA, p. 66). Each person’s pace of work in an activity, in ploughing, breaking stone, or building a house, differs by activity and is the basis of measurement. A product made in a measured time, with a definite technical mentality and dexterity, counts as one unit of labour and can be exchanged for labour equal to it (AA, p. 119). The committee responsible for exchange evaluates labour through the combination of skill, proficiency, speed of work, time, and means, and remains alert so that neither unnecessary profit nor unbearable loss occurs (AA, p. 145).

          Differences of skill do not have to be weighed inside the family. Some days one member contributes more and some days less; the family’s goal is the product, not more or less labour (AA, pp. 119–120). Valuation by labour is needed between families and villages, where exchange takes place. Usefulness value itself is constant: a kilogram of sesame has always had the same usefulness, whatever its monetary valuation (JV, pp. 136–137).

          3.6 Use, right use, and purposeful use

          Use, right use, and purposeful use name three levels at which resources serve human purposes. Usefulness is the utilisation of body, mind, and wealth in the nourishment and protection of the body and in the motion of society. Right use is their utilisation in orderliness and in the motion of undivided society. Purposeful use is their utilisation in evidencing, from generation to generation, knowledge of coexistence, of jeevan, and of humane conduct (AA, pp. 72, 94). Body, mind, and wealth are indivisible in all three, because no object can be used without body and mind (AA, p. 94). The treatise pairs usefulness with justice, right use with resolution, and purposeful use with authenticity (AA, p. 116).

          Values are ordered by what they serve. In offering and surrender, object values are dedicated to civic values, civic values to established values, established values to human values, and human values to jeevan values, which are happiness, peace, contentment, and bliss (AA, p. 92). The right use of resources is itself cyclicality, and the earth’s endowment is rightly used by the method of balance (AA, p. 91). Non-accumulation means earning for expenditure, and its righteous expenditure is mandatory (MVD, p. 150). Those who take no part in production lack awakening regarding use and right use (AA, p. 75).

          3.7 Production within the balance of nature

          The provision cycle is nested within the cycles of nature, and its production must keep them intact. Natural assets, minerals and vegetation, are to be used in proportion to their production, in the sense of balance; their production is not to be obstructed; and human beings are to become helpful in it. Without keeping nature prosperous, the human being cannot be prosperous (AA, pp. 142, 146). The state policy of the darshan names production security as maintained “through the principle of maintaining nature’s balance”, and economic security through producing more than the needs of the family by way of circularity (MVD, p. 264).

          The treatise is specific about energy. Mineral oil, coal, and radioactive fuels are the most harmful sources; cyclical sources, the force of flowing water, falling water, wind, solar heat, and gas from dung and refuse, are pollution-free (AA, pp. 31–32, 106–107). Vegetable oil from roadside oil-bearing trees forms a closed cycle: tree, seed, oil, smoke, the smoke taken up as food by trees, and seed again (AA, p. 32). Agriculture is to keep animal husbandry, compost, and dung gas together, so that the village’s own manure restores fertility and soil degradation is halted (AA, pp. 134–135). The human-made environment must be planned with attention to the interventions made upon nature, the utilisations of labour, their returns, and their usefulness, right use, and purposeful use (AA, p. 114). Determining the land of each village and district also determines how many villages can live there with prosperity (AA, p. 92).

          3.8 The economy within the five dimensions of orderliness

          Economics in isolation has no definition or standing (AA, p. 117). Cyclical economics is one integral part of self-governance, alongside sociology, the science of the constitution, psychology, and the science of production (AA, pp. 63, 110). Self-governance means accessibility to justice, to production, and to exchange, sustained by education- and health-restraint (AA, p. 63). The five dimensions of orderliness are depicted as a circle joined to a pentagon: education-sanskar to understanding, health-restraint to expression consistent with science and wisdom, justice-security to honesty, production to prosperity, and exchange-reserve to balance (AA, p. 112). How Undivided Society Is Established §5.4 describes how these dimensions constrain one another.

          The village of about a hundred families is the first unit of this order. Ten families form a family cluster, ten clusters a village council, and so on through ten tiers to a world family council, each council nominating five committees for the five dimensions (AA, pp. 27–28, 125–127). Production scale rises with the tier: cottage industry within the village, small industry at the village cluster, medium industry at the district, medium and large industry at the province, and large industry at the region, continent, and world, where the world council’s committees maintain balance across the whole (AA, pp. 29–30, 115). Every village is to be autonomous in the objects of common aspiration, and a shortfall in a few items is met within ten or a hundred villages (AA, p. 72). Common aspirations are assessed through local survey and special aspirations through local acceptability (AA, p. 112). Justice is assessed in four dimensions: in character, in behaviour, in production, and in exchange (AA, pp. 140–143).

          3.9 The path of transition

          The treatise does not assume that the money economy disappears at once. Its plan for a village begins with a detailed survey: population, education, occupations, land, water, crops, livestock, crafts, shops, storage, health, and the state of justice in production and in exchange (AA, pp. 122–125). The village’s exchange is then conducted through an autonomous cooperative exchange-reserve working without profit and loss. Members hold accounts, sell to the reserve what they produce in excess of need, and buy what they need. In the first period, values follow the nearby market rate less the cost of transport; any incidental gain from trading with outside markets goes to the village’s common facilities; members without funds receive interest-free loans repaid in produce; and a nationalised cooperative bank stands as guarantor until the reserve can return its capital (AA, pp. 136–138).

          Justice in exchange is reached in three stages. First, the value of labour is evaluated through the prevailing currency, using wholesale rates. Second, the currency itself is evaluated on the basis of labour. Third, object values are evaluated on the value of labour alone, through usefulness and aesthetic value, and exchange proceeds free of profit, loss, and accumulation (AA, pp. 142–143). The Jeevan Vidya lectures add that the wish for a cyclical economy is present even in people living within a profit-obsessed system, and that producing in excess of need by one’s own labour is open to anyone now (JV, pp. 153–154). Each committee’s work is evaluated against stated criteria. Production is evaluated by total output, speed, quality, productivity, skill, access to raw materials, and pollution-free technical improvement; exchange by its ease, its freedom from profit and loss, its cleanness, its measurement, and its management of means (AA, pp. 146–148).

          4. Economic theory on wants, scarcity, and wealth

          4.1 Aristotle: household management and unlimited acquisition

          Aristotle distinguished the art of household management, which acquires what is needed for living well, from the art of wealth-getting through exchange for gain. The first has a limit set by the needs of the household and the city; the second has no limit to its end, because it pursues money as such. He judged retail trade for gain contrary to nature and usury the most unnatural mode of acquisition, since it makes money breed money (Aristotle, Politics I.8–10; SEP-AP). The parallel with the darshan’s closure condition is close. Natural wealth has a measure because it serves use, and acquisition becomes limitless when it attaches to the symbol of wealth.

          4.2 Classical political economy and the stationary state

          The classical economists located the source of wealth in labour and expected growth eventually to give way to a stationary state of capital and population. John Stuart Mill declined to regard that prospect with the aversion of his predecessors. A stationary condition of capital and population, he argued, implies no stationary state of human improvement, and might leave more room for culture, moral progress, and the preservation of nature than a society absorbed in getting on (Mill, Principles IV.6). Mill’s stationary state halts accumulation by choice, for the sake of a better life; it lacks the darshan’s account of why accumulation outruns need in the first place.

          4.3 Marx: use, accumulation, and the value of labour

          Marx contrasted the circuit of simple commodity circulation, in which a commodity is sold for money in order to buy another commodity for use, with the circuit of capital, in which money buys commodities in order to be sold for more money. The first ends in consumption and so has a natural limit; the second begins and ends with money, and its movement is therefore limitless (Marx, Capital I, ch. 4). He grounded value in socially necessary labour and analysed accumulation as the defining drive of capital. The darshan shares much of this diagnosis: profit as giving less and taking more, labour as the source of all product, and the endlessness of accumulation in money. It rejects the ground of Marx’s remedy. The hypothesis that everything functions through conflict mistakes existence, in which every unit has inherent strength and progresses through mutual complementarity; class struggle cannot be the foundation of happiness (JV, p. 156). Axiology: Value Theory §3.8 compares the two accounts of labour value in more detail.

          4.4 Scarcity and non-satiation

          Modern economics took its standard definition from Lionel Robbins: economics studies human behaviour as a relationship between ends and scarce means which have alternative uses (Robbins 1932; SEP-PE). Scarcity in this sense is relative to wants that are taken as given and, in consumer theory, as non-satiated, so that more of a good is always preferred or at least not refused. Economists commonly present non-satiation as a modelling assumption rather than a claim about human nature, and the theory of choice can be formulated without it. In practice, the assumption that wants exceed means organises the discipline’s central problems: allocation, efficiency, and growth. The darshan inverts the ordering. Nature is abundant, bodily needs are definite, and scarcity appears where indefinite want, or a deliberate withholding of goods, meets finite means.

          4.5 Absolute and relative needs

          John Maynard Keynes distinguished absolute needs, felt whatever the situation of others, from relative needs, satisfied only if they lift a person above others. The latter, he wrote, may indeed be insatiable, while the former could be met within a century, after which the economic problem might cease to be the permanent problem of humankind (Keynes 1930). Thorstein Veblen had described conspicuous consumption and pecuniary emulation, in which goods serve as marks of standing (Veblen 1899). Fred Hirsch argued that positional goods, whose value depends on others not having them, set social limits to growth that more production cannot overcome (Hirsch 1976).

          These accounts come nearest to the darshan’s distinction between definite bodily need and indefinite want. They locate insatiability in social comparison. The darshan agrees that comparison of more and less is a form of delusion: the method of relativity keeps widening the distances of big and small, more and less, and a point of contentment is needed that is the same in all humans (AA, p. 60). It traces comparison further back, to a that has not found its own satisfaction and so measures itself against others.

          4.6 Money, credit, and growth

          Most money in modern economies is created by commercial banks when they make loans, rather than lent out from prior deposits, and its quantity is limited mainly by the demand for credit, the banks’ assessment of risk, and monetary policy (BoE 2014). Interest on debt-created money is one of the mechanisms through which ecological economists argue that present economies depend on continued growth (Jackson 2017). Silvio Gesell made the darshan’s contrast between perishable goods and imperishable money the centre of his reform. Because goods decay and money does not, money holders can demand interest as the price of releasing money into circulation; he proposed money that loses value over time, so that it circulates like the goods it represents (Gesell, Natural Economic Order). The darshan’s critique of symbolic value reaches the same point by a different road, and its account of the exchange-reserve aims to remove the occasion for interest rather than to tax money’s advantage.

          4.7 Ecological and post-growth economics

          Nicholas Georgescu-Roegen argued that the economic process transforms low-entropy matter and energy into high-entropy waste, so that no economy can grow indefinitely on a finite earth (Georgescu-Roegen 1971). Herman Daly proposed a steady-state economy, with constant stocks of people and artefacts maintained by the lowest feasible throughput of matter and energy (Daly 1974). Kate Raworth’s doughnut joins a social foundation of human needs to an ecological ceiling of planetary boundaries (Raworth 2017), and Tim Jackson defines prosperity as the capability to flourish within ecological limits rather than as rising consumption (Jackson 2017). Earth-system scientists now assess six of nine planetary boundaries as transgressed (Richardson et al. 2023). The circular economy, as promoted in industry and policy, seeks to keep materials in use and design waste out of production.

          These programmes share the darshan’s judgement of unlimited growth and its insistence that production stay within natural balance. Their accounts of why growth continues are institutional: debt, competition, employment, and political pressure. The darshan adds a reason at the level of the person, and its cycle includes the renewal of human capacity and the closure of need, not only the closure of material loops.

          4.8 Gandhian economics: permanence and right livelihood

          J. C. Kumarappa contrasted an economy of transience, which draws down stocks accumulated by nature over ages, with an economy of permanence, which lives within the cycles of nature and recognises the interdependence of all its parts (Kumarappa 1945). He classified economies by their relation to others and to nature, from the parasitic and predatory to those of service, and advocated decentralised village production. E. F. Schumacher’s “Buddhist economics” sought maximum well-being with minimum consumption, treated non-renewable resources as capital to be conserved, and held that work develops human faculties rather than being only a cost (Schumacher 1973). Of all the economic traditions considered here, these are closest to avartansheel arthshastra in content: village autonomy, cyclical energy and agriculture, production by the many, and restraint in wants.

          4.9 Needs, knowledge, and institutions

          Several bodies of work address the questions that cyclical economics must answer in practice. Manfred Max-Neef and his colleagues argued that fundamental human needs are few, finite, and classifiable, and the same across cultures and periods, while the means of satisfying them, the satisfiers, vary (Max-Neef et al. 1989). Len Doyal and Ian Gough grounded universal basic needs in health and autonomy, and distinguished them from wants and preferences (Doyal and Gough 1991). Friedrich Hayek argued that knowledge of particular circumstances is dispersed among many people and that the price system communicates it without any central authority possessing it (Hayek 1945). Elinor Ostrom showed that communities can govern shared resources through polycentric institutions that neither markets nor states supply (Ostrom 2010). Each of these bears on a specific part of the darshan’s proposal: the definiteness of need, the information that exchange beyond the village requires, and the governance of common natural assets.

          5. Indian and Advaita teaching on desire

          5.1 Desire as an unquenchable fire

          The insatiability of desire is a familiar theme in Indian thought. The Bhagavad Gita traces a chain from dwelling on sense objects to attachment, from attachment to desire, from desire to anger, and from anger to delusion and the loss of discrimination (BG 2.62–63). It names desire, born of rajas, as the enemy that covers knowledge, an insatiable fire (BG 3.37–39). The Manusmriti states that desire is never extinguished by the enjoyment of its objects, but grows like a fire fed with clarified butter (Manu 2.94). These texts agree with the darshan that enjoyment does not quiet desire and that desire obscures understanding.

          5.2 The fullness of the Self and the place of wealth

          Advaita Vedanta explains desire by ignorance of the Self. The Self is complete, and things are dear for the sake of the Self rather than for their own sake; desire arises when the Self is mistaken for the body and mind, so that a limited being seeks completion in objects (Axiology: Value Theory §§2.1–2.4; BU 2.4.5). The remedy is knowledge of the Self, prepared by disciplined action without attachment to its fruits (BG 3.19). Wealth, artha, is a legitimate aim of life when pursued within , but liberation stands above it, and the renunciate’s path leaves economic life behind (Axiology: Value Theory §§2.2, 2.4–2.5).

          The structural parallel with the darshan is clear. In both, insatiability arises from mistaking one’s being for the body and seeking in objects a fulfilment that objects cannot give. The darshan locates the remedy in understanding and coexistence, which is directed into family, production, and exchange, while Advaita locates it in knowledge of a Self for which the world of exchange has only empirical reality.

          6. The sciences of satiation and natural limits

          6.1 Homeostatic satiety

          Physiology confirms that the body regulates its own intake. The central nervous system integrates signals from fat stores and the gut, among them leptin, insulin, and ghrelin, to match food intake to energy expenditure and to keep body weight within a range over long periods (Morton et al. 2006). Bodily need has a definite measure, and the body has mechanisms that recognise when it has been met.

          6.2 Wanting, liking, and hedonic hunger

          The same research distinguishes this homeostatic regulation from motivation that runs beyond need. Kent Berridge and Terry Robinson separate “wanting”, the incentive salience mediated largely by dopamine systems, from “liking”, the hedonic impact of a reward. The two can come apart: wanting can be sensitised and grow while liking does not, which helps explain compulsive pursuit of rewards that no longer satisfy (Berridge and Robinson 2016). Michael Lowe and Meghan Butryn describe “hedonic hunger”, appetite driven by the anticipated pleasure of food in the absence of any energy deficit, as distinct from homeostatic hunger (Lowe and Butryn 2007). These findings give an empirical counterpart to the darshan’s distinction between definite bodily need and the inexhaustible cycle of selection and taste.

          6.3 Adaptation and the income–well-being debate

          People adapt to many changes in circumstance, though less completely than the original “hedonic treadmill” theory supposed. Set points are not neutral, differ among individuals, and can change (Diener et al. 2006). Richard Easterlin found that richer people within a country report greater happiness, while average happiness did not rise as national income grew over time (Easterlin 1974). Daniel Kahneman and Angus Deaton found that life evaluation rises with the logarithm of income while day-to-day emotional well-being levelled off at moderate incomes (Kahneman and Deaton 2010). Later work found no plateau on average, and a joint reanalysis located the plateau among the least happy minority (Killingsworth 2021; Killingsworth et al. 2023). Axiology: Value Theory §4.3 discusses this evidence. The data support neither a simple satiation point for income nor its absence. They show that more income does not close the gap between life evaluation and lived experience for many people, which is consistent with, though it does not establish, the darshan’s claim that material means cannot meet the need being placed on them.

          6.4 Earth-system limits

          The biogeochemical cycles that sustain life, of carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, and water, have been altered by human activity on a scale that now pushes several of them beyond the conditions of the past ten thousand years (Richardson et al. 2023). Thermodynamics sets a further limit on every production process (Georgescu-Roegen 1971). The darshan’s claim that extraction disturbs the earth’s balance and that production must keep to the proportions of natural regeneration is, in its general form, consistent with this science. Several of its specific causal hypotheses, such as the possible effects of extraction on the earth’s magnetic current or of cosmic rays on the stability of water (AA, pp. 31, 80–82), are not supported by current earth science and are treated as open in §9.7.

          7. Comparison

          7.1 Where insatiability comes from

          The traditions disagree most sharply on the source of insatiable want. Neoclassical theory takes non-satiation as given and does not ask its source. Aristotle and Marx locate limitlessness in the pursuit of money as such. Veblen, Keynes, and Hirsch locate it in social comparison and positional goods. The Gita and Advaita locate it in desire arising from ignorance of the Self. The sciences describe mechanisms, sensitised wanting, hedonic hunger, and adaptation, without a theory of what the person is seeking. The darshan places the source in the joint form: a whose higher activities lack understanding, whose discontent is read as a shortage of objects, and whose inexhaustible capacity for selection and taste is left without regulation. On this account the other explanations describe real consequences of the same error.

          7.2 What counts as wealth, and what closes the cycle

          For the money economy, wealth is measured in currency and its growth is the aim. For classical and Marxian economics, wealth is the product of labour, but capital accumulates in money. For ecological economics, wealth must stay within throughput limits, and the circular economy closes material loops. For the darshan, wealth is only the produced object; money is a symbol; and the cycle closes in three places together: need is definite, goods are used rather than hoarded, and natural assets are used in proportion to their regeneration. The circular economy addresses the third. Daly’s steady state addresses the second and third. Only the darshan, Gandhian economics, and in part Keynes make the first, the definiteness of need, a condition of the economy’s structure.

          8. Making the cycle visible: measurement, instruments, and artificial intelligence

          8.1 What can be measured and what must be judged

          The darshan joins two kinds of order in one sequence. Knowledge is law, law is regulation, and regulation is balance; balanced living is justice in humans; living within universal orderliness with justice is , that is, resolution; and realisation in coexistence is dharma and truth (MVD, p. 173). Law (niyam), regulation (niyantran), and balance (santulan) are already evident in the orders other than the human, which are bound by their own laws (AA, p. 117). Justice (), dharma, and truth () are the evaluations by which humans establish their own orderliness, and they are the activities the treatise describes as still under review in human tradition (AA, p. 18).

          This distinction suggests a division of labour for any instrument used in a cyclical economy. Where a relation is governed by law, regulation, or balance, and can be stated as a quantity, a rate, or a rule, it can be measured, recorded, and in part handled by an instrument: the regeneration rate of a forest, the fertility of a field, the stock in a store, the labour time in a product, the balance of an account. Where a question concerns the fulfilment of a relationship, the purpose of a choice, or its accord with coexistence, it belongs to human evaluation. The treatise itself anticipates the first part. It prescribes a detailed survey before a village begins its self-governing system (AA, pp. 122–125), sets measurable criteria for evaluating each committee (AA, pp. 146–149), expects computers to be used in the exchange-reserve (AA, p. 138), and envisages every committee linked by a computer system from the village to the world, so that information located anywhere is available to the village (AA, p. 132).

          Artificial Intelligence and Coexistence §§1.2–1.5 places present AI systems in the material order as instruments, yantra. Such instruments can reproduce the outward pattern of evaluation by pleasure, advantage, and gain, and so amplify delusion, or, directed by humane criteria, they can make the basis of a decision visible to the people who make it. An economy organised for profit will use AI to raise profit. A cyclical economy can use the same capacities for a different purpose: to make the conditions of the cycle visible quickly, so that failures are seen before they compound, and to return to people the questions that only people can answer.

          8.3 What the instrument can carry

          Within this framework an AI system can carry several kinds of work. It can maintain the village survey as a living record rather than a one-time exercise, and summarise it for families and committees in plain language. It can keep the exchange-reserve’s accounts, track stock and its age, and record the labour time and means that the valuation of labour requires (AA, pp. 119, 145). It can compare extraction with regeneration for each natural asset and project when a trend will cross a limit. It can watch the indicators in §8.2 continuously, flag a failure point as soon as a signal appears, and show the evidence that produced the flag. Where a rule has been settled by the people concerned, for example the proportion of a forest that may be cut in a year, the reordering of a store, or the routing of surplus between villages, the instrument can apply it and report what it did. Across the tiers from village to world, it can aggregate records so that a district or province sees the balance of production and need across its villages, which is the coordination problem that markets solve through prices (§4.9).

          Each of these tasks lies within law, regulation, and balance. Each is measurement, accounting, projection, or the application of a rule already settled. The instrument’s value is speed and visibility. It shortens the time between a failure’s first trace and its recognition, and it makes the state of the cycle legible to everyone who participates in it.

          8.4 What must return to people

          The questions in the last column of §8.2 are of a different kind. Whether a family’s assessment of need is true and fair, whether an exchange is free of exploitation, whether a member’s share in production matches ability and receptivity, whether a technique accords with orderliness, and whether a use of the forest serves the earth’s balance are questions of justice, dharma, and truth. The treatise assigns them to people and institutions: the family representative evaluates the members of the family, the family cluster evaluates its representative, the village council decides the method of valuing objects, and the justice-security committee resolves disputes through a reformative process (AA, pp. 67, 128–129, 139–140). An instrument that flags a failure point therefore ends its work by presenting the evidence and the question to the people responsible, not by deciding it.

          Several limits follow from the darshan’s own account. The instrument must not be directed towards the perspectives that drive the open spiral, maximising throughput, engagement, or gain, since that would turn it into an amplifier of the error described in §2 (AI study §1.5). Its records belong to the families and villages that produce them and must remain open to them, because accountability to the villagers is a duty of the council (AA, p. 128). It must not become a new seat of rulership: power and centralised rule carry the direction of non-coexistence, and universal orderliness is participatory (AA, p. 82). And its own material and energy use belongs within the cycle it serves, counted like any other production (AI study §1.7).

          8.5 The scope of this proposal

          The use of AI described here is an application of the darshan’s distinctions, not a claim found in the texts, which speak of computers only as aids to information and accounting. Whether settled rules can be stated precisely enough for an instrument to apply them, whether flagging a failure changes behaviour, and whether communities can govern such systems without ceding judgement to them are empirical questions, collected with the other open problems in §9.7. The companion technical note on a personal AI assistant sets out tests of source fidelity and human benefit that a system of this kind would also need.

          9. Critical review

          9.1 Madhyasth Darshan — an explanation of insatiability with a demanding institutional path

          Strengths.

          • It explains rather than postulates insatiability. The distinction between the body’s definite needs and ’s need for continuity of happiness, together with the account of an inexhaustible selection-and-taste cycle without higher regulation, gives a mechanism for why want outruns need and why the satiation point recedes.
          • It unifies diagnoses that other traditions hold separately: Aristotle’s and Marx’s account of limitless money-making, Keynes’s and Hirsch’s relative needs, the ecological account of throughput, and the Gita’s account of desire.
          • Its definition of wealth as produced objects and of capital as human labour power keeps economics tied to provision and makes the critique of symbolic value precise.
          • Its cycle includes the renewal of human capacity and the closure of need, not only the closure of material loops, and it nests the economy within the balances of nature.
          • It offers a concrete institutional path, with surveys, an exchange-reserve, a staged transition from market prices to labour values, and evaluation criteria for each committee.

          Weaknesses.

          • The definiteness of need depends on awakening, which the darshan itself describes as not yet established in human tradition. The economic proposal therefore rests on a prior transformation whose spread has not been demonstrated at scale.
          • The valuation of labour by pace, time, and means meets the problem Marx faced in reducing heterogeneous labour to a common measure. Midpoint averages may work for wheat within a village; their extension to complex, multi-stage products made across many places is not worked out.
          • Coordination beyond the village relies on councils and committees. The information problem that prices address (§4.9) is acknowledged only indirectly, through the transitional use of market rates.
          • The critique of specialisation (AA, pp. 70–71) sits uneasily with the special aspirations the darshan endorses, telephony, broadcasting, and transport, which depend on highly specialised production; the treatise’s own allowance for large industries at higher tiers suggests the objection is to specialisation severed from wholeness, but this is not made explicit.
          • Several of the treatise’s natural-science claims, on the earth’s magnetic current and on cosmic rays and water, go beyond present evidence and weaken its ecological argument where its general claims are well supported.

          gives the most complete account of why human economies do not reach balance, and its institutional proposal is more specific than most alternatives, but its practicability depends on conditions it has yet to evidence beyond small communities.

          9.2 Neoclassical economics — coordination without a stopping point

          Strengths.

          • It provides powerful tools for coordinating production and exchange among very large numbers of people who do not know one another.
          • Its methods of measurement, national accounts, input–output analysis, and the study of incentives, are indispensable to any economy that must assess its own condition.
          • Treating non-satiation as an assumption rather than a doctrine allows its models to be adapted.

          Weaknesses.

          • By taking wants as given, it has no account of why they grow and no criterion for when enough has been produced.
          • Its measure of success, growth of output valued in money, counts depletion of natural assets as income and rewards artificial scarcity.
          • Its separation of economics from the purposes of human life leaves those purposes to be supplied by whatever wants prevail.

          Neoclassical economics coordinates without a stopping point, and its tools can serve a cyclical economy only if that economy supplies the criteria the discipline leaves out.

          9.3 Marxian and ecological economics — a sound diagnosis on a different ground

          Strengths.

          • Marx’s analysis of the circuit of capital and of the endlessness of accumulation in money matches the darshan’s critique closely.
          • Ecological economics grounds the limits of growth in thermodynamics and earth-system science and has developed measures of throughput and planetary boundaries.

          Weaknesses.

          • Marx’s reliance on class conflict as the motor of change conflicts with the darshan’s account of complementarity and has produced coercive regimes in practice.
          • Ecological economics explains continued growth by institutions, debt, competition, and employment, without an account of the person whose wants those institutions serve, and so it struggles to make restraint chosen rather than imposed.

          Marxian and ecological economics diagnose the open spiral soundly from the side of capital and of nature, and the darshan supplies the side of the person that both leave undeveloped.

          9.4 Gandhian economics — the closest relative

          Strengths.

          • Kumarappa’s economy of permanence and Schumacher’s Buddhist economics share the darshan’s village autonomy, cyclical agriculture and energy, production by the many, and restraint of wants.
          • They have a long record of practical experiment in Indian villages and in appropriate-technology movements.

          Weaknesses.

          • Their restraint of wants rests on moral and religious exhortation, simplicity as a virtue, rather than on an account of why wants become insatiable and how understanding makes them definite.
          • They give little attention to the valuation of labour and to a staged transition from money exchange.

          Gandhian economics is the nearest relative of cyclical economics in practice, and the darshan offers it a foundation in the understanding of jeevan that it lacks.

          9.5 Advaita and Indian ethics — a diagnosis of desire without an economic architecture

          Strengths.

          • The Gita and Advaita give a penetrating account of desire as arising from mistaken identification with body and mind, and agree with the darshan that enjoyment does not quiet desire.
          • The ordering of artha under gives wealth a place without making it the aim of life.

          Weaknesses.

          • Their remedy is directed inward, to knowledge of the Self and detachment, and offers no institutional design for production and exchange.
          • Treating the empirical world as of lower reality makes the economy a field to be transcended rather than an order to be established.

          Advaita Vedanta and Indian ethics share the darshan’s diagnosis of desire, and the darshan differs in turning the remedy outward into family, production, and exchange.

          9.6 What the darshan can learn from economics

          Five lessons from economic theory bear directly on cyclical economics. First, coordination beyond the village needs information, and the treatise’s own transitional reliance on market rates shows that prices carry knowledge which councils will have to obtain in some other form; the instruments discussed in §8 are one possible way. Second, prosperity and production security can be evidenced only if they are measured, and national accounting, input–output analysis, material-flow accounting, and life-cycle assessment provide methods for doing so. Third, Ostrom’s work on commons shows that the governance of shared natural assets can be designed and tested, which bears on the village’s jurisdiction over its forests, water, and minerals. Fourth, the theories of human need of Max-Neef, Doyal, and Gough can sharpen the family’s assessment of need and guard it against the charge of paternalism. Fifth, an account of money as it actually works, as credit created through lending, would let the darshan’s critique of symbolic value address present institutions directly rather than a commodity-money system that no longer exists.

          9.7 Open problems

          Several questions remain unsettled.

          • Need at scale. How does family-level assessment of need aggregate across a district or a nation, and how are disagreements between families’ assessments resolved without either imposing a standard or abandoning definiteness?
          • Labour valuation for complex goods. How can the value of labour be measured for products that pass through many hands and places, and how does the midpoint method extend beyond simple agricultural goods?
          • Coordination without prices. What replaces the information carried by prices in the third stage of the transition, and how much can measurement and AI instruments supply?
          • Specialisation and the special aspirations. What division of labour is compatible with the darshan’s critique of specialisation, given that telephony, broadcasting, and transport require it?
          • Transition within a debt economy. How does an exchange-reserve working without interest coexist with banks that create money through interest-bearing loans, and what happens when the guarantor bank’s terms change?
          • Evidence. What observations in communities practising the darshan would show that needs become definite, that production exceeds need, and that material throughput falls? No such evidence has been assembled for this study.
          • Natural-science claims. Which of the treatise’s claims about the earth’s balance, beyond the general dependence of production on natural regeneration, can be tested, and how should those not supported by present earth science be treated?
          • Instruments and judgement. Can communities govern AI systems of the kind described in §8 without ceding to them the judgements of justice, dharma, and truth, and can such systems be kept from being directed towards profit?
          • Source status. Which interpretations in this study will change when the working translation of Avartansheel Arthshastra has been reviewed against the Hindi, particularly for the definition of cyclicality and the pairing of use, right use, and purposeful use with justice, resolution, and authenticity?

          10. Conclusion

          Human economies do not reach balance because the need they are asked to meet is not the need they can meet. The body’s requirements are definite, and the rest of nature shows what balance with them looks like. seeks continuity of happiness, and in delusion it pursues that continuity through the transient satisfactions of selection and taste, reading each failure as a shortage of objects. Accumulation, especially of money, gives this pursuit an object that never decays and never satisfies, and an economy organised around it extracts from the earth without limit and calls its imbalance growth.

          Cyclical economics answers by closing the cycle at the points where it now runs open. Need is assessed and made definite within the family; labour applied to natural abundance produces objects for use; the objects nourish and protect the body and sustain social life, which renews the capacity to produce; labour is valued by the usefulness it establishes, and exchange follows that value, so that remuneration equals production and leaves no room for profit; production stays within the proportions of natural regeneration; and the remainder flows into right use. The economy is one dimension of a self-governing order of families and villages, and it cannot be separated from education, health, and justice.

          Economic theory has much to teach this proposal about coordination, measurement, commons, needs, and money, and modern instruments can make the conditions of the cycle visible far faster than surveys and ledgers alone. The darshan’s distinction between law, regulation, and balance on one side and justice, , and truth on the other gives these instruments a clear place: they can measure, account, warn, and apply settled rules, and they must return to people the questions of whether a need is real, an exchange just, and a use consistent with the order of the whole. The economy the darshan describes depends finally on understanding, and no instrument can supply that. What instruments can do is show quickly and plainly where understanding is missing.

          Appendix: Quick Glossary

          • Cyclical economics (avartansheel arthshastra): the right use, security, and prosperity of resources in the form of body, mind, and wealth, organised as a cycle that returns to its starting point with its purpose fulfilled. The working economy is the cyclical economy (avartansheel arthvyavastha).
          • Cyclicality (avartansheelta): complementarity and mutual sustenance, a movement that passes through complementary activity towards a goal and returns to its starting point.
          • Resources (artha): body, mind, and wealth together.
          • Wealth (dhan): produced objects carrying usefulness value and aesthetic value; not money.
          • Natural abundance (prakritik aishvarya): earth, air, water, forest, and minerals, the sites where labour is utilised.
          • Value (mulya): a unit’s participation in the orderliness of the whole; for produced things, their usefulness and aesthetic value.
          • Value of labour (shram mulya): the value of human labour, judged by the usefulness and aesthetic value it establishes; the basis on which produced objects are valued and exchanged. Valuing labour is the act of evaluating that usefulness and aesthetic value through the labour that established them.
          • Common aspirations and special aspirations (samanya akanksha, mahatvakanksha): food, shelter, and adornment; telephony, broadcasting, and transport.
          • Production in excess of need (avashyakta se adhik utpadan): the condition of prosperity in every family.
          • Prosperity (samriddhi): the absence of the sentiment of lacking; production in excess of need, with its right use and security.
          • Use, right use, and purposeful use (upyog, sadupyog, prayojansheelta): utilisation of body, mind, and wealth in the nourishment and protection of the body and the motion of society; in orderliness and undivided society; and in evidencing understanding from generation to generation.
          • Exchange-reserve (vinimay kosh): the village institution through which produced objects are exchanged without profit and loss.
          • Accumulation (sangrah): gathering beyond use; hoarding where it exceeds need.
          • Profit-obsession (labhonmad): the pursuit of giving less and taking more without a satiation point.
          • Symbolic currency (prateek mudra): metal or paper currency standing for objects; its value is symbolic value.
          • Satiation point (tripti bindu): the point at which an activity is fulfilled; accumulation, comfort, and profit have none.
          • Autonomous human (swayatt manav): a person who trusts in oneself, respects excellence, is balanced in talent and personality, social in behaviour, and self-reliant in occupation.
          • Balance (santulan): the definite, mutually sustaining condition that each order maintains in its existent state; for the human order it is evidenced as resolution, prosperity, fearlessness, and coexistence.
          • Law, regulation, and balance (niyam, niyantran, santulan): the orderliness evident in nature, which humans understand and maintain.
          • Justice, , and truth (, dharma, ): the human evaluations of relationship, of participation in universal orderliness, and of coexistence.

          Editorial Notes

          Source status of Avartansheel Arthshastra

          Avartansheel Arthshastra (AA) is cited from the project’s working English translation of the 2024 printing, a complete first pass that is under the author’s review and not yet fidelity-reviewed against the Hindi page by page. Following the practice adopted for the Karm Darshan working translation, AA is paraphrased with page citations and is not block-quoted; the block quotations come from Rakesh Gupta’s published translation of Vidya. Page numbers are the printed pages of the book body. Passages whose Hindi structure is unclear are indexed in the translation workspace; where the argument depends on one, the reading adopted is recorded in the notes below, on the definition of cyclicality and on the pairings of use with justice, resolution, and authenticity. Before this study is released, every AA passage it relies on should be checked against the page images and its paraphrase revised where the review changes the reading.

          The definition of cyclicality

          The treatise’s definition of cyclicality (AA, p. 15) is flagged as unclear in the translation workspace (U-011). Its central phrase is an unpunctuated compound of natural abundance, naturalness, and natural complementarity, and it is not certain what is “linked”: resources, the process, or the points of the cycle. §3.2 adopts the reading that a cycle starts from any point, passes through complementary activity in the sense of prosperity towards the fulfilment of a goal, and returns to its starting point. This reading is supported by the examples on the same page, by the identification of cyclicality with complementarity and mutual sustenance (AA, p. 42), and by the provision sequence (AA, p. 106). The study’s argument does not depend on choosing among the parsings.

          The provision cycle and replenishment

          The project’s introductory primer presents avartansheel as cyclical or replenishing production, attending to what is taken from nature and returned. The Jeevan Vidya lectures define the cycle as the renewal of human capacity through production, nourishment, and social participation (JV, p. 154). The treatise supports both and relates them. The provision cycle is the definition (AA, pp. 26, 106), and the use of natural assets in proportion to their production, without obstructing and while helping their production, is its condition (AA, pp. 114, 142, 146). §3.3 and §3.7 present them in that relation.

          Cyclicality and natural recurrence

          The Karm Darshan working translation renders sahajavritti and anukshan-vikshan- as “natural recurrence”. These terms concern the continuity of contemplation in jeevan, not economic or natural cyclicality, and are not used in this study.

          The synthesis on insatiability

          §2.3’s claim that insatiability arises from pursuing a continuous need of jeevan through transient means is assembled from connected passages: the four and a half effective activities and the internal conflict of the rest (JV, pp. 72–73), the inexhaustibility of selection and taste (AA, pp. 17–18), the transience of sensory satisfaction (JV, pp. 162–163; AA, p. 48), the indefiniteness of needs pursued as indulgence (MVD, p. 128), and the regulation of taste by the higher faculties in awakening (AA, p. 95). No single passage states the whole claim, and its phrasing as a “category error” is this study’s.

          Pairings of use with justice, resolution, and authenticity

          The treatise pairs usefulness with justice, right use with resolution, and purposeful use with authenticity (AA, p. 116). A later passage describes usefulness as the exposition of the primacy of justice, , and truth “in sequence” (AA, p. 118), which is flagged as unclear (U-129) and may have lost the other two terms of the triad. §3.6 follows the clearer passage.

          Specialisation

          The treatise criticises specialisation for losing sight of wholeness, citing pest control and fertiliser that damage the agriculture they were meant to serve (AA, pp. 70–71), and criticises specialists who demand much while producing little (JV, p. 153). It also endorses telephony, broadcasting, and transport as special aspirations and provides for large industries at the higher tiers of self-governance (AA, pp. 29–30, 115). The study reads the criticism as directed at specialisation severed from wholeness and from production, and records the tension in §9.1 and §9.7.

          Market rates in the transition

          In the first stage of exchange, the exchange-reserve values local produce at the nearby wholesale rate less transport (AA, pp. 136–137, 142). This is a transitional use of prices, not a departure from valuation by labour, which the treatise reaches in the third stage. §9.6 reads it also as evidence that prices carry information which a labour-based system will need to obtain in another way.

          Artificial intelligence as an application

          The texts do not discuss artificial intelligence. §8 applies the darshan’s distinction between law, regulation, and balance and justice, dharma, and truth (MVD, p. 173; AA, pp. 18, 117), together with the treatise’s provisions for surveys, committee evaluation, and computer-linked information (AA, pp. 122–125, 132, 138, 146–149), to the question of what an instrument can carry in a cyclical economy. It follows the placement of AI in the material order and the distinction between amplifying and examining uses developed in Artificial Intelligence and Coexistence. The division of tasks proposed there is this study’s extension.

          Terminology

          Santulan is rendered as balance throughout, following MVD’s account of each order as balanced in its existent state (MVD, p. 105). “Stability” was considered for the study’s question but would suggest mere persistence, whereas santulan names a mutually sustaining relation with the rest of nature. The English renderings of AA’s economic vocabulary follow the decisions approved for the translation and adopted project-wide in October 2026: cyclical economics and cyclical economy for avartansheel arthshastra and arthvyavastha, cyclicality for avartansheelta, value of labour for shram mulya, production in excess of need for avashyakta se adhik utpadan, satiation point for tripti bindu, exchange-reserve for vinimay kosh, and food, shelter, and adornment for ahar, avas, alankar. Where the published JV translation uses “clothing” for alankar in a quotation, the quotation keeps its wording.
