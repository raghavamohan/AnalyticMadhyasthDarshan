          Prosperity Economics and Right Use

          Author: AnalyticMadhyasthDarshan.org — a group of people studying philosophy. Source repository: raghavamohan/AnalyticMadhyasthDarshan.

          Edited on: October 18, 2026, 7:22 AM IST

          The question: Why do human economies, unlike every other order of nature, fail to reach balance (santulan), and what would an economy look like that gives human labour its true value, judged by the usefulness it establishes for human living and for the wider order of nature, rather than by scarcity, accumulation, and price?

          A forest, a herd, or a field left to itself settles into a definite balance with its surroundings. Soil feeds plants, plants return to soil, and animals take what their bodies need and then stop. The human body belongs to the same nature. Its requirements for food, shelter, and protection are definite and can be counted, and in principle they could be met and then held in balance like those of any other organism. Human economies nevertheless behave differently. They extract more each generation, accumulate wealth that no one can use, and treat a continuing rise in consumption as the measure of success. Mainstream economics takes this imbalance as its starting point: wants are assumed to be unlimited and means scarce, so economic life becomes the permanent allocation of what is never enough.

          Heterodox and reformist traditions—classical and Marxian political economy, mutual-credit and institutional economics, ecological and post-growth economics, Gandhian village economics, and basic-needs or capability frameworks—challenge parts of that mainstream picture, yet none has been able to close the economic cycle across a whole society. Each hits a structural ceiling because all of them, like the orthodoxy they criticise, model the human agent without an explicit ontology of the conscious unit () and without realisation () of coexistence. So long as the human being is treated as a single, body-bound organism whose evaluations rest on subjective pleasure, bodily advantage, and gain (priya, hit, labh) and for whom work is a disutility endured for extrinsic reward, six problems become jointly insoluble: (1) demand rebounds into new goods, status rivalry, or financial accumulation even when basic bodily needs are met, while moral calls for ascetic restraint repeatedly erode; (2) subjective preferences admit no uncoerced interpersonal agreement on value or sufficiency; (3) removing profit and wage fear dulls work effort and technical innovation unless replaced by bureaucratic coercion; (4) unconditional need-based sharing and unpaid public service break down beyond biological kin; (5) interest-free ledgers and labour-time accounting are gamed through strategic withholding and shadow pricing; and (6) agents who imagine private prosperity apart from the whole discount the distant future, rendering multi-century ecological restraint fragile.

          Madhyasth Darshan introduces a fundamentally different premise. The human being is the joint form of a finite chemical-physical body and jeevan, a constitutionally complete sentient atom (gathan-poorna parmanu) whose powers of hope, thought, and desire are inexhaustible (akshay) and continuously surging (uditodit), and which has the capacity to realise (anubhav) in the coexistence of all-pervasive omnipresence (, vyapak) and the whole of nature at once (AA, pp. 16–18, 47–51; MVD, p. 203). The body’s needs are definite; jeevan seeks continuity of happiness, which only understanding and realisation (anubhav) in coexistence can fulfil. In delusion, jeevan operates through only four and a half of its ten activities and evaluates by pleasure, bodily advantage, and gain. Its inexhaustible energy is then forced through finite sensory organs, and the dissatisfaction of its unexpressed higher faculties is misread as a shortage of material objects. Because sensory satisfaction is transient while jeevan’s demand for continuity cannot be switched off, want loses its stopping point and locks onto money, the one symbolic object that escapes physical decay. Conversely, when awakening opens all ten activities of jeevan through realisation in the coexistence of satta (vyapak) and nature as a whole at once—an observer-invariant reality (trikalabadh , AA, pp. 47, 51, 58–60; MVD, p. 203)—jeevan’s inexhaustible powers find continuous, non-entropic fulfilment in understanding, relationship, creative labour, and benevolence (upkar). The body is relieved of the impossible task of supplying infinite happiness through finite matter.

          Shri A. Nagraj set out the economic architecture of this awakening in Arthshastra, cyclical economics. Body, mind, and wealth are deployed as labour on natural abundance; the objects produced nourish and protect the body and support social life; and these renew the capacity to produce. Exchange rests on the value of labour, need is assessed and shared without individual labour accounting within the ten-member family, production exceeds that need because jeevan’s powers are inexhaustible while bodily intake is finite, and the surplus flows naturally into right use and unpaid benevolent service (upkar-pradhan seva) in society.

          At the centre of this alternative is a different valuation of human labour. In the darshan, the value (mulya) of anything is its participation in the order of the whole, what its activity fulfils for others (Axiology: Value Theory §1.1). Labour is the outward projection (paravartan) and inward strengthening (pratyavartan) of jeevan powers through bodily dexterity to establish usefulness and beauty in what nature provides (AA, pp. 18–19, 48–50), so the true value of labour lies in the usefulness it establishes: in nourishing and protecting the body, in sustaining social life, and, through right use, in the orderliness of family, society, and nature (Axiology §§1.5–1.6). The money economy values labour instead by the price it can command. Labour is bought and sold like any other commodity, the accumulation of currency becomes the measure of a person’s standing, and the producer of useful objects has generally remained poor (AA, pp. 7, 45, 52, 73).

          The study reconstructs this account from the treatise and the darshan’s other texts (§§1–3), examines economic theory, Indian and Advaita teaching on desire, and the sciences of satiation and natural limits to show both what each contributes and why none can close the cycle without jeevan and realisation (§§4–7), and establishes why a strong multi-level AI agentic backbone—paired with individuals, families, committees, and councils across all ten tiers—is not an optional convenience but a structural requirement for operating a cyclical economy at planetary scale while human beings are still in the process of realisation (§8). Its central thesis, assessed in the critical review (§9), is that Avartansheel Arthshastra is not an eclectic combination of existing heterodox reforms: by grounding economics in the ontology of jeevan and the observer-invariant realisation of coexistence, it resolves the structural deadlocks that block every body-centric economic theory, while requiring an intelligent agentic backbone to make facts, material balances, and logical inconsistencies continuously visible to collective councils so that communities across the Earth can act and self-correct together without coercive bureaucracy.

          1. Why human economies do not reach balance

          1.1 Balance in every order

          describes nature as four orders: material, biological (plant), animal, and knowledge, the last being the human. Each order maintains its own definite conduct. The material order keeps its conduct through the conformance of its constitution and results; plants reproduce their kind through seed conformance; animals live within the conduct of their species. Every unit of nature except the human exhibits definite conduct, and the human reaches definite conduct only through , the acceptances formed by understanding (JV, pp. 47–48; AA, pp. 54–55). All traditions other than the human are bound by their own laws and are already orderliness; for the human alone, recognising its own orderliness remains a task (AA, p. 117).

          The same account holds that each order is balanced in its existent state. The material order has the innateness of existence, the plant order of existence and growth, and the animal order of existence, growth, and the hope of living, and each is balanced in its existent state. The knowledge order is balanced in its existent state only when it evidences resolution, prosperity, fearlessness, and coexistence (MVD, p. 105). The earth itself is described in these terms. Its seasonal regularity is maintained by definite proportions of minerals and plants, and the coal and mineral oil buried in its interior had a part in making the planet’s heat balance and its later greenery possible (AA, pp. 30, 78–79; KD §3.7, pp. 73–81). Prosperity, on this view, is first a property of the earth, which became rich in itself before any human labour touched it (AA, pp. 77–78).

          The conclusion drawn from this account is stark. Whatever unit causes disorder in existence is the deluded human being alone (AA, p. 41). Nature other than human is already complementary to the human; the awakening needed for the human to be complementary to the rest of nature is still awaited (AA, p. 42).

          1.2 The definite needs of the body

          The human body is a chemical and physical formation (AA, p. 16). Its needs are of two kinds. Common aspirations are food, shelter, and adornment, together with the objects and equipment needed for health and education; special aspirations are telephony, broadcasting, and transport (AA, pp. 26, 73, 93, 112; JV, p. 113). Both are needs for objects, and objects have definite properties. Grain can be stored for a few years and then rots; a machine left unused rusts; what is produced is for use, and hoarding beyond a point becomes a problem in itself (JV, p. 152). The accumulation of material objects is not feasible, because their use or transformation is inevitable (MVD, p. 183). Even prosperity is a temporal need: work for it is necessary while the body lives, and the need ends with the body (MVD, p. 96). The body also sets the limit of consumption. A person can know more than they can desire, desire more than they can do, and do more than they can consume, and the limit of consuming is the body alone; whatever people produce, they are unable to consume that much (KD, p. 114). A person who understands this produces more and consumes less, with little waste (MVD, p. 282).

          The quantities involved can therefore be assessed. For a village of a hundred families, the quantity and quality of food, shelter, and adornment, and the need for medicine, become definite, and on that basis production can be distributed among the families (AA, p. 26). The body’s balance is not an ideal imposed from outside. It is the ordinary condition of a living body whose requirements are met.

          1.3 What an account must explain

          If the body’s needs are definite and the rest of nature is balanced, the imbalance of human economies requires an explanation that goes deeper than market rules or institutional design. An adequate account must resolve six connected problems: (1) why human want becomes insatiable when bodily need is finite, and why moral appeals to simplicity fail to hold it in check; (2) how a society can reach uncoerced, universal agreement on need, value, and justice when bodily tastes and self-interest differ in every individual; (3) why people would work wholeheartedly and share technical inventions openly without the goad of poverty, wage competition, or patent monopolies; (4) how household sharing and unpaid societal services such as education, health care, and self-governance can be sustained beyond biological kin without commercialising them or extracting coercive taxes; (5) why exchange and credit clearing would settle at equal labour value rather than degenerating into hoarding, artificial scarcity, and bargaining; and (6) why living generations would keep production within natural regeneration rather than discounting the distant future. Existing economic theories treat these as separate institutional puzzles and cannot solve them together because they leave out the conscious unit () and its awakening. The darshan’s account of the joint form of jeevan and body supplies the common ground on which all six are resolved, so the study begins there.

          2. The joint form and the source of insatiability

          2.1 Jeevan seeks continuity of happiness

          Every human being carries out all activities in the hope and expectation of happiness, peace, contentment, and bliss. This expectation is the human’s innateness (dharana), present in every person, and whatever one recognises through the senses is recognised from the expectation of happiness at its root (AA, p. 13). Contentment is accepted by every human being and discontent by none (AA, p. 48).

          The darshan locates this expectation in , and its ontology of jeevan is the starting point that separates Arthshastra from both materialist economics and ascetic ethics. The human being is the joint form of jeevan and body: the body is a temporary physical-chemical formation, whereas jeevan is a constitutionally complete atom (gathan-poorna parmanu, sentient unit) that exists before the body’s journey, operates the body through the brain and cognitive and work organs during life, and remains after the body’s journey (AA, pp. 16, 47–49, 51). Crucially, every human being across every race, class, sect, language, and country is jeevan, endowed with the same constitutional formation, inexhaustible (akshay) power and strength, and capacity for awakening through realisation in coexistence (AA, p. 47). Jeevan has ten activities, five as powers (hope, thought, desire, resoluteness, and evidence, communicated in projection as selection, analysis, visualisation, resolve, and authenticity) and five as strengths (tasting, deliberation, contemplation, enlightenment, and realisation in reflection), borne by its five faculties: , with selection and taste; , with analysis and deliberation; , with visualisation and contemplation; , with resolve and enlightenment; and atma, with authenticity and realisation (AA, p. 50; JV, pp. 72–73). (Here mun names the specific faculty of selection and taste, whereas in the recurring triad “body, mind, and wealth” the word “mind” refers to the jeevan powers as a whole; AA, pp. 59, 62.) Unlike physical or metabolic energy, which is used up as it is spent, the powers of jeevan remain surging (urmit) and continuously risen (uditodit) however much they are projected; jeevan never sleeps and remains perpetually at work (AA, pp. 50–51, 58, 60). The four jeevan values name harmony between adjacent faculties: happiness is harmony between mun and vritti, peace between vritti and chitta, contentment between chitta and buddhi, and bliss between buddhi and atma (AA, pp. 20–21, 92; JV, p. 137). Happiness in this sense is not a fleeting neural sensation; it is the continuous state of the whole conscious unit when all ten of its activities are awakened.

          Deliberation (tulan, in vritti) is the pivot on which this harmony—and the possibility of economic agreement—turns. Deliberation in terms of pleasure, health, and profit (priya, hit, labh) is relative respectively to the senses, the body, and objects (AA, pp. 58–59). Because these perspectives are rooted in the body and no two bodies or sensory histories are the same, deliberation by priya, hit, and labh can never reach universal agreement (sarvasammati) between even two human beings (JV, pp. 72–73; AA, pp. 58–60). In delusion, justice, , and truth (, dharma, ) are themselves interpreted through the lens of pleasure, health, and profit, so there is no universal agreement on them either, and the method of relativity keeps widening the distances of big and small, more and less (AA, pp. 18, 59–60). This structural impossibility of agreement under body-rooted evaluation is the fundamental reason human economies fail to reach balance. In awakening, jeevan is established in the seer status through realisation () in coexistence—realising the coexistence of all-pervasive omnipresence (, vyapak) and the whole of nature submerged in it at once—which is the eternal present and truth unhindered in all three times (trikalabadh satya, AA, p. 51; MVD, p. 203). Because every human being is jeevan with the capacity to realise this same undivided reality of satta and nature as a whole, the evaluation perspectives of justice, dharma, and truth grounded in realisation yield universal agreement in one and the same form for all humans (AA, pp. 19–20, 47, 51, 58–60; MVD, p. 203). That invariant reference point is the basis of uncoerced balance in awakened human living and in cyclical economics (AA, pp. 47, 51).

          2.2 Four and a half activities and unregulated taste

          In delusion, only four and a half of the ten activities are effective: selection, taste, analysis, visualisation, and half of deliberation. Taste and selection operate by what is pleasant to the senses; deliberation is confined to the perspectives of pleasure, health, and profit; and the person lives identified with bodily sensation, mistaking the body itself for jeevan (JV, pp. 72–73; AA, pp. 51, 60). The remaining five and a half activities—deliberation by justice, contemplation, resolve, enlightenment, authenticity, and realisation—lack the substance of understanding, and the result is continuous internal conflict and emptiness within jeevan (JV, pp. 72–73; AA, p. 59).

          The treatise draws a consequence from this that bears directly on economics. Selection and tasting form a cycle of their own: after selecting comes tasting, and after tasting the activity of selection arises again. However often the cycle runs, the arising of selection and tasting remains as it was, ready to be done again, because the powers of jeevan are inexhaustible and continuously surging (AA, pp. 17–18, 50–51). When all ten activities are compressed into four and a half, the inexhaustible energy of jeevan is forced through the narrow aperture of the five sensory organs and comparative calculation. Deliberation in terms of pleasure, health, and profit has been pushed to decisive conclusions in human tradition, while deliberation in terms of justice, dharma, and truth has remained under review, its decisive point, form, motion, and purpose not yet settled (AA, p. 18).

          This also explains why neither materialist restraint nor traditional asceticism and meditation has been able to halt the spread of an accumulation-obsessed economy. Because jeevan’s powers are constitutionally inexhaustible, they cannot be switched off or suppressed by moral exhortation to want less; nor can inner contentment be produced by alternating between outward-looking materialist accumulation and inward-looking postures, meditation practices (sadhana), or sermons on devotion and renunciation, which historically drifted back toward objects and accepted social orders ruled by fear and temptation (AA, pp. 46–47, 61). Inner contentment comes about only in the form of awakening—becoming evidenced in knowledge of jeevan, knowledge of coexistence, and knowledge of humane conduct (AA, pp. 46–47). The awakened alternative is therefore not the suppression of jeevan’s energy or of taste, but the opening of the higher five and a half activities so that jeevan’s inexhaustible activity is fulfilled in understanding, relationship, and benevolence, while taste is regulated from above. In awakened jeevan, every tasting is balanced and regulated on the touchstone of deliberation, every deliberation on the touchstone of contemplation, all contemplation on the touchstone of enlightenment, all enlightenment on the touchstone of realisation, and all realisation on the touchstone of coexistence. This chain is the darshan’s formula of self-regulation and balance in every individual (AA, p. 95). Insatiability, on this account, is what selection and tasting become when that chain is missing: an inexhaustible conscious power demanding continuous fulfilment through a finite bodily channel with no higher activity to give it measure.

          2.3 Transience as a signal, misread as lack

          Decisions based on sensitivity have temporary validity. Eating feels good, but no one can keep eating; sleeping feels good, but no one can keep sleeping; no sensory activity can be maintained without change. The darshan reads this not as a defect but as a provision:

          “The transient nature of sensitivity is such a wonderful provision in humans, serving as an alarm, an inspiration, and a direction for their awakening.” — JV, p. 163

          Whatever process of indulgence a person carries out through the five cognitive organs cannot have continuity, and so discontent with it inevitably remains in the activity of reflection (pratyavartan) that follows (AA, p. 48). Temptation towards accumulation and indulgence brings relief for a moment, and in the moments after, fear and suspicion pervade more than relief (AA, p. 33). Pleasure, health, and profit are relative respectively to the senses, the body, and objects; they are the sources of dissatisfaction, whereas justice, dharma, and truth are the sources of satisfaction and its continuity (AA, pp. 58–59). In the same way, all human effort looks toward the restfulness of labour (shram ka vishram), which the darshan identifies not with the cessation of work or with sensory relief, but with intellectual resolution (), happiness, and awakening; seeking that restfulness through materialistic indulgence and accumulation proves unsuccessful and leaves the person in effortful strain (KD, pp. 1, 12; AA, p. 23; MVD, pp. 96, 143).

          These passages together give the darshan’s diagnosis of insatiability. The deluded person experiences the discontent that follows every sensory satisfaction and the internal conflict of faculties without content. Lacking understanding of jeevan, the person reads both as a shortage of objects and seeks more of what has just failed to satisfy. The satiation point then moves ahead like a mirage (AA, p. 78). The fulfilment of duties is possible because duties are definite and limited, while the fulfilment of needs pursued as indulgence is impossible because such needs are indefinite and unlimited (MVD, p. 128).

          The texts also name the lesson that turns this error into economic doctrine. People stray when they are taught that necessities are infinite and means limited, so that necessity can be met only through struggle. That lesson directs attention to accumulation and comfort, neither of which has a point of satisfaction, and leads to the assumption that necessity itself is infinite. Release from it needs clear evidence that each family’s necessity is limited, that the possibility of fulfilling it is greater, that the powers of jeevan are inexhaustible, and that the body’s necessity is limited (KD, p. 115). Profit, which has no point of satisfaction, has no definite measure, and so it cannot be universal (SB, p. 234).

          Insatiability is thus not a fact about bodily need. It is a category error: a continuous, qualitative need of jeevan pursued through transient, quantitative means. The construction of this synthesis from several connected passages is recorded in the Editorial Notes.

          2.4 Accumulation, symbolic value, and artificial scarcity

          The treatise traces how human provision turned from sharing and barter into profit-obsession. As early communities moved through the forest, stone, and metal ages, they recognised natural materials as resources, shared goods within the family, and bartered between communities to meet real needs (AA, pp. 1–3). Out of fear of natural forces and of one another, people surrendered to the seat of state (rajyapith) for protection in the present life and to the seat of religion (dharmapith) for security in the next (AA, pp. 3–4). When thrones imposed taxes on exchange to fill royal treasuries, traders began adding a margin beyond the tax, initiating what the treatise calls the first stage of profit-obsession (AA, p. 4). Over time, the seat of the market and the seat of leadership displaced royal and religious treasuries as the primary centres of accumulation (AA, pp. 83, 90–91).

          Profit has always meant giving less and getting more, and prevailing economics has fixed no limit to how much less and how much more (AA, p. 5). The treatise applies this critique to both competing economic systems of the modern era, which it calls unrestrained (aniyantrit, market or capitalist) and regulated (niyantrit, state-controlled or socialist) economics. Neither has determined a fixed point or limit (dhruv) for how much profit or how much accumulation is enough; both evaluate by profit and assume a zero-sum world in which for one unit to gain, another must suffer loss (AA, p. 5). The darshan defines profit against labour: it is seeking more worth than the labour performed, obtaining a higher value in exchange for a lower one (MVD, pp. 57, 66). Profit keeps sinking, through exploitation, into accumulation, indulgence, over-indulgence, and multi-indulgence (AA, p. 7). The treatise concludes that no individual can become prosperous by the method of trade, because accumulation, comfort, and profit have no satiation point; nor can society be formed, contradictions among communities removed, or universal good brought about by that method (AA, pp. 7–8).

          Accumulation settles on money because money escapes the constraint that governs real goods. Metal became the symbol of objects and paper the symbol of metal, so that economics became the study of a symbol, its accumulating, and objects valued through it (AA, pp. 5–6, 52). Although gold has minimal practical usefulness in daily life, reserves of it are locked in central banks and the International Monetary Fund, while paper currency is also backed by the national stock of every commodity and by central-bank bonds (AA, pp. 44–45). The treatise identifies three allurements that gave paper currency its hold over human society: hundreds of thousands in currency can be carried in a pocket, millions or billions can be stored in a small box, and once all objects and human labour are subjected to currency, accumulating currency becomes the principal basis for recognising a person’s social standing (AA, p. 45). However much paper currency or metal reserve exists, it cannot replace grain for one person or quench one person’s thirst (AA, p. 6; JV, pp. 151–152). The accumulation is only of money, which is symbolic value (MVD, p. 182). Changes in price over decades are changes in the symbol alone; rice, gold, and cement carry the same usefulness as before (AA, pp. 52–53). The practice of collecting interest and the share market multiply claims in symbolic currency further still (AA, p. 52).

          Subjecting society to currency gave rise to what the treatise classifies as seven kinds of trade: five forms of trade in persons (trading in strength by hiring the strong, in learning by hiring the educated, in knowledge by selling understanding, which the treatise calls the most wicked trade, in position or office, and in beauty), together with trade in produced objects and trade in industrial technology, which buys salaried scholars and technicians to secure monopolies (AA, pp. 45–46). As the centres of accumulation shifted from royal and religious treasuries to middlemen and traders, the highest accumulators in the second half of the twentieth century came to include film and stage performers and figures of commercialised beauty alongside merchants and financiers (AA, pp. 90–91).

          Two further consequences follow. Artificial scarcity becomes a principal technique for gaining profit: where currency is concentrated, objects are held back from the motion by which they would become accessible to all (AA, pp. 5–6, 52). And because accumulation has no satiation point, it cannot be accessible to all, since the earth is limited (AA, p. 52). Those who remain engaged in production are unable to accumulate, while those who accumulate become slack in production, and the producer of objects has generally remained poor (AA, pp. 7, 75). The widening distance between producer and consumer, filled by profit, is one of the two discords the treatise finds in present production; pollution is the other (AA, p. 106).

          The darshan names the error in terms of evaluation. Delusion means taking a thing to be more than, less than, or other than what it is (AA, p. 17). Over-evaluation of symbolic currency, under-evaluation of usefulness value, mis-evaluation of the human being as a kind of animal, and under-evaluation of naturalness, the forests, minerals, and earth on which all provision depends, are its economic forms (AA, p. 53).

          2.5 The consequence in the earth

          The same evaluation produces the ecological crisis. Here the treatise draws a sharp line between production and extraction. Humans do not produce mineral oil, coal, or ores; nature placed them in the strata of the earth before human beings existed, as part of the planet’s own balance. Tearing open the earth to remove them is extraction and seizure, not production, and is defended only by the circular excuse of preserving the livelihoods tied to that extraction (AA, p. 78). Coal and mineral oil needed to remain in the earth for its balance; extracting and burning them produced pollution, rising heat, a damaged protective layer in the upper atmosphere, and rising seas (AA, pp. 30–31). Chemical fertilisers increased quantity while the concentration of nutrition in grain declined and soils were driven towards excess acidity or alkalinity (AA, p. 70). Breeding produced larger grains and flowers whose basic nutritive content stayed the same (AA, pp. 53, 55–56). Mechanised and input-dependent agriculture replaced autonomy with dependence in seed, manure, ploughing, and threshing (AA, p. 71). Pollution policy, in the treatise’s assessment, does not ask how to stop pollution but how much of it people can live in (AA, p. 74).

          The whole form of exploitation is the exploitation of the earth, and the human being is both exploited and exploiter (AA, p. 82). The only production the treatise names as opposed in itself to a coexistential economy is the manufacture of war materials; machine production as such is not contrary to economics (AA, p. 47). The objection is to the purpose and the evaluation that direct production, not to technique.

          3. Cyclical economics

          3.1 Resources as body, mind, and wealth

          Cyclical economics is the right use, security, and prosperity of resources (artha) in the form of body, mind, and wealth (AA, p. 25). The mind is an inseparable activity of . When hope, thought, desire, and resolve act by a realisation-based method, the mind works through the body, applying labour with skill and proficiency to natural abundance. In this process, the powers of jeevan project outward (paravartan) through the cognitive and work organs as dexterity (hastalaghav) to establish usefulness value and aesthetic value in natural materials, and reflect inward (pratyavartan) as the growth and strengthening (pushti) of jeevan’s own faculties, yielding resolution and prosperity as a continuous source of contentment (AA, pp. 18–19, 48–50). Because jeevan never sleeps and its powers are inexhaustible (akshay), labour in an awakened person is not a disutility endured only for an external wage or profit margin; it is the natural expression of jeevan’s own activity, finding its restfulness (shram ka vishram) in intellectual resolution () (AA, pp. 23, 50–51, 58, 60; KD, pp. 1, 12). The objects produced carry usefulness value and aesthetic value, and these objects are wealth (dhan); wealth is only the produced object, not money (AA, pp. 25, 105). The right use of the body is evidenced when the mind uses it consistently with realisation, enlightenment, contemplation, and deliberation (AA, p. 25).

          This changes what counts as capital. All production is the outcome of human labour applied to natural abundance, so the primary wealth is the labour power inherent in human beings, and this primary capital is always the human being’s own (AA, p. 73). The means of a human being are only body, mind, and wealth (MVD, p. 243). Earning wealth is the definite measure of usefulness and aesthetic value realised from natural abundance through the planned deployment of human labour (MVD, p. 56). Because the powers of jeevan are inexhaustible while the body’s capacity to consume is finite, every human being has the capacity to produce more than immediate necessity, and this asymmetry between inexhaustible jeevan power and finite bodily intake is the foundational principle of human prosperity (SB, p. 268; KD, p. 114; AA, p. 114). Public dialogue on the economy can therefore begin from the recognition that human labour is the primary capital (JVD, p. 215). Economics that treats money as the primary resource has studied the symbol rather than the thing (JV, pp. 151–152). Resources must include body, mind, and wealth together; economics cannot exist if any one of these is left out (JV, p. 151).

          3.2 What cyclicality means

          The treatise gives cyclicality a wide sense before it gives it an economic one. Complementarity itself is the meaning of cyclicality, and cyclicality is mutual sustenance: particles sustain one another as atoms, atoms as molecules, molecules as formations, and the orders of nature as one another’s support (AA, p. 42). It can be seen at many scales. Seed becomes tree and tree becomes seed; bodies form according to species and species traditions continue through bodies; knowing and believing ground recognising and fulfilling, and these return to knowing and believing; relationship grounds value, value grounds evaluation, and evaluation returns to relationship. The individual’s complementarity is linked into the family and the family’s into the individual, and the same holds between family and village. Production through the utilisation of labour, the valuation of products by the value of labour, and exchange on that basis together form a cycle, and so do the four orders, each complementary to the others (AA, p. 15). Water evaporates, gathers as cloud, falls as rain, and runs back to the sea; plants decompose and make the earth fertile for further plants (AA, pp. 34–35; MVD, p. 152).

          The common structure is a movement that starts from any point, passes through complementary activity towards the fulfilment of a purpose, and returns to its starting point with that purpose fulfilled, so that the movement can continue (AA, p. 15). The treatise’s own definition of this structure is a compressed sentence whose Hindi admits more than one parsing; the reading adopted here is recorded in the Editorial Notes. Two features of it matter for economics. A cycle returns: whatever is drawn out is restored to the conditions of the next round. And a cycle is purposive: it is linked in the sense of prosperity, so its return is the fulfilment of a goal, not mere repetition.

          3.3 The provision cycle

          Applied to provision, the cycle has a definite sequence. A need for production arises; a mentality fitted to that need, endowed with skill, proficiency, and scholarliness, forms; through a healthy body, labour establishes usefulness value and aesthetic value in objects drawn from natural abundance; the objects are rightly used according to their usefulness; the need is supplied; and the remainder is exchanged (AA, p. 106). The Jeevan Vidya lectures state the same cycle as a renewal of capacity:

          “We deploy our resources in the form of body, mind, and wealth on natural abundance for production, and the produced goods become the basis of nourishment and protection of our body and social participation. We again become capable of producing on the basis of this social participation, nourishment, and protection. In this way, it is cyclical.” — JV, p. 154

          The cycle runs through the joint form itself. The mind operates the body; mind and body together manifest object values; and object values are useful to the body and satisfying to jeevan (AA, p. 26). Earth, air, water, forest, and minerals are the sites where labour is utilised (AA, p. 26). Tools and other means made by one generation serve the next, so that means accumulate across generations in a form that remains in use: each generation can offer the next means in excess of need, the means of production increase, and the time required decreases (AA, pp. 106, 118). The cycle grows richer without growing larger in consumption.

          3.4 Need, the family, and prosperity

          The cycle closes only where need is definite, and the darshan locates that definiteness in the family. A family is a limited number of autonomous people, at least ten, living together with justice: recognising their relationships, fulfilling the values in them, evaluating, and reaching mutual satisfaction, and complementary to one another in the family’s production (AA, pp. 14, 26, 65, 72). The treatise states that the cyclical economy becomes meaningful only after balance is achieved in human relationships: where relationships are balanced, objects are used in the service of those relationships, free of profit and loss (AA, p. 21). In an unawakened setting, unconditional sharing according to need is ordinarily confined to biological nuclear kin because agents who identify jeevan with the body treat non-kin as competitors for scarce goods; in the darshan, by contrast, every member is jeevan, endowed with the ability to realise () in the coexistence of all-pervasive omnipresence (, vyapak) and nature as a whole at once (AA, pp. 47, 51, 60; MVD, p. 203), and awakening to that reality removes bodily identification (AA, pp. 48, 60) and forms the basis on which a ten-member household of autonomous family humans (parivar manav, AA, pp. 48–49, 104–105) shares according to need without individual labour accounting. Needs are ascertained at the level of the family, not the individual (JV, p. 153; AA, p. 9), and they are assessed by the family itself rather than rationed from above. This definiteness is not austere uniformity: it encompasses both common aspirations (food, shelter, adornment, health, and education) and special aspirations (telephony, broadcasting, and transport), together with aesthetic value and regulated taste (AA, pp. 95, 112). In a humane family, needs become limited because use, right use, and purposeful use become definite, and production in excess of need is therefore possible in every family (AA, p. 26). Production beyond necessity is already evident before awakening, since relatively few people produce what many more use; in a wise family, two to four members may produce beyond the family’s whole necessity (KD, p. 115). For a person who understands jeevan, all needs are useful only in the nourishment and protection of the body and in social participation, so needs appear limited, while the labour power for meeting them, being the inexhaustible power of jeevan, gives confidence in production in excess of need (AA, p. 114).

          Prosperity follows directly. The lack of the sentiment of lacking is prosperity, and production in excess of needs is prosperity (MVD, p. 71). Its basis is the sum of objects produced in excess of need, together with their right use and security (AA, p. 113). Prosperity cannot be private. For one family to be prosperous, more than one family must be; both the imagining of prosperity alone and the imagining of prosperity through accumulation prove to be delusion (AA, pp. 62–63). The person who can sustain this is the autonomous human: trusting in oneself, respecting excellence, balanced in talent and personality, social in behaviour, and self-reliant in occupation (AA, pp. 65, 71–72). The texts give two reasons why sharing according to need within the family need not invite shirking. First, the household is a small community of ten people who see one another’s work and complement one another across days of greater and lesser contribution (AA, pp. 14, 119–120; KD, pp. 12–13), and for an awakened person the restfulness of labour (shram ka vishram) lies in resolution (samadhan) and the fulfilment of relationship rather than in withdrawing from work (AA, pp. 21, 23; KD, p. 1; MVD, pp. 96, 218). Second, social honour and eligibility to represent the family in the self-governing councils depend on living with resolution and producing beyond need (AA, pp. 125–126; MVD, p. 265). On this account, both the anonymity and the view of work as a mere burden that let effort slip in large wage or welfare systems are absent (AA, pp. 75–76).

          3.5 Use, right use, and purposeful use

          Use, right use, and purposeful use name three levels at which resources serve human purposes. Usefulness is the utilisation of body, mind, and wealth in the nourishment and protection of the body and in social participation. Right use is their utilisation in orderliness and in the motion of undivided society. Purposeful use is their utilisation in evidencing, from generation to generation, knowledge of coexistence, of jeevan, and of humane conduct (AA, pp. 72, 94). The darshan’s glossary states the same levels in terms of participation: use is the deployment of means for production and service, right use is their utilisation within the family-based self-governing order, and purposeful use is their utilisation, upon awakening, towards undivided society and universal orderliness (MVD, p. 56). Body, mind, and wealth are indivisible in all three, because no object can be used without body and mind (AA, p. 94). The treatise pairs usefulness with justice, right use with resolution, and purposeful use with authenticity (AA, p. 116). In terms of scale, production is used within the family, rightly used in society, and put to purpose in the universal order (KD, p. 116). Among the human goals, prosperity is realised through the right use and security of object values, fearlessness through established and civic values, and resolution through human and jeevan values (AA, p. 68).

          Values are ordered by what they serve across a thirty-value hierarchy (Axiology: Value Theory §1.6). In offering and surrender, the two object values (usefulness value and aesthetic value) are dedicated to the nine civic or expressed values, the nine civic values to the nine established values of relationship (such as trust, respect, and affection), the nine established values to the six human values (steadfastness, courage, generosity, kindness, grace, and compassion), and the six human values to the four jeevan values, which are happiness, peace, contentment, and bliss (AA, pp. 20–24, 92). The right use of resources is itself cyclicality, and the earth’s endowment is rightly used by the method of balance (AA, p. 91). The pursuit of wealth is ranked in seven steps against the classical aims of life: wealth placed in the service of liberation, , or the fulfilment of desire is ranked high, those three purposes placed in the service of wealth are ranked low, and wealth sought for its own sake falls in between (KD, p. 4). Non-accumulation means earning for expenditure, and its righteous expenditure is mandatory (MVD, p. 150). Those who take no part in production lack awakening regarding use and right use (AA, p. 75).

          3.6 Value, labour, and exchange

          Value in is not price. The value of a unit is its essentiality, its participation in the orderliness of the whole: what its activity fulfils in relation to others. For produced things this participation is usefulness, what food, shelter, a tool, or a vehicle fulfils in nourishing and protecting the body and in social participation, and aesthetic value adds convenience to usefulness. As §3.5 showed, usefulness serves need, and need serves relationships, humane character, and finally the harmonies of jeevan (Axiology: Value Theory §§1.1, 1.5–1.6; AA, p. 92). Human labour is the activity that establishes usefulness and beauty upon natural abundance, and the primary wealth of any economy is this labour power, which is always the human being’s own (AA, p. 73). To give labour its true value is to value it by the usefulness it establishes in this larger sense, not by the currency it can command.

          Production is the usefulness value and aesthetic value established through labour on natural abundance, in the sense of prosperity (AA, p. 68). It is also described as the human establishment of usefulness and beauty upon nature other than human, and as a qualitative change in body, mind, and natural abundance in the direction of usefulness (AA, p. 116). The value of an object is determined in a chain: labour-based object, object-based usefulness, usefulness-based valuation (AA, p. 49). Evaluation can be made only of labour, not of symbolic objects (AA, p. 112).

          Labour in the darshan is not physical exertion alone; it is the joint deployment of mind (manas, the powers of jeevan) and body together with means upon natural abundance, requiring skill (kushalata), a mind full of proficiency (nipunata), and scholarliness (panditya) (AA, pp. 26, 59, 62, 118–119). The treatise proposes a practical method of valuing this labour. Wheat sown in a village under various circumstances yields various quantities; the midpoint of those quantities gives a common value for the labour of producing wheat. Once one object of common aspiration has been valued in this way, every product of the village can be valued similarly, and the valuation can be consolidated within the village (AA, p. 66). Each person’s pace of work (karya-gati) in an activity, in ploughing, breaking stone, building a house, weaving, or making machines, differs by activity and is the basis of measurement. A product of measured quantity and quality made in a definite time, through a definite technical mentality and dexterity, counts as one unit of labour and can be exchanged for labour equal to it (AA, p. 119). The production and exchange-reserve committees jointly determine the quantity, quality, and labour values of village products, and the exchange committee evaluates labour through the fivefold combination of skill, proficiency, speed of work, time, and means, remaining alert so that neither unnecessary profit nor unbearable loss occurs (AA, pp. 143, 145). Valuing labour by this fivefold combination measures the useful output established with competent skill and tools, rather than rewarding slowness or counting raw hours of physical toil. Because tools and means accumulate across generations, the labour required per unit of output decreases over time, and the unit value of labour in objects is revised downward accordingly (AA, pp. 118, 145).

          This valuation establishes a satiation point within exchange itself (vinimay ka tripti bindu). Production is the remuneration of labour, and after exchange the remuneration remains just as much as was produced. This is the satiation point of exchange: in the utilisation of labour there is neither profit nor loss, only more or less production as a matter of work practice (AA, p. 76). Here again the mechanism depends on awakening. In any exchange or credit-clearing system where participants still seek satisfaction in labh (giving less and getting more), producers have a standing incentive to withhold scarce goods, inflate reported effort, or demand shadow premiums. For an awakened producer whose sovereignty of satisfaction (samprabhuta) is already assured through objects of use, household prosperity, and mutual satisfaction (ubhay-tripti) in justice (AA, pp. 50–51, 54, 62–63, 76), exchange is simply the complementary transfer of useful goods between prosperous families, so equal labour for equal labour is experienced as complete fulfilment rather than a missed margin. The treatise extends the point to existence as a whole. Neither development in the atom, nor the transition to jeevan, nor the formation and decomposition of physical and chemical substances shows any sign of profit and loss; what is seen throughout is coexistence and prosperity (AA, pp. 77–78).

          Differences of skill do not have to be weighed inside the family. The treatise asks explicitly whether dexterity of hand, skill, proficiency, and scholarliness can be the same in every person, and answers that within a family of ten members some days one person is more complementary in production and some days less; the family’s goal is the product, not more or less individual labour (AA, pp. 119–120). Valuation by labour is needed between families and villages, where exchange takes place. Usefulness value itself is constant: a kilogram of sesame has always had the same usefulness, whatever its monetary valuation (JV, pp. 136–137).

          3.7 Righteous wealth, generosity, and care

          The ethics of the economy are stated in terms of character. Humane character consists of righteous wealth, marital faithfulness, and kindness in work and behaviour (AA, p. 54). Righteous wealth (svadhan) is wealth received as the remuneration of one’s own labour, as a gift, or as an award (puraskar) for a special display of art, technique, or scholarship; whoever holds it, it is the result of someone’s labour on natural abundance (AA, p. 140; AVD, p. 170). Its opposite, ill-gotten wealth, is wealth obtained through exploitation and hypocrisy, and the darshan counts it, with adultery and hurting others, as crime (MVD, pp. 59, 263). Moral responsibility across economic life extends to three modes of participation across body, speech, and mind: doing an act oneself (krit), commissioning another to do it (karit), and consenting to or endorsing it (anumodit) (KD, pp. 1–2, 117; SB, pp. 207, 258). Financing or knowingly profiting from exploitation or ecological harm therefore carries the same accountability as direct action. The same distinction separates lawful from unlawful use of nature. Spending minerals and forests in proportion to their regeneration and maintaining circularity in natural abundance are lawful; spending beyond regeneration and exploiting non-renewable materials are not (MVD, p. 263).

          The treatise connects these economic orientations to five kinds of human living within the knowledge order (AA, p. 101). Before awakening, the animalistic human (pashu manav) lives in narrow selfishness and indulgence, and the demonic human (rakshas manav) in cruel selfishness and over-indulgence. Upon awakening, the humane human (manav) evidences resolution and prosperity by producing in excess of need and practising right use; the deific human (dev manav) evidences benevolence by dedicating body, mind, and wealth to the awakening and orderliness of others; and the divine human (divya manav) evidences compassion in unbroken universality (AA, pp. 23–24, 101). Awakening replaces interest-based () consumption, driven by fluctuating sensory preferences, with value-based () and goal-based (lakshyamulak) living, in which education fosters more production and less consumption (AA, pp. 101–102, 132).

          The darshan does not ask people to renounce wealth. While animals are bound by four instincts (food, sleep, fear, and reproduction), humans act with three motives (): the desire for progeny (putreshana), for wealth (vitteshana), and for reputation or public welfare (lokeshana) (MVD, p. 318). In an awakened person attraction takes the form of pursuing prosperity with righteous motives (MVD, pp. 59, 137). Among the three, the motive for public welfare is the highest, because through it a person extends participation from the family to undivided society and universal orderliness (MVD, p. 320). People, wealth, and reputation are assets of the family and of the undivided society rather than of the individual (MVD, p. 221), and wealth finds its success and satisfaction through generosity (MVD, pp. 211, 254). Generosity is the happy offering of body, mind, and wealth, according to need, for the awakening, bodily health, and prosperity of others, and the treatise reads every kind of cyclicality as a sign of it (MVD, p. 339; AA, p. 22).

          Within the family, goods are shared and allotted; between families and communities they are exchanged (AA, p. 1). Those who cannot produce are provided for through kindness, which includes making an object available where there is receptivity for it but no object. The works of kindness the treatise lists include helping people who are helpless, in distress, afflicted by disease, or struck by natural calamity (AA, pp. 23–24, 140). Guardians who feed their child first and eat what is left already show the human tendency to let others live and then live oneself (AA, p. 102), and parents nurture and protect their children and make them capable of production (MVD, p. 340). In an unawakened economy, production beyond immediate bodily consumption is diverted into accumulation (sangrah), which has no satiation point. In an awakened ten-member family, where two to four members already produce more than the household’s limited physical needs, once the family is endowed with resolution and prosperity, the question of where its further production should go is answered not by accumulation, but by the natural arising of the propensity of benevolence (upkar) (KD, pp. 115–116). Benevolence is defined as help and inspiration for awakening and prosperity (MVD, p. 72), and the treatise identifies the tendency to benevolence (upkar-pravritti) as the defining pursuit across all three awakened planes (manav, dev manav, and divya manav), reaching its fullest expression in the deific and divine planes (AA, pp. 23–24, 101). Out of this household abundance, surplus is channelled into use (upyog) within the family, right use (sadupyog) in society, and purposeful use (prayojansheelta) in the universal order rather than hoarded (KD, pp. 116–117; AA, p. 116; MVD, p. 150). That same principle explains why the higher societal services that sustain awakening and orderliness, above all education (shiksha-), health care (swasthya-), justice, and self-governance, must be performed with benevolence rather than turned into commercial trades (vyapar): selling learning, knowledge, or medical care for money and accumulation distorts their purpose (AA, pp. 45–46; JV, pp. 166–168), whereas abundance in the family frees individuals to offer these services as benevolence and kindness (AA, pp. 24, 29, 140; KD, pp. 116–117).

          3.8 Services, benevolence, and remuneration

          How are services (seva) organised across the village? The texts treat agriculture and industry as the two primary forms of production and classify all other vocations as dependent on or complementary to them (MVD, p. 267). Within that framework, they distinguish two forms of service: benevolence-focused service (upkar-pradhan seva), in which satisfaction lies in performing acts of benevolence without material return, and remuneration-focused service (pratiphal-pradhan seva), performed for remuneration (parishramik), which counts as righteous wealth (svadhan) (MVD, pp. 72, 263; AA, p. 140).

          Which vocations fall under each kind of service? Benevolence-focused service encompasses household care, child and elder support, teaching and scholarly guidance under the education-sanskar committee (AA, pp. 24, 29, 130–132, 140), medical care and healing by doctors and health practitioners under the health-restraint committee at village and regional medical centres (AA, pp. 139–140; JV, pp. 166–168), part-time duties on the five committees (AA, p. 127), and full-time representation in the ten-tier council order (lok-pratinidhi, KD, pp. 116–117). Conversely, remuneration-focused service covers the ninth branch of the village production system and exchange-reserve administration: repairing bicycles, radios, televisions, and agricultural or industrial machines, washing clothes, barbering, and staffing the exchange-reserve (AA, pp. 133, 136, 138). Healing the body falls on the unpaid side of this line because restoring a person in distress or disease is a direct work of kindness (daya) and generosity in human relationship that commercial fees corrupt into a trade in suffering (AA, pp. 22–24, 45–46, 140; JV, pp. 166–168; MVD, p. 339), whereas repair, laundry, barbering, and reserve staffing maintain produced objects and village operations as an extension of material production (while the physical gathering and preparation of herbal medicines itself belongs to the forest-produce and cottage-industry branches of production, and school and clinic buildings are maintained as common village facilities, AA, pp. 129, 135–137, 139).

          How, then, do practitioners of each kind of service obtain the physical objects that fulfil their needs when they do not themselves bring a new material commodity to exchange? Those engaged in benevolence-focused service—every teacher, doctor, committee member, and council representative—belong to a self-reliant ten-member family (AA, pp. 127, 130) that produces in excess of its needs, and all objects needed for their bodily nourishment, protection, and societal motion remain provided by their prosperous household without salaried employment (KD, pp. 115–117; AA, pp. 119–120, 137). For those engaged in remuneration-focused service, the village family council fixes the remuneration for the work (AA, p. 136), and because every resident holds an account in the village’s autonomous cooperative exchange-reserve (AA, pp. 137–138), that remuneration credits their account so they can draw food, clothing, and other needed objects at labour value; since every villager is also trained in more than one occupation (AA, p. 131) and belongs to a ten-member family, remunerated upkeep complements household production rather than creating a landless service class.

          3.9 Production within the balance of nature

          The provision cycle is nested within the cycles of nature, and its production must keep them intact. Natural assets, minerals and vegetation, are to be used in proportion to their production, in the sense of balance; their production is not to be obstructed; and human beings are to become helpful in it. Without keeping nature prosperous, the human being cannot be prosperous (AA, pp. 142, 146; KD, p. 7). The state policy of the darshan names production security as maintained “through the principle of maintaining nature’s balance”, and economic security through producing more than the needs of the family by way of circularity (MVD, p. 264).

          The treatise is specific about energy. Mineral oil, coal, and radioactive fuels are the most harmful sources; cyclical sources, the force of flowing water, falling water, wind, solar heat, and gas from dung and refuse, are pollution-free (AA, pp. 31–32, 106–107; JV, pp. 115–116). Vegetable oil from roadside oil-bearing trees forms a closed cycle: tree, seed, oil, smoke, the smoke taken up as food by trees, and seed again (AA, p. 32). Agriculture is to keep animal husbandry, compost, and dung gas together, so that the village’s own manure restores fertility and soil degradation is halted (AA, pp. 134–135). The human-made environment must be planned with attention to the interventions made upon nature, the utilisations of labour, their returns, and their usefulness, right use, and purposeful use (AA, p. 114). Determining the land of each village and district also determines how many villages can live there with prosperity (AA, p. 92). Because a realised person sees the prosperity of oneself and one’s family in the prosperity of the whole—both undivided society and nature (AA, pp. 62–63, 142, 146; KD, p. 7)—and purposeful use (prayojansheelta) is defined as evidencing understanding from generation to generation in the perpetuity of human tradition (AA, pp. 72, 94; MVD, p. 56), keeping the earth’s regenerative balance intact for future centuries is not an external sacrifice imposed against present self-interest, but the direct expression of one’s own prosperity in the whole.

          3.10 Skill, knowledge, and dissemination

          Since labour is the primary capital, the formation of skill is central to the economy. Education is to make skill, proficiency, and scholarliness accessible to every person, so that each becomes competent in production (AA, p. 113). The darshan’s state policy calls for skills to be made commonly accessible together with understanding, for resources to be allotted to people with skill, and for research and development directed towards food, shelter, adornment, telecommunication, and transport (MVD, pp. 267–268). In the village, the education committee works with the production and exchange-reserve committees to decide who should be trained in which occupation, in view of the village’s present and future needs, and ensures that villagers become proficient in more than one occupation so that seasonal and technical shifts do not leave anyone idle (AA, p. 131). Teachers and scholars carry technical knowledge to ordinary people as a work of benevolence (AA, p. 29). Education that imparts only vocational knowledge serves material prosperity alone (MVD, p. 307); humane education adds the understanding that keeps production within its purposes.

          Knowledge is not to be enclosed. Farmers handed on their seeds and methods without concealment, and research and its dissemination are inseparable. Monopolies over seed and what modern law calls intellectual property rights obstruct that relation, turning discoveries meant for universal welfare into instruments of commercial exploitation; since the powers of jeevan cannot be deposited in any bank or reserve, intellectual reservation is meaningless (AA, pp. 56–58; JV, pp. 88–89). What one human being can understand, every human being can understand, and what one attains, all can attain (AA, p. 107). Trading in knowledge, like trading in human labour and in religion, is among the forms of profit the texts reject (AA, p. 45; AVD, p. 53).

          Rejecting patent monopolies does not mean leaving invention unrecognised or unmotivated. Under the treatise’s provisions for the security of production, whenever a person or group employs scholarliness (panditya) to evidence a special originality (maulikta) in production, such as an original tool, procedure, method, design, or medicinal or craft discovery not previously in the tradition, the justice-security committee, working with the production, health-restraint, and education committees, formally secures its drawings, formulas, and procedures in writing for the tradition and arranges prizes and awards (puraskar), which constitute righteous wealth (svadhan) (AA, pp. 140, 144). Four factors motivate technical invention in the absence of commercial monopolies. First, because household needs are definite, disseminating a labour-saving tool lowers the unit labour value of goods and directly reduces the working hours required from every family, including the inventor’s own (AA, pp. 118, 145). Second, once basic quantities are met, the attention of jeevan turns naturally towards quality and creative doership (kartritva) (AA, pp. 116, 118). Third, original contributions earn public honour (lokeshana, the highest of the three human motives) together with formal awards (MVD, pp. 318, 320; AA, pp. 140, 144). Fourth, sharing a discovery so that other families can produce with less strain directly fulfils benevolence (upkar) (AA, pp. 29, 101).

          3.11 Machines, automation, and the participation of every person

          Machine production is not contrary to economics (AA, p. 47). Technique multiplies the speed of whatever humans do. Until people can live as members of society and of an orderly system, that multiplication increases the speed of problems and the number of crimes; only with such understanding do machines become available to every family as prosperity (SB, p. 267). The treatise names two drives that have kept technique from serving this purpose: the preoccupation with making all production automatic, and the centralisation of industry. Both rest on capital investment and on buying labour power. Automatic production has reduced the number of people engaged in production, so that of two equally educated people one finds work and the other feels useless (AA, p. 107). A working mindset has given way to a managing mindset, one reason why no balance has yet been reached in the evaluation of remuneration for the objects of common and special aspiration (JVD, p. 35). Salaried work tends to slacken once employment is secure, and those most adept in production present themselves at home as entitled to comfort and rule (AA, pp. 75–76).

          Against this, every person is to be engaged in production according to capacity, receptivity, and ability (MVD, p. 265; AA, p. 127). When every member of every family takes part in production, poverty, deprivation, and the trap of accumulation and comfort are left behind together (JVD, p. 216). The exchange-reserve works with the education committee to replace the salaried mentality with a production mentality, and helps traders turn from hoarding to production (AA, p. 137). Large industry keeps its place at the higher tiers of the order, while most technology stays within small industry (AA, pp. 29–30, 115). Means rightly used let each generation pass on more means in excess of need, so that the means of production increase and the time required decreases (AA, p. 118). The darshan’s criterion for technique is therefore twofold: it should increase the usefulness that labour establishes and reduce the time it takes, and it should keep every person a participant in production rather than displacing people from it.

          3.12 The economy within the five dimensions of orderliness

          Economics in isolation has no definition or standing (AA, p. 117). Cyclical economics is one integral part of self-governance, alongside sociology, the science of the constitution, psychology, and the science of production (AA, pp. 63, 110). Self-governance means accessibility to justice, to production, and to exchange, sustained by education-sanskar and health-restraint (AA, p. 63). The treatise grounds this order in the progression from the autonomous human (swayatt manav) to the family human (parivar manav) and the orderliness human (vyavastha manav), through three inseparable qualities: authority (adhikar, the strength of understanding and realisation), identity (swatva, evidencing that strength in living), and independence (swatantrata, unconstrained participation in family and universal orderliness) (AA, pp. 104–105). It contrasts this family-based order with power-centric rule (whether monarchy, majority-mandate party democracy, or power-centric village councils, panchayats), which seeks to enforce order through fear and inducement, and with international relief that measures human rights in money rather than awakening (AA, pp. 82–84). The five dimensions of orderliness are depicted as a circle joined to a pentagon: education-sanskar to understanding, health-restraint to expression consistent with science and wisdom, justice-security to honesty, production to prosperity, and exchange-reserve to balance (AA, p. 112). How Undivided Society Is Established §5.4 describes how these dimensions constrain one another.

          The village of about a hundred families is the first unit of this order. Ten families form a family cluster, ten clusters a village council, and so on through ten tiers to a world family council, each council nominating five committees for the five dimensions (AA, pp. 27–28, 125–127). Because representatives at each tier are selected by consensus from the tier below rather than through adversarial campaigning, election expenditure drops to zero (AA, p. 110). Committee members serve on a part-time basis while continuing their own production, so governance does not create a non-producing political class (AA, p. 127). Production scale rises with the tier: cottage industry within the village, small industry at the village cluster, medium industry at the district, medium and large industry at the province, and large industry at the region, continent, and world, where the world council’s committees maintain balance across the whole (AA, pp. 29–30, 115). Within the village, the production committee coordinates nine branches of work: agriculture, goatery and dairy (cow-rearing), clothing, shelter construction, food processing, repair and maintenance, forest resources, clean energy, and local manufacture (AA, pp. 133–136). The exchange-reserve is staffed by at least three trained persons who maintain accounts of production, consumption, and exchange (AA, p. 138). Every village is to be autonomous in the objects of common aspiration, while a shortfall in a few items is met reciprocally within ten or a hundred villages and complex goods for special aspirations (transport, telecommunications, broadcasting, and heavy equipment) are produced by medium and large industries at the higher tiers (AA, pp. 29–30, 72, 115). Risk from crop failure or natural calamity, and upfront provisioning for shared facilities, are met without commercial insurance or interest-bearing capital markets through three buffers: family and village surpluses held in the exchange-reserve, reciprocal deficit coverage and works of kindness across the ten-village and hundred-village tiers (AA, pp. 23–24, 72, 140), and common-facility funds administered by the councils (AA, pp. 128, 137–138). Common aspirations are assessed through local survey and special aspirations through local acceptability (AA, p. 112). Justice is assessed in four dimensions: in character, in behaviour, in production, and in exchange (AA, pp. 140–143).

          3.13 The three policies and exchange between regions

          The darshan names three policies for the resources of body, mind, and wealth. Their protection is the concern of state policy (raj-neeti), their right use of moral policy (dharm-neeti), and production beyond the family’s needs of economic policy (arth-neeti) (KD, p. 67; MVD, pp. 233, 239). Moral policy is primary and permanent, whereas state policy is subordinate to it and transitions from protective regulation into self-governing orderliness as understanding spreads (KD, p. 14; MVD, p. 239). The darshan’s state policy sets out six securities: national security through coexistence, economic security through producing more than the family’s needs by way of circularity, production security through maintaining nature’s balance, exchange security through the utilisation and exchange of labour, education security through knowledge of coexistence, and ethical security through the right use and security of body, mind, and wealth (MVD, pp. 264, 271).

          These provisions bear directly on the value of human labour. Economic security calls for every person to be enabled to work productively according to capacity, receptivity, and ability, for respect and honour to be conferred on that basis, for the disparity between rich and poor to end through resolution and prosperity in every family, and for righteous wealth to be established through higher production and lower consumption. Production security names agriculture and industry as the two primary forms of production (with all other vocations dependent on or complementary to them), calls for a policy that prevents the exploitation of the fruits of labour, and measures the success of the production system by every family producing more than its needs and achieving prosperity (MVD, pp. 259, 265–268). Exchange security defines exchange as making produced goods available to consumers on the basis of the evaluation of human labour, free of profit and loss (MVD, pp. 265–268). Exchange between nations is likewise to be free of profit and loss and based on object value and labour value (MVD, p. 268). While karma-governed exchange is free of gain and loss, exchange is freed from class sentiment (varg-bhavna) only when grounded in the humane rule-trio of intellectual, social, and natural laws (KD, pp. 15, 35, 42). Under present arrangements, countries holding large foreign exchange reserves are able to exploit others (JVD, p. 32).

          3.14 The path of transition

          The treatise does not assume that the money economy disappears at once. Its plan for a village begins with a detailed survey: population, education, occupations, land, water, crops, livestock, crafts, shops, storage, health, and the state of justice in production and in exchange (AA, pp. 122–125). The village’s exchange is then conducted through an autonomous cooperative exchange-reserve working without profit and loss. Members hold accounts, sell to the reserve what they produce in excess of need, and buy what they need. In the first period, values follow the nearby market rate less the cost of transport; any incidental gain from trading with outside markets goes to the village’s common facilities; members without funds receive interest-free loans repaid in produce; and a nationalised cooperative bank stands as guarantor until the reserve can return its capital (AA, pp. 136–138).

          Justice in exchange is reached in three stages. First, the value of labour is evaluated through the prevailing currency, using wholesale rates. Second, the currency itself is evaluated on the basis of labour. Third, object values are evaluated on the value of labour alone, through usefulness and aesthetic value, and exchange proceeds free of profit, loss, and accumulation (AA, pp. 142–143). The Jeevan Vidya lectures add that the wish for a cyclical economy is present even in people living within a profit-obsessed system, and that producing in excess of need by one’s own labour is open to anyone now; Nagraj records that he lived with prosperity through his own production within the prevailing currency-based system (JV, pp. 153–154; AVD, p. 52). The public dialogue proposed for families puts the choice plainly: whether to live with autonomy, resolution, and prosperity or in dependency and accumulation, and whether the economy is to be driven by profit and symbolic currency or by exchange on the value of labour (JVD, pp. 217–218). Each committee’s work is evaluated against stated criteria. Production is evaluated by total output, speed, quality, productivity, skill, access to raw materials, and pollution-free technical improvement; exchange by its ease, its freedom from profit and loss, its cleanness, its measurement, and its management of means (AA, pp. 146–148).

          4. Economic theory on wants, scarcity, and wealth

          4.1 Aristotle: household management and unlimited acquisition

          Aristotle distinguished the art of household management, which acquires what is needed for living well, from the art of wealth-getting through exchange for gain. The first has a limit set by the needs of the household and the city; the second has no limit to its end, because it pursues money as such. He judged retail trade for gain contrary to nature and usury the most unnatural mode of acquisition, since it makes money breed money (Aristotle, Politics I.8–10; SEP-AP). The parallel with the darshan’s closure condition is close: natural wealth has a measure because it serves use, and acquisition becomes limitless when it attaches to the symbol of wealth. Yet Aristotle’s remedy rests on civic moderation and customary hierarchy rather than an ontology of the conscious unit, leaving unexplained why household moderation repeatedly breaks down whenever monetised trade and new techniques expand the scope for accumulation.

          4.2 Classical political economy, differential rent, and the stationary state

          The classical economists located the source of wealth in labour and expected growth eventually to give way to a stationary state of capital and population. David Ricardo showed that when land or mines differ in natural fertility and location, equal quantities of labour yield unequal quantities of produce; if goods exchange at a common value set by less fertile land, producers on more fertile land capture a differential rent that arises from nature’s endowment rather than additional human effort (Ricardo 1817). Any system that values products by a midpoint or average of labour across varying soils and regions must account for such differential endowments (§§8.3, 9.1). John Stuart Mill declined to regard the prospect of a stationary state with the aversion of his predecessors. A stationary condition of capital and population, he argued, implies no stationary state of human improvement, and might leave more room for culture, moral progress, and the preservation of nature than a society absorbed in getting on (Mill, Principles IV.6). Mill’s stationary state halts accumulation by ethical preference for a better life, but without the darshan’s distinction between the finite body and inexhaustible , it neither explains why accumulation outruns need in the first place nor shows how a society whose members still evaluate by priya–hita–labha can stop competing for relative advantage once capital growth slows.

          4.3 Marx: use, accumulation, and the value of labour

          Marx contrasted the circuit of simple commodity circulation, in which a commodity is sold for money in order to buy another commodity for use, with the circuit of capital, in which money buys commodities in order to be sold for more money. The first ends in consumption and so has a natural limit; the second begins and ends with money, and its movement is therefore limitless (Marx, Capital I, ch. 4). He grounded value in socially necessary labour time, the labour required under prevailing average conditions of skill and technique, and treated skilled labour as compound or multiplied simple labour because prior labour went into forming the worker’s skill (Marx, Capital I, ch. 1). Under capitalism, an innovator who introduces a labour-saving tool captures temporary extra surplus value until the technique spreads and lowers the socially necessary labour time of the product. In his Critique of the Gotha Programme, Marx distinguished two sequential phases of post-capitalist distribution: a first phase in which producers receive individual labour-time certificates after deductions for common funds and draw from the social stock goods of equal labour value, and a higher phase in which, after the productive forces and all-round human development have grown, society distributes from each according to ability to each according to need. Because workers differ in skill, strength, and family responsibilities, Marx noted that first-stage labour certificates still operate under an unequal “bourgeois right”, yielding unequal individual consumption claims until the higher phase is reached (Marx, Gotha Programme).

          The darshan shares much of Marx’s diagnosis of capital: profit as giving less and taking more, labour as the source of all product, and the endlessness of accumulation in money. Where Marx separated need-based distribution and equal labour-value exchange into two historical stages and tied first-stage claims to the individual worker’s labour certificate, the darshan pairs both principles simultaneously across institutional spheres: within the ten-member family, goods are shared according to need without weighing individual differences in skill or daily output (AA, pp. 1, 119–120), while between families, villages, and regions, goods exchange at equal labour value evaluated across skill, proficiency, speed, time, and means, free of profit and loss (AA, pp. 66, 76, 119, 145). Rather than rewarding technical invention through patent monopolies or individual consumption differentials, the darshan honours original tools and methods with formal awards (puraskar as svadhan) and disseminates them openly so that unit labour values and working hours fall across every family (§3.10). At the same time, the darshan shows why Marxian and state-socialist (niyantrit) economics hit a structural ceiling without jeevan and realisation. Treating the human being as a material organism shaped by external production relations leaves body-rooted evaluation (priya–hita–labha) intact even when private capital is abolished: regulated state economies still evaluate by profit and loss without a fixed satiation point (dhruv, AA, p. 5), and removing the market goad of profit and unemployment without awakening the inward reflection (pratyavartan) and restfulness of labour in resolution (, AA, pp. 23, 48–50) leads to slackening work effort and a bureaucratic managing mindset (AA, pp. 75–76, 107; JVD, p. 35). Moreover, the hypothesis that everything functions through conflict mistakes existence, in which every unit has inherent strength and progresses through mutual complementarity; class struggle cannot be the foundation of happiness (JV, p. 156). Axiology: Value Theory §3.8 compares the two accounts of labour value in more detail.

          4.4 Marginal utility, scarcity, and non-satiation

          In the 1870s, William Stanley Jevons, Carl Menger, and Léon Walras abandoned the classical labour theory of value. They argued that labour time alone cannot explain why water is more useful than diamonds yet commands a lower price, why labour spent on an unwanted object creates no exchange value, or how heterogeneous capital goods and waiting time enter into prices. In place of labour cost, they grounded exchange value in subjective marginal utility, the additional satisfaction a consumer expects from the last unit of a good (Jevons 1871). Modern economics took its standard definition from this marginalist turn through Lionel Robbins: economics studies human behaviour as a relationship between ends and scarce means which have alternative uses (Robbins 1932; SEP-PE). Scarcity in this sense is relative to wants that are taken as given and, in consumer theory, as non-satiated, so that more of a good is always preferred or at least not refused. Although microeconomic choice theory can mathematically write down a utility function with a local “bliss point” by dropping the axiom of non-satiation on paper, doing so does not produce satiation in an actual economy: when agents evaluate by subjective preference (priya–hita–labha), satiation in one commodity or efficiency gains in production simply redirect spending and freed resources into new sensory varieties, status goods, or financial claims.

          Grounding economics in subjective preferences and the disutility of labour also locks mainstream theory into two formal deadlocks whenever it attempts to address social balance. First, because subjective utility is anchored in individual bodily taste and advantage, which differ in every person and admit no interpersonal metric, welfare economics and social-choice theory cannot aggregate individual preferences into an uncoerced, consistent collective ordering of social needs and priorities (Sen 1999; SEP-PE); market prices resolve this only by weighting preferences according to purchasing power. Second, standard microeconomics models labour as a disutility—a sacrifice of leisure endured solely for extrinsic pay or consumption—so that any move toward unconditional need-based sharing or the abolition of monopoly rents predicts free-riding, shirking, and stalled innovation unless offset by monitoring and penalties (SEP-PE).

          The darshan answers both the marginalist critique of mechanical labour value and the deadlocks of subjective utility. Against a purely mechanical labour theory, it holds that labour creates value only insofar as it establishes genuine usefulness and aesthetic value for assessed human need: the chain runs from labour-based object to object-based usefulness to usefulness-based valuation (AA, p. 49). Against marginalism, it refuses to collapse usefulness into subjective desire or money willingness to pay. Usefulness is an objective relation between the properties of an object and the definite requirements of the body and society, so a kilogram of grain retains the same usefulness whatever its market price (JV, pp. 136–137). Above all, the darshan shows why social agreement and uncoerced work motivation require jeevan and realisation. Deliberation by priya, hit, and labh is structurally incapable of universal agreement because no two bodies are alike, whereas every human being is jeevan with the ability to realise () in the coexistence of all-pervasive omnipresence (, vyapak) and nature as a whole at once—an eternal, observer-invariant reality (trikalabadh )—grounding universal agreement in justice, , and truth (AA, pp. 18–20, 47, 51, 58–60; MVD, p. 203). Likewise, because jeevan’s powers are inexhaustible and find their inward strengthening (pratyavartan) and restfulness (shram ka vishram) in creative labour and resolution (§§2.3, 3.1), work does not depend on scarcity, wage fear, or monopoly profit.

          4.5 Absolute and relative needs

          John Maynard Keynes distinguished absolute needs, felt whatever the situation of others, from relative needs, satisfied only if they lift a person above others. The latter, he wrote, may indeed be insatiable, while the former could be met within a century, after which the economic problem might cease to be the permanent problem of humankind (Keynes 1930). Thorstein Veblen had described conspicuous consumption and pecuniary emulation, in which goods serve as marks of standing (Veblen 1899). Fred Hirsch argued that positional goods, whose value depends on others not having them, set social limits to growth that more production cannot overcome (Hirsch 1976).

          These accounts come nearest to the darshan’s distinction between definite bodily need and indefinite want, yet they also show why rising productivity alone failed to fulfil Keynes’s prediction. Keynes treated relative, positional wants as a secondary habit that material abundance would eventually allow society to outgrow. In the darshan’s account, so long as jeevan lacks awakening, its inexhaustible demand for continuity of happiness and for identity (swatva) has no non-material outlet and therefore attaches itself to relative comparison (tulana of big and small, more and less) and currency-based social standing (AA, pp. 45, 60, 104–105). Material abundance cannot quiet positional rivalry when jeevan itself has not reached its point of contentment in knowing, believing, recognising, and fulfilling coexistence (AA, p. 60).

          4.6 Money, credit, and mutual-credit clearing

          Most money in modern economies is created by commercial banks when they make loans, rather than lent out from prior deposits, and its quantity is limited mainly by the demand for credit, the banks’ assessment of risk, and monetary policy (BoE 2014). Interest on debt-created money is one of the mechanisms through which ecological economists argue that present economies depend on continued growth (Jackson 2017). Silvio Gesell made the darshan’s contrast between perishable goods and imperishable money the centre of his reform. Because goods decay and money does not, money holders can demand interest as the price of releasing money into circulation; he proposed money that loses value over time, so that it circulates like the goods it represents (Gesell, Natural Economic Order). By contrast, cooperative mutual-credit clearing networks, such as Switzerland’s Wirtschaftsring (WIR), founded in 1934, allow participating producers and households to trade goods and services against book-entry credits and debits on a central ledger without interest-bearing bank loans, expanding trade when cash is scarce (Stodder 2009).

          The darshan’s critique of symbolic value reaches Gesell’s diagnosis by a different road, and its account of the village exchange-reserve (§3.14) uses ledger clearing of the kind demonstrated by mutual-credit cooperatives to remove the occasion for interest rather than to tax money’s advantage. At the same time, the darshan shows why monetary and mutual-credit reforms cannot by themselves eliminate profit-seeking or artificial scarcity. A demurrage rule or a mutual-credit ledger is an accounting instrument; if the participants using it still evaluate by labh (giving less and getting more), they withhold scarce real goods from the clearing ring, demand shadow premiums, or shift accumulation from currency into land, equipment, or external financial assets. Interest-free clearing reaches a stable satiation point (vinimay ka tripti bindu, AA, p. 76) only when prosperity is already realised in the family and awakening replaces the pursuit of gain with mutual satisfaction (ubhay-tripti) in justice (§3.6; AA, pp. 50–52, 62–63, 76).

          4.7 Ecological and post-growth economics

          Nicholas Georgescu-Roegen argued that the economic process transforms low-entropy matter and energy into high-entropy waste, so that no economy can grow indefinitely on a finite earth (Georgescu-Roegen 1971). Herman Daly proposed a steady-state economy, with constant stocks of people and artefacts maintained by the lowest feasible throughput of matter and energy (Daly 1974). Kate Raworth’s doughnut joins a social foundation of human needs to an ecological ceiling of planetary boundaries (Raworth 2017), and Tim Jackson defines prosperity as the capability to flourish within ecological limits rather than as rising consumption (Jackson 2017). Earth-system scientists now assess six of nine planetary boundaries as transgressed (Richardson et al. 2023). The circular economy, as promoted in industry and policy, seeks to keep materials in use and design waste out of production.

          These programmes share the darshan’s judgement of unlimited growth and its insistence that production stay within natural balance. Yet because they explain continued growth mainly by external institutions—debt, competition, employment, and political pressure—without an ontology of jeevan and realisation, they encounter two structural obstacles in practice. First, when human beings evaluate by narrow priya–hita–labha and imagine private or isolated prosperity apart from the whole, discounting the distant future is individually rational, making multi-century restraint on deep-earth extraction politically fragile whenever immediate livelihoods or comfort are invoked (AA, pp. 30–31, 62–63, 78). Second, imposing macro-level throughput caps on populations whose jeevan still seeks continuity of happiness through material consumption and positional standing causes ecological limits to be experienced as a zero-sum rationing of well-being. In the darshan, ecological restraint becomes self-sustaining from within because awakening opens the higher five and a half activities of jeevan—relieving material throughput of the burden of supplying happiness—while a realised human sees the prosperity of oneself and one’s family in the prosperity of the whole and in the perpetuity of human civilization across generations (prayojansheelta, AA, pp. 62–63, 72, 94, 142, 146; KD, p. 7), removing the basis for intergenerational discounting.

          4.8 Gandhian economics: permanence and right livelihood

          J. C. Kumarappa contrasted an economy of transience, which draws down stocks accumulated by nature over ages, with an economy of permanence, which lives within the cycles of nature and recognises the interdependence of all its parts (Kumarappa 1945). He classified economies by their relation to others and to nature, from the parasitic and predatory to those of service, and advocated decentralised village production. E. F. Schumacher’s “Buddhist economics” sought maximum well-being with minimum consumption, treated non-renewable resources as capital to be conserved, and held that work develops human faculties rather than being only a cost (Schumacher 1973). Of all the economic traditions considered here, these are closest to arthshastra in content: village autonomy, cyclical energy and agriculture, production by the many, and restraint in wants.

          The decisive difference lies in how wants become definite. Gandhian and Buddhist economics rely on moral and spiritual exhortation—voluntary simplicity, duty, and the curbing of desire. As the treatise points out, neither outward-looking materialism nor inward-looking spiritualism based on meditation postures (sadhana), devotion, and renunciation has been able to halt the historical drift toward an indulgence- and accumulation-oriented civilisation, because jeevan’s powers of hope, thought, and desire are constitutionally inexhaustible (akshay) and continuously surging (uditodit), and inner contentment comes about only through awakening (AA, pp. 46–48, 50–51, 61). Asking an unawakened person to suppress want leaves jeevan’s demand for continuous fulfilment unaddressed, so moral restraint repeatedly erodes under industrial temptation. Rather than asking jeevan to shrink its aspiration, the darshan opens the higher five and a half activities through realisation of coexistence, fulfilling jeevan’s inexhaustible energy in understanding, relationship, and benevolence so that bodily needs settle naturally at their finite measure (AA, pp. 50, 95).

          4.9 Needs, capabilities, embeddedness, and institutions

          Several bodies of work address the questions that cyclical economics must answer in practice. Manfred Max-Neef and his colleagues argued that fundamental human needs are few, finite, and classifiable, and the same across cultures and periods, while the means of satisfying them, the satisfiers, vary (Max-Neef et al. 1989). Len Doyal and Ian Gough grounded universal basic needs in health and autonomy, and distinguished them from wants and preferences (Doyal and Gough 1991). Amartya Sen showed that evaluation cannot rest either on commodity holdings or on subjective utility, which adapts to deprivation and persuasion, and proposed assessing economic arrangements by people’s substantive functionings and capabilities to lead lives they have reason to value (Sen 1999). Karl Polanyi argued that human economies were historically embedded in social relationships, and that treating land (nature), labour (human life activity), and money (a token of purchasing power) as “fictitious commodities” ruled by self-regulating market prices disembeds the economy and corrodes both society and nature (Polanyi 1944). Friedrich Hayek argued that knowledge of particular circumstances is dispersed among many people and that the price system communicates it without any central authority possessing it (Hayek 1945). Elinor Ostrom showed that communities can govern shared resources through polycentric institutions that neither markets nor states supply (Ostrom 2010).

          Each of these bears on a specific part of the darshan’s proposal: the definiteness of need and capability, the refusal to treat nature, labour, and currency as ordinary commodities, the information that exchange beyond the village requires, and the governance of common natural assets. At the same time, each reaches a limit when separated from jeevan and realisation. Objective-need and capability frameworks (Max-Neef et al. 1989; Doyal and Gough 1991; Sen 1999) deliberately leave the weighting of capabilities and the boundary between sufficiency and luxury to democratic deliberation; yet among unawakened agents who evaluate by priya–hita–labha, public negotiation lacks an observer-invariant reference point and reverts to interest-group bargaining (AA, pp. 18–20, 58–60). Similarly, Ostrom’s polycentric commons and Polanyi’s social embedding work where conserving a local resource aligns with the material livelihood (hit and labh) of a bounded group or where state regulation compels compliance, but struggle when fossil-fuel or mineral extraction yields immediate local gain while dispersing harm globally and across generations (AA, pp. 30–31, 78), or when society must sustain unpaid universal services—education, health care, and ten-tier self-governance—beyond biological kin without either commercial fees or coercive taxation (§§3.7–3.8).

          4.10 Calculation, planning, and labour accounting

          In the debate over economic calculation, Ludwig von Mises and Hayek argued that without market prices a planned economy cannot compare the costs of alternative uses of resources, because the knowledge that prices summarise is dispersed and often tacit. Wassily Leontief’s input–output analysis represents an economy as a table of the inputs each sector requires from every other (Leontief 1936). From such a table the total labour required, directly and indirectly, to produce a unit of any good can be computed. Paul Cockshott and Allin Cottrell argued that modern computing makes economy-wide labour-time accounting feasible, and that labour time can serve as a rational basis for calculation and exchange, with priorities decided democratically (Cockshott and Cottrell 1993; Cottrell and Cockshott 1993).

          Their work shows that valuing goods by the labour they embody can be extended beyond the village with accounting methods that already exist. Yet the debate around computerised labour accounting also illustrates why calculation tools cannot substitute for awakening. An input–output matrix can compute direct and indirect hours, but if producers still act from priya–hita–labha and experience work as a disutility, they have every incentive to pad reported hours, understate their pace capacity, shade product quality, hoard tacit technical improvements, or trade scarce goods on shadow markets. The darshan’s fivefold valuation of labour across skill, proficiency, speed, time, and means (AA, p. 145) and its open, patent-free dissemination of inventions (§3.10) operate without a surveillance bureaucracy only because they rest on relationship balance in the ten-member family, the inward strengthening of jeevan in labour, and the satiation point of exchange (AA, pp. 21, 48–50, 76, 119–120).

          5. Indian and Advaita teaching on desire

          5.1 Desire as an unquenchable fire

          The insatiability of desire is a familiar theme in Indian thought. The Bhagavad Gita traces a chain from dwelling on sense objects to attachment, from attachment to desire, from desire to anger, and from anger to delusion and the loss of discrimination (BG 2.62–63). It names desire, born of rajas, as the enemy that covers knowledge, an insatiable fire (BG 3.37–39). The Manusmriti states that desire is never extinguished by the enjoyment of its objects, but grows like a fire fed with clarified butter (Manu 2.94). These texts agree with the darshan that enjoyment does not quiet desire and that desire obscures understanding.

          5.2 The fullness of the Self and the place of wealth

          Advaita Vedanta explains desire by ignorance of the Self. The Self is complete, and things are dear for the sake of the Self rather than for their own sake; desire arises when the Self is mistaken for the body and mind, so that a limited being seeks completion in objects (Axiology: Value Theory §§2.1–2.4; BU 2.4.5). The remedy is knowledge of the Self, prepared by disciplined action without attachment to its fruits (BG 3.19). Wealth, artha, is a legitimate aim of life when pursued within , but liberation stands above it, and the renunciate’s path leaves economic life behind (Axiology: Value Theory §§2.2, 2.4–2.5).

          The structural parallel with the darshan is clear. In both, insatiability arises from mistaking one’s being for the body and seeking in objects a fulfilment that objects cannot give. The darshan locates the remedy in understanding and coexistence, which is directed into family, production, and exchange, while Advaita locates it in knowledge of a Self for which the world of exchange has only empirical reality.

          6. The sciences of satiation and natural limits

          6.1 Homeostatic satiety

          Physiology confirms that the body regulates its own intake. The central nervous system integrates signals from fat stores and the gut, among them leptin, insulin, and ghrelin, to match food intake to energy expenditure and to keep body weight within a range over long periods (Morton et al. 2006). Bodily need has a definite measure, and the body has mechanisms that recognise when it has been met.

          6.2 Wanting, liking, and hedonic hunger

          The same research distinguishes this homeostatic regulation from motivation that runs beyond need. Kent Berridge and Terry Robinson separate “wanting”, the incentive salience mediated largely by dopamine systems, from “liking”, the hedonic impact of a reward. The two can come apart: wanting can be sensitised and grow while liking does not, which helps explain compulsive pursuit of rewards that no longer satisfy (Berridge and Robinson 2016). Michael Lowe and Meghan Butryn describe “hedonic hunger”, appetite driven by the anticipated pleasure of food in the absence of any energy deficit, as distinct from homeostatic hunger (Lowe and Butryn 2007). These findings give an empirical counterpart to the darshan’s distinction between definite bodily need and the inexhaustible cycle of selection and taste. They also clarify what changes when is introduced: if a human being is modelled solely as a biological organism, dopamine-mediated wanting and status competition appear as hard-wired evolutionary traits that policy can only nudge, medicate, or externally ration. In the darshan, the brain and cognitive organs are instruments operated by jeevan (AA, pp. 48–49): compulsive sensory wanting is what appears in the neural instrument when jeevan’s inexhaustible powers of hope, thought, and desire are channelled entirely through the four and a half activities, and it is regulated at the root when awakening opens the higher five and a half activities (AA, pp. 50, 95).

          6.3 Adaptation and the income–well-being debate

          People adapt to many changes in circumstance, though less completely than the original “hedonic treadmill” theory supposed. Set points are not neutral, differ among individuals, and can change (Diener et al. 2006). Richard Easterlin found that richer people within a country report greater happiness, while average happiness did not rise as national income grew over time (Easterlin 1974). Daniel Kahneman and Angus Deaton found that life evaluation rises with the logarithm of income while day-to-day emotional well-being levelled off at moderate incomes (Kahneman and Deaton 2010). Later work found no plateau on average, and a joint reanalysis located the plateau among the least happy minority (Killingsworth 2021; Killingsworth et al. 2023). Axiology: Value Theory §4.3 discusses this evidence. The data support neither a simple satiation point for income nor its absence. They show that more income does not close the gap between life evaluation and lived experience for many people, which is consistent with, though it does not establish, the darshan’s claim that material means cannot meet the need being placed on them.

          6.4 Earth-system limits

          The biogeochemical cycles that sustain life, of carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, and water, have been altered by human activity on a scale that now pushes several of them beyond the conditions of the past ten thousand years (Richardson et al. 2023). Thermodynamics sets a further limit on every production process (Georgescu-Roegen 1971). The darshan’s claim that extraction disturbs the earth’s balance and that production must keep to the proportions of natural regeneration is, in its general form, consistent with this science. Several of its specific causal hypotheses, such as the possible effects of extraction on the earth’s magnetic current or of cosmic rays on the stability of water (AA, pp. 31, 80–82), are not supported by current earth science and are treated as open in §9.7.

          7. Comparison

          7.1 Where insatiability comes from

          The traditions disagree most sharply on the source of insatiable want, and that disagreement determines whether an economy can actually close its cycle. Neoclassical theory takes non-satiation as given—or drops it only as a formal exercise on paper while leaving subjective preference (priya–hita–labha) untouched—and so cannot prevent efficiency gains or satiation in one good from rebounding into new commodities, positional rivalry, and financial accumulation. Aristotle and Marx locate limitlessness in the circuit of money as such, Veblen, Keynes, and Hirsch locate it in social comparison and positional goods, and Sen, Max-Neef, and Doyal and Gough separate objective needs and capabilities from subjective preferences. The Gita and Advaita locate insatiability in desire arising from ignorance of the Self, while Gandhian and Buddhist economics call for voluntary moral restraint. The sciences describe neural and psychological mechanisms—sensitised wanting, hedonic hunger, and adaptation—without an account of the conscious agent operating the brain.

          The darshan places the source in the joint form of a finite body and an inexhaustible (AA, pp. 16–18, 47–51). Because jeevan is a constitutionally complete sentient atom whose powers of hope, thought, and desire remain continuously surging (uditodit), its demand for continuous fulfilment cannot be switched off by moral asceticism, nor can it be satisfied by transient sensory intake through the five cognitive organs (AA, pp. 46–48, 50–51, 61; JV, p. 163). When jeevan operates in only four and a half activities, its inexhaustible energy is forced through sensory selection, taste, and comparative calculation, turning money and positional goods into surrogates for continuity. Only when awakening opens the higher five and a half activities through realisation () of coexistence does jeevan’s inexhaustible activity find continuous, non-material fulfilment in understanding, relationship, and benevolence, allowing bodily consumption to settle naturally at its finite homeostatic measure (AA, pp. 50–51, 58–60, 95).

          7.2 What counts as wealth, and what closes the cycle

          For the money economy, wealth is measured in currency and its growth is the aim. For classical and Marxian economics, wealth is the product of labour, but capital accumulates in money. For ecological economics, wealth must stay within throughput limits, and the circular economy closes material loops. For Polanyi, treating land, labour, and money as market commodities disembeds the economy from society. For the darshan, economics has no standing in isolation from the five dimensions of human orderliness; wealth is only the produced object; money is a symbol; and the cycle closes in three places together: need is definite, goods are used rather than hoarded, and natural assets are used in proportion to their regeneration. The circular economy addresses the third. Daly’s steady state addresses the second and third. Only the darshan, Gandhian economics, and in part Keynes make the first, the definiteness of need, a condition of the economy’s structure.

          The traditions also value labour, skill, and invention differently, and here the structural necessity of jeevan and realisation across all six economic problems (§1.3) becomes clear. Since the marginalist revolution, market economics has priced goods by subjective marginal utility, treated labour as a disutility paid the price it commands (Jevons 1871), and rewarded technical invention through temporary monopoly rents and patents. Marx grounded the value of commodities in socially necessary labour time, reduced skilled labour to multiplied simple labour by the training embodied in the worker (Capital I, ch. 1), and separated post-capitalist distribution into a first stage of individual labour-time certificates—which still apply an unequal “bourgeois right” to workers of differing talent and family dependency—and a distant higher stage of distribution according to need (Marx, Gotha Programme). Computerised labour-time accounting likewise computes values from average hours and inputs (§4.10), while mutual-credit networks clear balances without interest (§4.6) and capability or commons frameworks rely on democratic negotiation (Sen 1999; Ostrom 2010).

          Without the ontology of jeevan and realisation (anubhav), each of these non-market mechanisms hits a wall: 1. Valuation and social agreement: Deliberation by body-rooted perspectives (priya–hita–labha) cannot reach universal agreement (AA, pp. 18–20, 58–60), so subjective utility admits no fair aggregation and democratic capability lists degenerate into interest-group bargaining; only the observer-invariant realisation (anubhav) of the coexistence of all-pervasive omnipresence (, vyapak) and nature as a whole at once (trikalabadh , AA, pp. 47, 51; MVD, p. 203) gives all humans an identical reference point in justice, , and truth. 2. Work and innovation incentives: If work is experienced as a disutility, removing profit, wage fear, and patents leads to shirking, quality shading, and bureaucratic stagnation, as seen in regulated (niyantrit) state economies (AA, pp. 5, 75–76, 107; JVD, p. 35); in the darshan, jeevan never sleeps and experiences skilled production as the outward projection (paravartan) and inward strengthening (pratyavartan) of its own inexhaustible faculties, culminating in the restfulness of resolution (shram ka vishram = , AA, pp. 18–19, 23, 48–50, 60) and motivating open, patent-free invention (§3.10). 3. Surplus sharing and public services: Without awakening, unconditional sharing stops at biological kin and vital services must either be commercialised as trades in learning, healing, and office (AA, pp. 45–46; JV, pp. 166–168) or funded by coercive state taxation; in the darshan, because every member is jeevan with the ability to realise in the coexistence of satta (vyapak) and nature as a whole at once (AA, pp. 47, 51, 60; MVD, p. 203), relationship balance forms the ten-member family where goods are shared by need without weighing individual labour (AA, pp. 1, 21, 119–120), and household prosperity naturally gives rise to benevolence (upkar-pravritti), supporting teachers, healers, and council representatives in unpaid benevolence-focused service (upkar-pradhan seva, §§3.7–3.8; KD, pp. 115–117). 4. Profit-free exchange and intergenerational ecology: Mutual-credit ledgers, labour-time computers, and ecological caps are gamed or resisted when agents seek private labh and discount the future; in the darshan, family prosperity and evaluation by justice establish a natural satiation point of exchange (vinimay ka tripti bindu, AA, p. 76) at fivefold labour value (AA, pp. 119, 145), while seeing the prosperity of oneself and one’s family in the prosperity of the whole and the perpetuity of human civilization (prayojansheelta) eliminates intergenerational discounting (AA, pp. 62–63, 72, 94, 142, 146; KD, p. 7).

          8. Bringing the economy into balance: measurement, intelligent systems, and multi-level AI agents

          8.1 What can be measured and what must be judged

          The darshan joins two kinds of order in one sequence. Knowledge is law, law is regulation, and regulation is balance; balanced living is justice in humans; living within universal orderliness with justice is , that is, resolution; and realisation in coexistence is dharma and truth (MVD, p. 173). Law (niyam), regulation (niyantran), and balance (santulan) are already evident in the orders other than the human, which are bound by their own laws (AA, p. 117). Justice (), dharma, and truth () are the evaluations by which humans establish their own orderliness, and they are the activities the treatise describes as still under review in human tradition (AA, p. 18).

          This distinction sets the division of labour for any instrument used in a cyclical economy. On one side, it shows why no computational or AI system can substitute for and realisation: where a question concerns the fulfilment of a relationship, the purpose of a choice, or its accord with coexistence, it belongs to human evaluation. On the other side, it explains why a strong intelligent infrastructure becomes indispensable while humanity is still in the process of realisation (-gami). Awakening does not occur instantaneously across eight billion people, and even individuals who have accepted the direction of humane conduct remain at varying stages of maturity and can commit mistakes, misjudge needs, or waste resources out of residual ignorance, over-expectation, or conditioning (AA, p. 139). The treatise’s safeguard against individual error is neither punitive policing nor reliance on isolated personal conscience, but collective evaluation and mutual correction across nested councils: when someone in a family makes a mistake, the duty of correcting it belongs to all the members of the family; the ten-family cluster evaluates family representatives and resolves inter-family difficulties; the village council and general assembly of account-holders evaluate the five committees; and the justice-security committee conducts a self-motivated process of reform (AA, pp. 128–130, 138–140, 146–149).

          That collective self-correction relies on a decisive premise grounded in the darshan’s account of human nature (§2.2): while a single individual in the process of realisation may act erroneously or slip into a self-serving (priya–hita–labha) impulse, a collective council of peers cannot quietly endorse injustice or ecological waste once the objective facts and logical consequences are laid bare in common view, because no human wants to be exploited or to see the shared order fail, and justice, dharma, and truth command universal agreement (sarvasammati) whenever reality is visible (AA, pp. 18–20, 58–60; MVD, p. 203). For a council or community to correct itself whenever an individual errs, however, two things must be continuously present before the group: unobscured visibility of the facts and checkable causal logic linking actions to their systemic consequences. Where a relation is governed by law, regulation, or balance—the regeneration rate of a forest, the fertility of a field, the stock and age of goods in a store, the fivefold labour embodied in a product, the balance of an account, or the matching of regional surpluses and deficits—an instrument can measure, cross-check, and trace its logic across tiers, placing any inconsistency directly before the responsible council so that the collective cannot ignore it and is prompted to act.

          The darshan also states how instruments should be trusted. Every instrument is conceived, planned, and produced by human beings, and their mentality is embodied in its planning. An instrument falls short of the purpose for which it was planned, so trust in it and use of it should stay below that purpose, and every signal it gives must still be read by a human being (KD, p. 139). Technique multiplies the speed of whatever it serves, problems included (SB, p. 267). Artificial Intelligence and Coexistence §§1.2–1.5 places present AI systems in the material order as insentient instruments, yantra, that can reproduce the outward pattern of evaluation by pleasure, bodily advantage, and gain and so amplify delusion, or, directed by explicit humane criteria, make the factual basis and logical consistency of every decision visible to the councils and communities who make it.

          8.2 Why databases and network connectivity alone are not enough

          Writing in the 1990s, Shri A. Nagraj anticipated the role of computing in a self-governing economy. The treatise prescribes a detailed household and village survey before self-governance begins (AA, pp. 122–125), sets measurable criteria for evaluating each of the five committees across ten tiers (AA, pp. 146–149), expects computers to be used in the village exchange-reserve (AA, p. 138), and envisages every committee linked by a computer network from the village to the world, so that information located anywhere is accessible to the village (AA, p. 132).

          A closer look at what an Earth-scale cyclical economy and the ten-tier self-governing order require shows why a strong AI agentic backbone is a structural requirement rather than an optional convenience, and why passive databases and network connectivity alone cannot establish or sustain this order. A database stores tables of records, and a network transmits them from one terminal to another. Neither can detect hidden inconsistencies across tiers, trace multi-stage causal logic, or place verifiable facts before human councils at the moment of deliberation. Five structural requirements explain why an intelligent agentic backbone is indispensable for a planetary civilization whose members are in the process of realisation.

          1. The planetary-scale visibility and collective self-correction requirement. In a single ten-member family or a tiny, isolated hamlet producing only grain by hand, material facts are directly visible to the naked eye: everyone can see whose granary is full, who is leaving work undone, or which grove of trees is being overcut, so the group can immediately notice an individual’s error and correct it face to face (AA, pp. 129–130). At the scale of an Earth-spanning civilization—ten nested tiers connecting hundreds of millions of families from the village to the world council (AA, pp. 27–30, 126–127) and producing telecommunications, transport, medical instruments, and industrial machinery alongside local food and crafts (AA, pp. 29–30, 112, 115)—naked-eye visibility disappears. Without an active agentic backbone that continuously cross-checks records against physical balances, traces upstream and downstream consequences across tiers, and surfaces factual inconsistencies in plain view of the relevant council, individual errors, quiet hoarding, labour-value distortions, and ecological drawdowns remain invisible or buried in raw tables until they become systemic crises. Making facts and checkable causal logic continuously visible to councils across all ten tiers is what allows a planetary civilization to govern itself through collective self-correction rather than either anonymous market prices or a coercive policing state.
          2. The fault-tolerance requirement while individuals are in the process of realisation. A cyclical economy cannot wait until every human being on Earth is already fully realised (anubhav-moolak) and incapable of error. It must operate across generations in which individuals are moving toward realisation (anubhav-gami) while still carrying conditioned habits of evaluating by pleasure, bodily advantage, and gain (priya–hita–labha; JV, pp. 72–73) and occasionally acting erroneously out of incomplete understanding or over-expectation (AA, p. 139). If an error or self-serving deviation remains private or ambiguous, an individual can rationalise or ignore it. When AI agents paired with families, clusters, and village or regional committees automatically surface the verified facts and the step-by-step logic of an inconsistency to the collective council (AA, pp. 128–130, 138, 146–149), the group cannot look away: common visibility of checkable facts compels collective deliberation, and even when one individual or committee errs, the people as a whole act to correct the course (AA, pp. 129–130, 139–140). During the three stages of transition (AA, pp. 142–143), the same agents also maintain dual ledgers in wholesale currency prices and midpoint labour values while protecting participating villages against outside speculative arbitrage (AA, pp. 136–138).
          3. The part-time governance and producer-staffing constraint. In the treatise’s institutional design, self-governance is not run by a class of full-time officials. Members of the village council and its five committees are part-time representatives who continue their own physical production in their families (AA, p. 127). The village exchange-reserve, which handles storage, valuation, and clearing for a hundred families, is staffed by as few as three people (AA, p. 138), and across society at least eighty percent of people remain prosperous in production while at most twenty percent serve in coordination (MVD, p. 259). If ten tiers of five committees had to manually enter and query database tables, reconcile seasonal surpluses and deficits across thousands of villages, trace multi-stage labour values, and audit ecological regeneration, governance would either stall under cognitive and administrative overload or require a large full-time bureaucracy. Creating such a managerial class would reproduce the shift from a working mindset to a managing mindset that the darshan identifies as an obstacle to universal participation in production (JVD, p. 35; AA, p. 107).
          4. The non-price coordination and calculation bottleneck. In a market economy, prices and interest rates compress dispersed local information about supply, demand, and scarcity into a single number that anyone can read, though they distort value through speculation, unequal purchasing power, and profit-seeking (§4.9; Hayek 1945). Replacing market prices with raw database records across millions of goods, variable seasonal yields, tool and building depreciation, and Ricardian differences in soil and mineral fertility (§§4.2, 4.10; Ricardo 1817; Leontief 1936) creates an immense coordination task. Passive databases do not propagate a local harvest shortfall through an inter-regional input–output matrix, separate natural endowment from human effort, or generate checkable options for routing surpluses to cover deficits (AA, p. 72) and scheduling multi-year infrastructure without interest rates. Without intelligent agents that continuously compute these relationships and present verifiable logical traces to human councils, non-price coordination cannot scale beyond a handful of villages.
          5. The qualitative-to-quantitative translation bottleneck. The treatise’s instruments of self-governance combine numerical stocks with qualitative local judgements: a family’s assessment of its common and special aspirations (AA, pp. 26, 112), the village survey of skills, land, water, and cottage industries (AA, pp. 122–125), the joint planning of multi-occupation training by the education, production, and exchange-reserve committees (AA, p. 131), and the periodic evaluation of all five committees (AA, pp. 146–149). Traditional database software demands rigid forms and technical query languages that turn producers into clerks. Intelligent agents can converse with ordinary producers and family representatives in natural language, structure qualitative observations into living records, cross-check them against settled rules of law, regulation, and balance, and translate multi-tier accounts back into clear, local summaries that every council member can inspect and verify.

          8.3 AI agents across the ten-tier order: from the individual and family to the world council

          The companion technical note on a personal AI assistant argues that while today’s commercial AI systems act as amplified four-and-a-half-activity machines tuned to capture attention and stimulate appetite, a personal AI assistant designed under the darshan’s criteria can serve an individual in transition (anubhav-gami) as a diagnostic mirror, an educational companion, and a practical assistant, helping the person recognise delusion in real time and act toward awakening. Establishing and governing a cyclical economy at planetary scale requires extending that principle from the individual to every collective unit in the ten-tier family-rooted self-governing order (parivar-mulak swarajya vyavastha). In this architecture, each human unit, from the person and the family to each committee and council across all ten tiers, works together with a corresponding AI agent that acts both as an operational coordinator and as a collective consistency mirror, ensuring that facts and causal logic remain continuously visible to the group responsible for acting on them.

Paired with the individual producer and learner, the personal AI assistant supports the transition from restricted (priya–hita–labha) evaluation to humane conduct (Technical Note: The Personal AI Assistant §§2–3). As a diagnostic mirror, it helps the person notice when consumption, work, or interpersonal choices are driven by sensory impulse (), status comparison, or the urge to take more and give less, prompting reflection against justice, dharma, and truth. As an educational companion, it supports sustained study () of coexistence alongside practical learning across multiple occupations, such as agriculture, dairy, cottage crafts, and machine repair, so the individual becomes self-reliant in occupation and free of occupational fear (AA, pp. 64–65, 131). As a practical assistant, it logs the person’s work pace, time, and skill development for the producer’s own review and, where the producer chooses, for village labour accounting.

          8.4 Failure points of the cycle and early warning

Because these signals are presented in common view to the entire family, cluster, or council rather than buried in private files, they cannot simply be ignored: even if a single individual or representative has acted erroneously, the visible discrepancy obliges the collective to examine the facts and take corrective action (AA, pp. 129–130, 139–140, 146–149).           8.5 Giving human labour its value across villages and regions

          The darshan’s valuation of labour needs records: the pace of work in each activity, the time and means each product requires, the quantities different producers achieve under different conditions, and the remuneration producers actually receive (AA, pp. 66, 119, 145). Within a single village for a staple crop, such records can be kept by hand. Between villages, districts, and regions, and for industrial products assembled from many inputs across tiers, manual calculation breaks down, and the connected production and exchange-reserve agents of §8.3 do the heavy computational work.

          Across the tier network, the agents keep labour records for each kind of product and compute a common value of labour from the range of yields and paces across many villages, as the treatise does for wheat within one village, updating it as techniques and generational means change (AA, pp. 66, 118, 145). They compute the labour embodied in complex goods by tracing their inputs back through the chain of production across cottage, small, medium, and large industries, using the input–output methods described in §4.10, including the depreciation of tools, machines, and buildings, so that the past labour used up in each round of production is accounted for without charging interest. Across villages and regions with unequal soil fertility, rainfall, or mineral access, the agents model differences in yield caused by natural endowment (Ricardo 1817) separately from differences in human effort and skill, so that midpoint valuation does not quietly penalise less fertile villages or award resource rents to more fertile ones. During the transition they maintain both measures side by side, prices in currency and values in labour, so that the second and third stages of the treatise’s path, evaluating currency on the basis of labour and then exchanging on labour value alone, have verified figures at every step (AA, pp. 142–143). They show each producer what their labour established and what they received for it, show each village where value is being siphoned off between producer and consumer, and match local skill profiles to the production a village needs so the education committee can plan training (AA, p. 131).

          The method of valuation remains a human decision. The village council decides how object values are evaluated and consolidated, and the production and exchange-reserve committees jointly determine the quantity, quality, and value of labour for the village (AA, pp. 67, 143). Inside the family, contributions are not weighed at all (AA, pp. 119–120). The agents supply measurement, input–output tracing, and counterfactual modelling; people decide what counts as a fair common value and how differences of skill and circumstance are honoured. Giving labour its true value is also more than computing an exchange ratio. It means recognising what labour establishes, nourishment, protection, and social participation, and through right use the orderliness of the whole (§§3.5–3.6). Records of what products actually fulfilled for the families who used them belong to the same account.

          8.6 Keeping the cycle in balance and accelerating the transition

          Within the framework of §§8.3–8.4, the agent network turns the village survey and the council evaluation rubrics (AA, pp. 122–125, 146–149) into a living operational model rather than a one-time paper census, and summarises it for families and part-time committees in plain language. At the village level, the committee agents keep the exchange-reserve’s accounts, track stock and its age, compare extraction with regeneration for each natural asset, project when a trend will cross a regenerative limit, and estimate from the land and water of a village or district how many families it can support with prosperity (AA, p. 92). When an early failure signal or an individual or committee error appears, the relevant agent flags it immediately and presents the verifiable facts and step-by-step causal trace to the council in common view. Because the evidence and logic are visible to all members of the council simultaneously, no single representative can quietly dismiss an anomaly, and the group as a whole is equipped to deliberate and correct the error through the treatise’s reformative procedures (AA, pp. 128–130, 139–140). Where a rule has already been settled by the council, for example the proportion of a forest that may be cut in a year, the reordering of a store, or the routing of seasonal surplus between neighbouring villages, the agent executes the routine coordination and reports what it did.

          Across the ten tiers from village to world, agent-to-agent communication addresses the civilizational coordination problem that markets delegate to prices and interest rates and state socialism delegates to central planning ministries (§§4.9–4.10). By continuously aggregating family and village need assessments alongside regional production capacities and ecological constraints, higher-tier agents let a district, province, or world council see the real-time balance of production and need across its constituent tiers, expose any inter-regional inconsistency to shared council scrutiny, plan which village or region supplies what another lacks, schedule multi-year investments in shared infrastructure across tiers without relying on an interest rate, and clear exchange between regions at labour value (AA, pp. 72, 115, 118; MVD, p. 268).

          This agentic backbone is equally required to make the transition from the current system viable at scale. Today, any community attempting to move toward cyclical economics must operate inside a global market economy whose price signals, credit terms, and supply chains are coordinated by advanced algorithmic systems tuned for profit. An isolated village cooperative relying on manual ledgers or static spreadsheets faces prohibitive friction in running dual accounting (currency and labour value), matching diverse household needs with local multi-occupation output, detecting internal drift while members are still unlearning priya–hita–labha habits, and coordinating with other transitioning communities. When each new family, cluster, and village is equipped with pre-configured agents that already speak the common protocol of the five committees and ten tiers, a transitioning community can immediately conduct internal need assessment, make inconsistencies visible for collective self-correction, run dual-stage exchange-reserve clearing (AA, pp. 142–143), pool surpluses with other participating villages across distances, and reduce its dependence on speculative currency markets step by step.

          8.7 Automation, participation, and knowledge as a common

          The darshan’s criterion for technique (§3.11) applies to AI with special force. AI and automation can take over drudgery, extend skill, and reduce the time production requires, which is the generational gain the treatise expects from means rightly used (AA, p. 118). They can also concentrate capital, displace producers, and replace the working mindset with a managing one, the very drives the treatise and the public discourse identify as obstacles (AA, p. 107; JVD, p. 35). A cyclical economy deploys AI to raise the usefulness each person’s labour establishes and to free time for study, family life, and participation in self-governance, while keeping every person engaged in physical production according to capacity, receptivity, and ability (MVD, p. 265; AI study §1.8). The test of any deployment is whether it keeps producers producing and lets them see the value of their work, or turns them into passive consumers and clerks managed by others.

          Because research and dissemination are inseparable and the powers of jeevan cannot be stored in any reserve (AA, pp. 56–58), the knowledge an AI system carries, its models, weights, protocols, and records of agricultural and craft practice, belongs among the means each generation hands on to the next. The treatise already expects every committee from the village to the world to share information through a computer system (AA, p. 132). The Personal, Family, and Committee AI Agents built for a cyclical economy must therefore be open common infrastructure owned by the communities that use them, carrying technical knowledge to ordinary producers as teachers are asked to do (AA, p. 29) rather than being enclosed as proprietary intellectual property for rent or profit.

          8.8 Governance, safeguards, and what must return to people

          The questions in the last column of §8.4 are of a different kind from the calculations performed by the agent network. Whether a family’s assessment of need is true and fair, whether an exchange is free of exploitation, whether a member’s share in production matches ability and receptivity, whether a technique accords with orderliness, and whether a use of the forest serves the earth’s balance are questions of justice, dharma, and truth. The treatise assigns them to people and institutions: the family representative evaluates the members of the family, the family cluster evaluates its representative, the village council decides the method of valuing objects, and the justice-security committee resolves disputes through a reformative process (AA, pp. 67, 128–129, 139–140). At every level of the ten-tier architecture, an AI agent is paired with a human unit as an instrument (yantra), never as an autonomous authority: it ends its work by presenting the evidence, the trade-offs, and the question to the person, family, or committee responsible, not by deciding on their behalf.

          Several architectural safeguards follow directly from the darshan’s account. First, no agent at any tier may be optimised for the perspectives that drive the open spiral, maximising throughput, engagement, attention capture, or monetary gain, since that would turn the infrastructure into an amplifier of the error described in §2 (AI study §1.5; Technical Note §1). Second, data custody follows the subsidiarity of the ten tiers: a person’s reflective logs belong to the person, a family’s internal records belong to the family, and village records remain open and accountable to the villagers (AA, p. 128), with higher-tier agents receiving only the aggregated balances needed for inter-tier coordination. Third, the agent network must not become a technocratic seat of rulership: power and centralised rule carry the direction of non-coexistence, and universal orderliness is participatory (AA, p. 82). Every model rule, labour-valuation formula, and ecological threshold used by the agents must remain inspectable and revisable by the councils they serve. Fourth, the material, mineral, and energy footprint of computing hardware and model training belongs within the cycle it serves, counted against regenerative limits like any other branch of production (AI study §1.7).

          The multi-level agent architecture described here applies the darshan’s distinctions to intelligent instruments that did not exist when the texts were written; the texts speak of computers as aids to exchange-reserve accounting and world-wide committee communication (AA, pp. 132, 138) and state the general rule for trusting instruments (KD, p. 139). Whether settled rules can be stated precisely enough for agents to apply them across ten tiers, whether diagnostic feedback helps anubhav-gami individuals and families overcome conditioned habits of overconsumption, how far computed labour values can be relied on across complex supply chains, and how communities can govern a federated agent network without ceding judgement to it are questions for practice, collected with the other open problems in §9.7. The companion technical note on a personal AI assistant sets out the criteria of source fidelity, non-manipulative design, and human benefit that every agent in this hierarchy would need to satisfy.

          9. Critical review

          9.1 Madhyasth Darshan — an explanation of insatiability with a demanding institutional path

          Strengths.

          • Its foundational novelty lies in introducing the ontology of —a constitutionally complete sentient atom (gathan-poorna parmanu) whose powers of hope, thought, and desire are inexhaustible (akshay) and continuously surging (uditodit) (AA, pp. 16–18, 47–51)—together with the observer-invariant realisation () of coexistence (trikalabadh , AA, pp. 47, 51, 58–60; MVD, p. 203) into the core of economic theory. This resolves the sixfold deadlock that blocks body-centric economic models across insatiability, social agreement, work motivation, surplus sharing, profit-free exchange, and intergenerational ecology.
          • By showing how awakening opens the higher five and a half activities of jeevan to regulate selection and taste from within (AA, pp. 50, 95), it explains how bodily demand becomes definite without relying on ascetic suppression or coercive rationing (AA, pp. 46–48, 61), why skilled labour and open, patent-free invention become self-rewarding expressions of jeevan’s outward projection and inward strengthening (paravartan–pratyavartan) culminating in resolution (, AA, pp. 18–19, 23, 48–50, 140, 144), and how relationship balance and seeing one’s own and one’s family’s prosperity in the prosperity of the whole and the perpetuity of human civilization sustain ten-member family sharing, unpaid benevolent societal service (upkar-pradhan seva), the satiation point of exchange (AA, p. 76), and multi-generational ecological stewardship (AA, pp. 21, 62–63, 72, 94, 142, 146; KD, pp. 7, 115–117).
          • Its definition of wealth as produced objects and of capital as human labour power keeps economics tied to provision and makes the critique of symbolic value precise.
          • By pairing need-based sharing inside the family with equal labour-value exchange between families, villages, and regions, it avoids both the market reduction of family life to exchange and the state-socialist deferral of need-based distribution to a distant historical stage.
          • Its cycle includes the renewal of human capacity and the closure of need, not only the closure of material loops, and it nests the economy within the balances of nature and the five dimensions of human orderliness.
          • It offers a concrete institutional path, with surveys, an exchange-reserve, a staged transition from market prices to labour values, and evaluation criteria for each committee.
          • Its policy names the value of human labour explicitly: everyone engaged in production, honour according to capacity, prevention of the exploitation of the fruits of labour, and exchange at labour value within and between nations.
          • It gives a clear rule for instruments: they embody human mentality, deserve bounded trust, and must have their signals read by people.

          Weaknesses.

          • Making awakening and realisation the precondition for economic balance is what enables the darshan to solve theoretical deadlocks that defeat purely institutional reforms, yet it is also the proposal’s steepest practical hurdle: the darshan itself describes awakening as not yet established in human tradition, and individuals in the process of realisation (anubhav-gami) remain capable of error (AA, p. 139). Operating the order before universal perfection therefore requires combining the treatise’s council-based mutual evaluation (AA, pp. 128–130, 146–149) with an active informational backbone that makes facts and inconsistencies visible to groups so they can self-correct collectively (§§8.1–8.4).
          • The valuation of labour by pace, time, and means meets the problems classical and Marxian economics faced in reducing heterogeneous labour to a common measure, accounting for capital depreciation without an interest rate, and adjusting for Ricardian differential rent when natural fertility or mineral access varies across villages and regions. Midpoint averages may work for wheat within a village; their extension to complex, multi-stage products made across unevenly endowed regions needs the accounting methods discussed in §§4.2, 4.10, and 8.5, which the treatise does not itself develop.
          • Coordination across an Earth-scale civilization relies on part-time councils and committees linked by computers (AA, pp. 127, 132, 138). Passive databases and network connectivity alone cannot perform the real-time information processing, multi-stage consistency checking, and intertemporal allocation that prices and interest rates address (§§4.4, 4.9, 8.2); §§8.2–8.6 show why a federated ten-tier AI agentic backbone is a structural requirement—not an optional add-on—for making facts and checkable causal logic visible to councils across the Earth without creating a full-time bureaucracy.
          • The critique of specialisation (AA, pp. 70–71) sits uneasily with the special aspirations the darshan endorses, telephony, broadcasting, and transport, which depend on highly specialised production; the treatise’s own allowance for large industries at higher tiers suggests the objection is to specialisation severed from wholeness, but this is not made explicit.
          • Several of the treatise’s natural-science claims, on the earth’s magnetic current and on cosmic rays and water, go beyond present evidence and weaken its ecological argument where its general claims are well supported.

          provides a genuinely new foundation for economics by showing why human economies cannot reach balance without jeevan and realisation, and its institutional proposal is more specific than most alternatives, while scaling it across an Earth-spanning civilization of individuals in the process of realisation requires both educational transformation and the ten-tier AI agentic backbone developed in §8.

          9.2 Neoclassical economics — coordination without a stopping point

          Strengths.

          • It provides powerful tools for coordinating production and exchange among very large numbers of people who do not know one another.
          • Its methods of measurement, national accounts, input–output analysis, and the study of incentives, are indispensable to any economy that must assess its own condition.
          • Its formal analysis of incentive compatibility, information asymmetry, and social choice clarifies the exact conditions under which agents who evaluate by self-interest (priya–hita–labha) and treat work as a disutility will free-ride, game rules, or fail to reach collective agreement (SEP-PE).

          Weaknesses.

          • By taking wants as given, it has no account of why they grow and no criterion for when enough has been produced; dropping the mathematical axiom of non-satiation on paper does not stop real-world rebound, positional escalation, or financial accumulation while agents evaluate by subjective preference (priya–hita–labha).
          • Grounding value in body-rooted subjective preferences leaves it without an uncoerced interpersonal basis for agreeing on social needs, while modelling labour as a disutility makes work and innovation dependent on wage pressure, profit margins, and patent monopolies.
          • Its measure of success, growth of output valued in money, counts depletion of natural assets as income and rewards artificial scarcity, and its separation of economics from the purposes of human life leaves those purposes to be supplied by whatever wants prevail.

          Neoclassical economics coordinates without a stopping point, and its tools can serve a cyclical economy only if that economy supplies the ontology of jeevan and the evaluative criteria the discipline leaves out.

          9.3 Marxian and ecological economics — a sound diagnosis on a different ground

          Strengths.

          • Marx’s analysis of the circuit of capital and of the endlessness of accumulation in money matches the darshan’s critique closely.
          • Ecological economics grounds the limits of growth in thermodynamics and earth-system science and has developed measures of throughput and planetary boundaries.

          Weaknesses.

          • Abolishing private capital without an ontology of jeevan and realisation leaves body-rooted evaluation (priya–hita–labha) and the disutility of labour intact: first-stage labour certificates preserve unequal claims, while removing profit and wage fear without awakening the inward strengthening (pratyavartan) and restfulness of labour in resolution (samadhan) produces bureaucratic management and slackening effort (AA, pp. 5, 75–76, 107; JVD, p. 35), and reliance on class conflict contradicts mutual complementarity (JV, p. 156).
          • Ecological economics explains continued growth by institutions—debt, competition, and employment—without an account of the conscious unit whose demand for continuous happiness those institutions serve; when agents seek private prosperity apart from the whole and look for fulfilment in consumption, distant centuries are discounted and macro-level throughput caps are experienced as zero-sum rationing rather than chosen balance.

          Marxian and ecological economics diagnose the open spiral soundly from the side of capital and of nature, and the darshan supplies the ontology of jeevan and realisation that both need to close the cycle without coercion.

          9.4 Gandhian economics — the closest relative

          Strengths.

          • Kumarappa’s economy of permanence and Schumacher’s Buddhist economics share the darshan’s village autonomy, cyclical agriculture and energy, production by the many, and restraint of wants.
          • They have a long record of practical experiment in Indian villages and in appropriate-technology movements.

          Weaknesses.

          • Their restraint of wants rests on moral and religious exhortation—voluntary simplicity and the suppression of desire—which repeatedly erodes because jeevan’s powers of hope, thought, and desire are constitutionally inexhaustible (akshay) and continuously surging (uditodit), and cannot find contentment until awakening opens the higher five and a half activities (AA, pp. 46–48, 50–51, 61).
          • They give little attention to the fivefold valuation of labour across skill, proficiency, speed, time, and means or to a staged transition from money exchange.

          Gandhian economics is the nearest relative of cyclical economics in practice, and the darshan offers it a foundation in the ontology of jeevan and realisation that replaces moral asceticism with the fulfilment of jeevan’s higher faculties.

          9.5 Advaita and Indian ethics — a diagnosis of desire without an economic architecture

          Strengths.

          • The Gita and Advaita give a penetrating account of desire as arising from mistaken identification with body and mind, and agree with the darshan that enjoyment does not quiet desire.
          • The ordering of artha under gives wealth a place without making it the aim of life.

          Weaknesses.

          • Their remedy is directed inward, to knowledge of the Self and detachment, and offers no institutional design for production and exchange.
          • Treating the empirical world as of lower reality makes the economy a field to be transcended rather than an order to be established.

          Advaita Vedanta and Indian ethics share the darshan’s diagnosis of desire, and the darshan differs in turning the remedy outward into family, production, and exchange.

          9.6 What the darshan can learn from economics

          Six lessons from economic theory bear directly on cyclical economics. First, coordination beyond a single village or local community requires active information processing and common-knowledge visibility, not only passive database connectivity; the treatise’s own transitional reliance on market rates shows that prices carry dispersed knowledge which part-time councils across an Earth-scale ten-tier civilization must obtain instead through the required AI agentic backbone of §§8.2–8.6. Second, prosperity and production security can be evidenced only if they are measured, and national accounting, input–output analysis, material-flow accounting, and life-cycle assessment provide methods for doing so. Third, Ostrom’s work on commons and Polanyi’s analysis of embeddedness show how institutions that govern shared natural assets and keep land, labour, and credit within social bounds can be designed and tested. Fourth, the theories of human need and capability of Max-Neef, Doyal and Gough, and Sen can sharpen the family’s assessment of need and guard it against both adaptive preference and paternalism. Fifth, an account of money as credit created through lending, together with the experience of mutual-credit clearing networks such as the Swiss WIR (BoE 2014; Stodder 2009), lets the darshan’s critique of symbolic value and its design for the exchange-reserve address present financial institutions directly. Sixth, input–output analysis, the debate over labour-time calculation, and classical rent theory show how the labour embodied in complex goods, capital depreciation, and differential natural fertility can be computed and separated (§§4.2, 4.10, 8.5).

          9.7 Open problems

          Several questions remain unsettled.

          • Need at scale. How does family-level assessment of need aggregate across a district or a nation, and how are disagreements between families’ assessments resolved without either imposing a standard or abandoning definiteness?
          • Labour valuation, depreciation, and differential rent. How should common values of labour be set and revised when skills, tools, capital depreciation, and natural endowments of soil, water, and minerals differ widely across regions, and how far can computed labour values for goods that pass through many hands and places be relied on (§§4.2, 4.10, 8.5)?
          • Coordination and long-term investment without prices or interest. What replaces the information carried by prices and the intertemporal rationing performed by interest rates in the third stage of the transition, and how effectively can a federated ten-tier AI agentic backbone supply that coordination across the Earth without creating a technocratic bottleneck (§§8.2–8.3, 8.6)?
          • Automation and participation. How can AI and other machines reduce drudgery and the time production takes without displacing people from production or concentrating control of the means (§8.7)?
          • Specialisation and the special aspirations. What division of labour is compatible with the darshan’s critique of specialisation, given that telephony, broadcasting, and transport require it?
          • Transition within a debt economy. How does an exchange-reserve working without interest coexist with banks that create money through interest-bearing loans, and what happens when the guarantor bank’s terms change?
          • Evidence. What observations in communities practising the darshan would show that needs become definite, that production exceeds need, that producers receive the value of their labour, and that material throughput falls? Systematic observations of this kind remain to be gathered.
          • Natural-science claims. Which of the treatise’s claims about the earth’s balance, beyond the general dependence of production on natural regeneration, can be tested, and how should those not supported by present earth science be treated?
          • Agent-mediated visibility and collective self-correction. How effectively does making factual inconsistencies and checkable causal logic visible to families, clusters, and councils through paired AI agents prompt collective self-correction when individuals in the process of realisation (anubhav-gami) act erroneously, and how can communities govern this required ten-tier agentic backbone without ceding to it the judgements of justice, dharma, and truth or allowing it to be redirected toward profit (§§8.1–8.4, 8.8)?

          10. Conclusion

          Human economies do not reach balance because the need they are asked to meet is not the need they can meet, and existing economic theories—mainstream and heterodox alike—cannot close that gap because they model the human actor without the conscious unit () and without realisation (). So long as the human being is treated as a body-bound organism evaluating by pleasure, bodily advantage, and private gain (priya, hit, labh) and experiencing work as a disutility, no rearrangement of market prices, mutual-credit ledgers, steady-state caps, moral exhortations, or labour-time algorithms can simultaneously halt demand rebound, produce uncoerced social agreement, sustain work and open invention without profit or wage fear, extend need-based sharing and unpaid public service beyond biological kin, prevent strategic hoarding in exchange, and eliminate intergenerational discounting. The body’s requirements are definite, whereas jeevan is a constitutionally complete sentient atom whose powers are inexhaustible (akshay) and continuously surging (uditodit). In delusion, jeevan forces that inexhaustible demand for continuity of happiness through four and a half sensory and comparative activities, reading every transient satisfaction as a shortage of objects and locking onto money as the one symbol that never decays.

          Arthshastra is a genuinely new departure in economic thought because it resolves these six deadlocks at their common root through the awakening of jeevan in the realisation (anubhav) of the coexistence of all-pervasive omnipresence (, vyapak) and nature as a whole at once. When realisation opens the higher five and a half activities of jeevan, continuity of happiness is fulfilled in understanding, relationship, and benevolence, relieving bodily consumption of a task that finite matter can never perform. Because every human being is jeevan with this ability to realise in the coexistence of satta (vyapak) and nature as a whole at once (trikalabadh ), evaluation by justice, , and truth provides an uncoerced basis for universal agreement, and need is assessed and shared without individual labour accounting within the ten-member family. Labour is experienced as the outward projection (paravartan) and inward strengthening (pratyavartan) of jeevan powers culminating in resolution (); production exceeds finite bodily need, and household abundance naturally gives rise to benevolence (upkar), sustaining education, health care, and ten-tier self-governance as unpaid benevolent service (upkar-pradhan seva); exchange between families and regions settles at the satiation point of equal fivefold labour value (vinimay ka tripti bindu); and because a realised human sees the prosperity of oneself and one’s family in the prosperity of the whole and the perpetuity of human civilization (prayojansheelta, AA, pp. 62–63, 72, 94, 142, 146), production stays within natural regeneration across generations.

          For an Earth-scale civilization whose members are still in the process of realisation (anubhav-gami), however, inner awakening and economic theory must be joined to a third structural requirement: a strong, federated ten-tier AI agentic backbone. In a single family or an isolated village, facts are visible to the naked eye, so neighbours immediately see an error and help correct it. Across a planetary civilization of ten nested tiers, passive databases and network connectivity alone cannot keep governance part-time, compute multi-stage labour values and ecological balances without prices or interest, or prevent individual errors and priya–hita–labha drift from hiding in opacity. Pairing an AI agent with each human unit—from the Personal AI Assistant and Family AI Agent to the cluster, committee, and council agents across all ten tiers—makes objective facts, material balances, and checkable causal logic continuously visible in common view to the councils and communities responsible for acting on them. Because no human collective wants to suffer injustice or see its shared order fail, visible facts and verifiable logic prevent councils from ignoring inconsistencies: even when an individual errs out of incomplete awakening, the group as a whole is equipped and obliged to correct itself through the treatise’s reformative procedures. Governed by the darshan’s boundary between law, regulation, and balance on one side and justice, dharma, and truth on the other, this agentic backbone never substitutes for jeevan awakening or usurps human judgement; rather, it provides the planetary nervous system of transparent facts and checkable logic through which an awakening humanity can govern a cyclical economy at the scale of the Earth.

          Appendix: Quick Glossary

          • Cyclical economics ( arthshastra): the right use, security, and prosperity of resources in the form of body, mind, and wealth, organised as a cycle that returns to its starting point with its purpose fulfilled. The working economy is the cyclical economy (avartansheel arthvyavastha).
          • Cyclicality (avartansheelta): complementarity and mutual sustenance, a movement that passes through complementary activity towards a goal and returns to its starting point.
          • Constitutionally complete atom and inexhaustible powers (gathan-poorna parmanu, akshay shakti–bal, uditodit): as a sentient unit whose constitutional formation, inexhaustible and continuously surging (uditodit) five powers and five strengths, and ability to realise () in the coexistence of all-pervasive omnipresence (, vyapak) and nature as a whole at once are shared by all human beings, and which remains continuous before and after the bodily journey (AA, pp. 47–51, 58, 60; MVD, p. 203).
          • Resources (artha): body, mind, and wealth together (where “mind” denotes the jeevan powers as a whole, distinct from the specific faculty ).
          • Wealth (dhan): produced objects carrying usefulness value and aesthetic value; not money.
          • Natural abundance (prakritik aishvarya): earth, air, water, forest, and minerals, the sites where labour is utilised.
          • Value (mulya): a unit’s participation in the orderliness of the whole; for produced things, their usefulness and aesthetic value.
          • Value of labour (shram mulya): the value of human labour, judged by the usefulness and aesthetic value it establishes; the basis on which produced objects are valued and exchanged. Valuing labour is the act of evaluating that usefulness and aesthetic value through the labour that established them.
          • Restfulness of labour (shram ka vishram): the culmination of jeevan’s effort in intellectual resolution (), happiness, and awakening, rather than the mere cessation of work.
          • Common aspirations and special aspirations (samanya akanksha, mahatvakanksha): food, shelter, and adornment (together with objects for health and education); telephony, broadcasting, and transport.
          • Production in excess of need (avashyakta se adhik utpadan): the condition of prosperity in every family.
          • Wise family (samajhdar parivar): a family endowed with understanding, in which two to four members may produce beyond the family’s whole necessity.
          • Prosperity (): the absence of the sentiment of lacking; production in excess of need, with its right use and security.
          • Social participation (samaj gati): the contribution and movement of a person and family towards society and orderliness; paired with the nourishment and protection of the body as the domain in which produced objects are useful.
          • Use, right use, and purposeful use (upyog, sadupyog, prayojansheelta): utilisation of body, mind, and wealth in the nourishment and protection of the body and social participation; in orderliness and undivided society; and in evidencing understanding from generation to generation.
          • Exchange-reserve (vinimay kosh): the village institution through which produced objects are exchanged without profit and loss.
          • Righteous wealth (svadhan): wealth received as remuneration of one’s own labour, as a gift, or as an award; its opposite is ill-gotten wealth, obtained through exploitation and hypocrisy.
          • Generosity (udarta): the happy offering of body, mind, and wealth, according to need, for the awakening, health, and prosperity of others.
          • Benevolence (upkar): help and inspiration for awakening and prosperity; the propensity that naturally arises once a family achieves resolution and prosperity, directing surplus production into right use in society rather than accumulation.
          • Service (seva): work complementary to production, distinguished into unpaid benevolence-focused service (upkar-pradhan seva) and paid remuneration-focused service (pratiphal-pradhan seva) (§3.8).
          • Three motives (, vitteshana, lokeshana): the humane propensities for progeny, wealth, and public welfare or reputation.
          • Three policies (dharm-neeti, arth-neeti, raj-neeti): moral policy (the right use of resources), economic policy (production beyond the family’s needs), and state policy (the protection of resources).
          • Accumulation (sangrah): gathering beyond use; hoarding where it exceeds need.
          • Profit-obsession (labhonmad): the pursuit of giving less and taking more without a satiation point.
          • Symbolic currency (prateek mudra): metal or paper currency standing for objects; its value is symbolic value.
          • Satiation point (tripti bindu): the point at which an activity is fulfilled; accumulation, comfort, and profit have none, whereas exchange on the value of labour has a natural satiation point (vinimay ka tripti bindu, AA, p. 76).
          • Autonomous human (swayatt manav): a person who trusts in oneself, respects excellence, is balanced in talent and personality, social in behaviour, and self-reliant in occupation.
          • Authority, identity, and independence (adhikar, swatva, swatantrata): the strength of understanding and realisation, evidencing that strength in living, and unconstrained participation in family and universal orderliness.
          • Balance (santulan): the definite, mutually sustaining condition that each order maintains in its existent state; for the human order it is evidenced as resolution, prosperity, fearlessness, and coexistence.
          • Law, regulation, and balance (niyam, niyantran, santulan): the orderliness evident in nature, which humans understand and maintain.
          • Justice, , and truth (, dharma, ): the human evaluations of relationship, of participation in universal orderliness, and of coexistence.

          The definition of cyclicality

          The treatise’s definition of cyclicality (AA, p. 15) is flagged as unclear in the translation workspace (U-011). Its central phrase is an unpunctuated compound of natural abundance, naturalness, and natural complementarity, and it is not certain what is “linked”: resources, the process, or the points of the cycle. §3.2 adopts the reading that a cycle starts from any point, passes through complementary activity in the sense of prosperity towards the fulfilment of a goal, and returns to its starting point. This reading is supported by the examples on the same page, by the identification of cyclicality with complementarity and mutual sustenance (AA, p. 42), and by the provision sequence (AA, p. 106). The study’s argument does not depend on choosing among the parsings.

          The provision cycle and replenishment

          The project’s introductory primer presents avartansheel as cyclical or replenishing production, attending to what is taken from nature and returned. The Jeevan Vidya lectures define the cycle as the renewal of human capacity through production, nourishment, and social participation (JV, p. 154). The treatise supports both and relates them. The provision cycle is the definition (AA, pp. 26, 106), and the use of natural assets in proportion to their production, without obstructing and while helping their production, is its condition (AA, pp. 114, 142, 146). §3.3 and §3.9 present them in that relation.

          Cyclicality and natural recurrence

          The Karm Darshan working translation renders sahajavritti and anukshan-vikshan- as “natural recurrence”. These terms concern the continuity of contemplation in jeevan, not economic or natural cyclicality, and are not used in this study.

          The synthesis on insatiability

          §2.3’s claim that insatiability arises from pursuing a continuous need of jeevan through transient means is assembled from connected passages: jeevan as a constitutionally complete sentient atom (gathan-poorna parmanu) whose powers and strengths are inexhaustible (akshay) and continuously surging (uditodit) across waking, dream, and deep sleep (AA, pp. 47, 50–51, 58, 60), the four and a half effective activities and the internal conflict of the rest (JV, pp. 72–73; AA, p. 59), the absence of universal agreement in body-rooted deliberation by pleasure, health, and profit versus the observer-invariant universal agreement of justice, , and truth in jeevan’s realisation () of the coexistence of all-pervasive omnipresence (, vyapak) and nature as a whole at once (trikalabadh , AA, pp. 18–20, 47, 51, 58–60; MVD, p. 203), the inexhaustibility of selection and taste (AA, pp. 17–18), the failure of both materialism and ascetic or postural spiritualism to produce inner contentment without awakening (AA, pp. 46–47, 61), the transience of sensory satisfaction (JV, pp. 162–163; AA, p. 48), the indefiniteness of needs pursued as indulgence (MVD, p. 128), and the regulation of taste by the higher faculties in awakening (AA, p. 95). No single passage states the whole claim, and its phrasing as a “category error” is this study’s.

          Pairings of use with justice, resolution, and authenticity

          The treatise pairs usefulness with justice, right use with resolution, and purposeful use with authenticity (AA, p. 116). A later passage describes usefulness as the exposition of the primacy of justice, dharma, and truth “in sequence” (AA, p. 118), which is flagged as unclear (U-129) and may have lost the other two terms of the triad. §3.5 follows the clearer passage.

          Specialisation

          The treatise criticises specialisation for losing sight of wholeness, citing pest control and fertiliser that damage the agriculture they were meant to serve (AA, pp. 70–71), and criticises specialists who demand much while producing little (JV, p. 153). It also endorses telephony, broadcasting, and transport as special aspirations and provides for large industries at the higher tiers of self-governance (AA, pp. 29–30, 115). The study reads the criticism as directed at specialisation severed from wholeness and from production, and records the tension in §9.1 and §9.7.

          Market rates in the transition

          In the first stage of exchange, the exchange-reserve values local produce at the nearby wholesale rate less transport (AA, pp. 136–137, 142). This is a transitional use of prices, not a departure from valuation by labour, which the treatise reaches in the third stage. §9.6 reads it also as evidence that prices carry information which a labour-based system will need to obtain in another way.

          Artificial intelligence and the multi-level agent architecture

          The primary texts do not discuss artificial intelligence. They speak of computers in the village exchange-reserve (AA, p. 138) and of a computer system linking committees from the village to the world so that information located anywhere is available to the village (AA, p. 132), and they state the general rule for trusting instruments (KD, p. 139), the criterion for technique (AA, pp. 107, 118; SB, p. 267), and the institutional procedures by which families, clusters, and village councils evaluate conduct and collectively correct individual mistakes (AA, pp. 128–130, 138–140, 146–149). §8 analyses why passive databases and network connectivity alone cannot satisfy the planetary-scale visibility, fault-tolerance during the process of realisation, staffing, calculation, and translation requirements of the treatise’s institutional design, and extends the placement of AI in the material order and the personal-assistant architecture developed in Artificial Intelligence and Coexistence and its technical note on a personal AI assistant into a required multi-level agentic backbone paired with individuals, families, the five committees, and all ten tiers of self-governance. Both the critique of passive database infrastructure and the multi-level agentic backbone are this study’s extension, governed by the darshan’s distinction between law, regulation, and balance and justice, dharma, and truth (MVD, p. 173; AA, pp. 18, 117).

          Terminology

          Santulan is rendered as balance throughout, following MVD’s account of each order as balanced in its existent state (MVD, p. 105). “Stability” was considered for the study’s question but would suggest mere persistence, whereas santulan names a mutually sustaining relation with the rest of nature. The English renderings of AA’s economic vocabulary follow the decisions approved for the translation and adopted project-wide in October 2026: cyclical economics and cyclical economy for avartansheel arthshastra and arthvyavastha, cyclicality for avartansheelta, value of labour for shram mulya, production in excess of need for avashyakta se adhik utpadan, satiation point for tripti bindu, exchange-reserve for vinimay kosh, social participation for samaj gati, wise family for samajhdar parivar, and food, shelter, and adornment for ahar, avas, alankar. Where the published JV translation uses “clothing” for alankar in a quotation, the quotation keeps its wording.
