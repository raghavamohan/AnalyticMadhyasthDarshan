          Spiritual Practice and Realization

          Author: AnalyticMadhyasthDarshan.org — a group of people studying philosophy. Source repository: raghavamohan/AnalyticMadhyasthDarshan.

          Edited on: September 27, 2026, 6:35 PM IST

          The question: What does Madhyasth Darshan mean by spirituality and realisation, what practice leads to realisation, and how would anyone know it had occurred? How does its answer compare with Advaita Vedanta’s path of knowledge, and with what modern philosophy and contemplative science have established about practice and religious experience?

          This study examines anubhav — realisation — and the practice Madhyasth Darshan holds leads to it. The darshan presents its account of these matters as anubhavatmak adhyatmvad, realisation-centred spiritualism (AVD, p. 2), and the account is compared here with Advaita Vedanta, whose path of knowledge is its nearest and most demanding rival, and with modern Western philosophy and contemplative science, which turn out to have a great deal to say about this subject and not what one might expect.

          The relevance of modern philosophy is worth stating in advance, because the natural assumption is that spiritual practice lies outside its range. It does not. Ancient philosophy was itself organised around spiritual exercises, as Hadot’s work established; the epistemology of religious experience is a developed field; the perennialist–constructivist dispute is exactly the question of whether contemplative states have content independent of the tradition that trains them; L. A. Paul’s account of transformative experience formalises the problem of appraising a state one has not undergone; and contemplative science has produced both suggestive findings and a serious methodological reckoning. Each bears directly on claims the darshan makes.

          The Epistemology of Coexistence §1.6 states the transmission and evidence conditions in outline and §§7.1–7.5 records the resulting evidential problems; The Ontology of Coexistence §1.14 sets out realisation in coexistence as an activity of ; and The Sixty-One Activity Pairs of Jeevan records the 122 activities of jeevan pair by pair. This study takes the practice itself as its subject: what is done, which activities of jeevan it makes effective and how, what is claimed to result, and what would count as knowing that it had.

          1. The Madhyasth Darshan Answer

          holds that realisation is the understanding of existence as coexistence — of every unit saturated in one omnipresent reality — reached by through study and the refinement of its own activities, and evidenced in behaviour, work, and the capacity to convey it. Its founder reports that the absorbed state he attained left his foundational questions unanswered: the event of knowing the unknown did not occur. The darshan therefore directs practice towards understanding that can be communicated and evidenced in living. What replaces the absorbed state is a positive account: spirituality as the understanding of the omnipresent, realisation as the awakening of all ten activities of jeevan and their evidence in 122 named conducts, and liberation as release from delusion.

          1.1 Spirituality as the understanding of the omnipresent

          The darshan uses the word adhyatma, spirit, for the omnipresent reality itself. Every unit in existence — atom, plant, animal, human — is saturated in one all-pervading, non-active reality, and the darshan’s name for its account of spirituality comes from that fact:

          “One of the names I have given to this omnipresent reality is ‘adhyatma’ (spirit). This scripture has been named as ‘Realisation Centred Spiritualism’ because all the units are seen to be submerged, soaked & surrounded in this omnipresent reality.” — AVD, p. 236

          The same reality is called Omnipotence (), Brahma, space, and knowledge. Knowledge itself is the omnipresent Omnipotence, referred to as space, and it is called paramatma for being subtler than atma (MVD, p. 35); omnipresence is what the word God refers to (MVD, p. 163). Humans have the opportunity to understand, study, and experiment with three aspects of existence — the spiritual, which is the omnipresent; the deific, which is the intellectual or sentient; and the metaphysical, which is insentient nature — and every man and woman has the right to realisation and experimentation in coexistence (MVD, p. 285). The spiritual accomplishment is named precisely: atma-bodh, the enlightenment of the self, is itself the spiritual accomplishment (MVD, p. 286), and upon definitive understanding, as turns towards atma, atma-bodh and Brahma-realisation — realisation in coexistence — come into effect together (MVD, pp. 286–287).

          Two consequences follow for practice. Spirituality is not a domain of experience set apart from ordinary life: the omnipresent is present in everything, so understanding it is understanding the whole of existence and oneself within it. And spiritual knowledge is not ineffable. The idealist scriptures and mystery-based contemplation hold knowledge to be unmanifest and ineffable; this darshan holds that knowledge is manifest, effable, and understandable through study, and that its evidence becomes accessible to all through behaviour (MVD, p. 4).

          The founder’s own questions set the terms of the account. How could the world be false if it arose from Brahma, the truth? How could Brahma be the cause of both bondage and liberation? And what counts as evidence — the word or its bearer, the credible saying or its originator, the scripture or its author (MVD, pp. 4–6)? The darshan’s answers to these three questions organise what follows: Brahma is truth and the world is perpetual (MVD, p. 13); bondage is delusion and liberation is its removal (§1.5); and the awakened human alone is the basis of evidence (SB, p. 5).

          1.2 What realisation is

          Realisation has a definite object and a definite location. It is realisation in the ultimate truth which is in the form of coexistence, distinguished from contemplation, the awareness of truth (MVD, p. 316). Its object is not something absent that practice must bring into being. Omnipotence in the form of omnipresence has been uniformly inherent in every entity since eternity, and its realisation is the goal of humans (MVD, p. 317); truth is present even before awakening (MVD, p. 303); and while union with any finite thing is attained and lost, no entity or activity is ever without the pre-existing union with the omnipresent, which can only be realised (MVD, p. 302). What study and practice develop is the capacity, ability, and receptivity for that realisation, which is the right of every human being (MVD, p. 302). Realisation is an activity of atma (MVD, pp. 204, 307), and its effect is transmitted to buddhi, , , and (MVD, p. 318).

          Its content is given as three kinds of knowledge — of existence as coexistence, of jeevan, and of humane conduct (MVD, pp. 13, 127, 300; KD, p. 30). Complete enlightenment is the enlightenment of these three, and it is called enlightenment of the self (MVD, p. 300): an awakened human has the awareness of their own being and of the being of the entire existence (MVD, p. 84). Awakening is correspondingly defined as knowing, believing, recognising, and fulfilling jeevan and existence as they are (AVD, p. 259). Realisation is thus neither a feeling nor a vision. It is the understanding, obtained through the natural progression of things, of their states and motions, their emergence and their results (MVD, p. 77).

          Each kind of knowledge has determinate content. To understand any existing thing is to understand its rup, gun, swabhav, and — form, property, essential nature, and innateness (MVD, p. 273). Rup is determined by shape, volume, and density; gun is the generative, degenerative, and mediative; swabhav differs across the four orders; and dharma is cumulative across them — existence in the material order, growth along with existence in the biological, the hope of living along with both in the animal, and happiness along with all three in the knowledge order (MVD, pp. 50–51). Form, property, and essential nature can be understood; dharma can only be realised (MVD, p. 273). Visualisation of all four, together with generative, degenerative, and mediative knowledge, is the innateness of chitta, where sakshatkar, direct recognition, takes place (MVD, pp. 126, 203–204).

          Human dharma is happiness; its continuity is fulfilled through resolved thought and participation in orderliness (MVD, pp. 51, 156, 174). Knowledge of humane conduct is specified by justice, dharma, and truth. Deciding justice and injustice, dharma and adharma, truth and untruth is the innateness of vritti (MVD, pp. 203–204), and the three are levels of realisation itself: justice is the manifestation of realisation in behaviour, dharma the manifestation of realisation in thought, and coexistence the realisation in atma that is the ultimate truth (MVD, p. 156). Correct evaluation occurs only through awakening; otherwise over-, under-, or mis-evaluation takes place (MVD, p. 203). Knowledge reaches conduct through law, regulation, and balance:

          “Knowledge itself is law; law itself is regulation; regulation itself is balance; balanced living itself is justice in humans; living within the universal orderliness with justice itself is dharma, i.e., resolution; realisation in coexistence itself is dharma and truth.” — MVD, p. 174

          The six of niyam, niyantran, santulan, , dharma, and — law, regulation, balance, justice, dharma, and truth — are evidenced in the fruition of realisation (MVD, p. 80). It follows that a person cannot have realised and still misjudge, or have realised and still live unregulated, and the darshan states the observable mark: absence of mistakes and crimes in behaviour characterises complete awakening, and it is observed in human interactions (MVD, p. 285). This is what makes the account testable in principle; §6.3 examines how much the test establishes.

          Realisation has an intrinsic and an expressed evidential character. It is named the ultimate evidence and is connected with understanding, resolution, work, behaviour, and awakened tradition (MVD, p. 12). Study is in, by, and for realisation in coexistence (MVD, p. 35), and the proof of comprehension includes the ability to convey it to others (JV, p. 26).

          Anubhav is one stage of four — sakshatkar, bodh, anubhav, praman — whose relations §1.11 sets out. Where this study says “realisation” without qualification it means anubhav; where the distinction between the stages matters, the Hindi terms are used.

          1.3 Samadhi and the unanswered questions

          The account of practice begins with a negative finding reported in the first person. Shri A. Nagraj records that he was assured by the scholars and sages of his time that the only way to know the unknown is samadhi, accepted this, went to Amarkantak, and practised from 1950 with maturity in the 1960s (MVD, p. 7). The practice was traditional. He followed the Agama-Tantra method of upasana, which seeks samadhi by visualising the deities in every part of the body (nyas), until one day he found himself free of their imagined proximity: the awareness of “I am” remained, there were no other thoughts, and there was no thought of achieving, having, or doing anything (JV, p. 12). His practice also followed the instructions available for nididhyasan, samadhi, control of the mind, and the method of observing (AVD, p. 72). He reports the result:

          “In 1970, I accepted that I had attained samadhi. In this state, my hopes, thoughts, and desires were silent, and it was clear that the event of ‘knowing the unknown’ did not occur.” — MVD, p. 7

          The state recurred for twelve to eighteen hours a day (MVD, p. 7). It answered none of his questions: he received no answers from understanding Vedanta and none after achieving samadhi (JV, p. 13), and he describes the attained thoughtless condition under the inherited names samadhi, nididhyasan, complete enlightenment, and nirvana without finding answers in it (AVD, p. 72). He also reports his difficulty presenting evidence of the attainment:

          “There was nothing to describe in the state of samadhi; how could I present its evidence?” — JV, p. 13

          The reported result has two aspects. In samadhi hope, thought, and desire fall silent — mun, vritti, chitta, and buddhi have no remaining motion in this account (AVD, p. 220) — while the foundational questions remain unresolved. The condition yields self-limited happiness (svantah sukh), free of fear, temptation, and the pain of lack (AVD, p. 220), and Nagraj counts himself among the very few who have reached it (AVD, p. 222). Yet he finds no understanding in that condition which answers his questions and can be presented as evidence. The silence and happiness of absorption therefore do not establish knowledge of existence. The positive evidential status of realisation rests on its understood content and its expression (§1.13).

          The darshan draws a practical conclusion as well. No programme of living arises in the thoughtless state, so the person’s usefulness becomes zero (AVD, p. 220); during the pursuit of samadhi the seeker cannot take part in family, society, or the system of living (AVD, p. 165); and the method of silence is completely against the natural manifestation of jeevan, whose activity never ceases (AVD, p. 74).

          1.4 Samyama, and an inverted ladder

          What Nagraj reports as productive is samyama, and he undertook it to test the samadhi he could not otherwise evidence (JV, p. 13). Patanjali’s definition — dharana, dhyana, and samadhi practised on one object — is cited and then departed from:

          “I could not agree with what was written about samyama. Hence, I devised my own design and method of samyama and subsequently carried it out.” — JV, p. 13

          The design he adopted inverts the received order:

          “Of my own accord, I accepted the sequence of samadhi, dhyan, and dharana as the process for samyama. Two years later, having accomplished samyama, I accepted the evidence of having attained samadhi.” — MVD, p. 7

          Its object is stated separately. In the thoughtless state, together with the acceptance of existence, all realities were seen to be ceaselessly immersed in space, and this prompted samyama on space itself; soon after, existence presented itself in its true form, as coexistence, and jeevan was seen within it (AVD, p. 72). Seeing, here, means understanding (JV, p. 13; AVD, p. 87). The reported outcome is realisation that all existence is in the form of coexistence, presented in literary form for study (MVD, p. 7) — a state in which the understanding can be imparted, together with sight of the jeevan atom and of the three stages of wanting to live, to be happy, and to evidence oneself (JV, pp. 13–14).

          Patanjali’s sequence ascends from holding to absorption. This one runs from absorption back through attention to dharana, a word the darshan elsewhere defines as innateness, that which cannot be separated from a thing (MVD, p. 43). The darshan presents samyama not as traditional authority but as a method its author devised because the received one did not satisfy him, and it does not recommend the route to others (§1.12). §6.1 takes up what the texts do and do not explain about the sequence.

          1.5 Mystery, delusion, and liberation

          The darshan names its target rahasya, mystery: failure to attain or communicate understanding, while reality itself remains knowable:

          “Not revealing understanding or not being able to do so, or not being able to attain it, is a mystery. The incapacity for this is ‘mysteriousness’.” — MVD, p. 273

          Mystery is also the incompleteness in understanding the absolute state and activity, and its eradication comes only through the holistic view of reality, which yields certitude (MVD, p. 209). Beneath mystery lies delusion, belief different from reality (MVD, p. 291), whose root is the assumption that the body is jeevan (MVD, pp. 80–81; AVD, p. 88). Delusion is at the root of all unhappiness (MVD, p. 318), and it shows as excess, deficiency, and omission in visualisation and as over-, under-, and mis-evaluation in judgement (MVD, p. 286; AVD, p. 55).

          The hope for liberation is found in all humans. The darshan distinguishes liberation from unhappiness and liberation from delusion, notes that all study, worship, experimentation, behaviour, vocation, practice, and dedication have been undertaken for these, and presents its own proposal as one for liberation from delusion (MVD, p. 149). It gives the old question of bondage and liberation a definite answer. What is bound, what binds, and what is freed is jeevan itself (AVD, pp. 139, 150). Bondage takes three forms: bondage of hope, directed at sensory gratification; bondage of thought, which takes one’s own thoughts to be superior; and bondage of desire, which takes the maximisation of physical creation to be superior (AVD, pp. 155, 167). To be free from the bondage of delusion is moksha, and this is the evidence of awakening (AVD, p. 84).

          The liberated condition is described in the traditions’ own vocabulary. The complete acceptance and realisation of the mortality of the body, the immortality and continuity of jeevan, and the laws of work and behaviour is seedless (nirbeej) thought; behaviour in accordance with it is seedless behaviour; and this is liberation from delusion (MVD, p. 81). The jeevan-mukta — one liberated while living, who has moved irreversibly out of animal consciousness and delusion — does not suffer from the recollection of the past or apprehension of the future and has no resistance with the present (MVD, p. 81), and nurturing and advancing human society is their essential nature (MVD, p. 157). The state of realisation is named kaivalya, absoluteness, in which there is liberation from suffering, resentment, fear, and the bondage of delusion (MVD, pp. 317–318). Kaivalya is evidenced as participation in the whole of orderliness together with expression, communication, and exposition; the positive form of liberation from bondage is awakening completeness (AVD, pp. 260–261). The darshan further holds that one who is evidence in that state has no need of further bodily journeys (AVD, p. 261).

          The goal of practice is the removal of delusion, from which the ending of suffering follows. Ego (ahankar) has a precise meaning here: buddhi without atma-bodh, expressed as conceit in chitta, stubbornness in vritti, and attachment and agitation in mun. Study, experimentation, and endeavour work to uproot attachment and agitation (MVD, p. 279). What changes is the deluded orientation of the faculties; the finite jeevan continues as the knower and agent. A deluded jeevan keeps yearning for a happiness the five senses cannot supply (MVD, p. 274), and the accomplishment of mental wellbeing or bliss is itself liberation from mystery (MVD, p. 301).

          1.6 Study as practice

          The positive method is study, and the darshan states it in the vocabulary of worship:

          “Study itself is worship. The authentic human is the teacher, in whose proximity study takes place.” — MVD, p. 150

          Through study one attains the authority of innateness and independence by coming into absolute-resonance and absolute-accordance with understanding (MVD, p. 150). Study is defined by what it discriminates: understanding the difference between the laws necessary and unnecessary for humaneness is study, and implementing the necessary ones at all levels is evidence and accomplishment (MVD, p. 161). The journey to realisation begins with methodical study, whose field is definitive understanding (avdharana) (AVD, p. 223). Dedication carries study to realisation: study for the human goal in the light of justice, dharma, and truth occurs through dedication, and dedication leads to realisation (MVD, p. 150).

          The learner listens to the teacher’s presentation (shravan), reasons through it (manan), and becomes accomplished in understanding its meaning (arth-bodh) (MVD, p. 257). The teacher’s fulfilment lies in imparting study from realisation with authenticity; the learner’s lies in this listening, reasoning, and understanding.

          Rakesh Gupta explains shravan and manan as overlapping activities. Shravan is listening with the intention of understanding meaning. Manan is ratiocination: drawing together different threads of listening into a coherent view, reaching logical conclusions about how reality is and, therefore, how one should live. It is accompanied by practice in living (RG). Listening, reasoning, and conduct thus work together as understanding develops.

          The faculty correspondences give a more specific account of affirmation through study. Acceptance in mun is named manan, qualitative deliberation in vritti is tulan, direct recognition is in chitta, and enlightenment in buddhi (MVD, p. 126). These functions support one another: the complete development of mun includes reflection upon the justice-based thoughts of vritti (MVD, p. 295). The ratiocination involved in the learner’s method draws these functions into the work of understanding; the acceptance-related use locates a particular function within it.

          Study proceeds by two means that correct each other, inquiry while studying and study while inquiring (AVD, p. 225). Instruction by sermon always carries a possibility of delusion, and a tradition’s commands remain sermons or information until they become studiable, that is, until their intent is understood in logic and behaviour (AVD, p. 225). Each person examines any presentation with their own imagination and freedom of action and accepts or rejects it on that basis (AVD, p. 226). The darshan’s formula for the relation between accepting and knowing is short: believe what is known; know what is believed (MVD, p. 12).

          Successful study also depends on the conditions in which it is taught. Responsibility rests jointly on teachers, guardians, teaching, and educational content and procedure, whose mutual support makes an effective education possible (MVD, p. 270). Study is therefore a shared undertaking as well as the learner’s activity of understanding.

          The older vocabulary of practice is kept and redefined around study. Sadhana is effort towards self-improvement, whose point of fulfilment is awakening (AVD, pp. 233–234); practice (abhyas) is carrying the awakening and prosperity so attained into behaviour and teaching as comprehensive resolution (AVD, p. 234); and the triad of aim, seeker, and means is reconstituted, with every human the seeker of awakening, awakening the aim, and the method of study the means (AVD, p. 224; MVD, p. 322). Experimentation is the endeavour, continued until it succeeds, to meet a human need by instilling usefulness and aesthetic value in natural abundance (MVD, p. 56). Humans have undertaken studies, experiments, explorations, and practices for realisation, reflected in humaneness and higher humaneness (MVD, p. 302), and the study and practice of the mediative activity, atma, as presented in this darshan, leads to integrality — justice, dharma, and truth-based behaviour, thought, and realisation together (MVD, p. 303).

          1.7 Upasana redefined

          The inherited sense of upasana is devotional practice towards deities, or ritual (MVD, p. 4; JV, p. 9). In this darshan its content is different altogether:

          “Upasana is an important part of human jeevan. It is the process of refining and transforming the fundamental tendencies, and this study also transforms sanskar and essential nature.” — KD, p. 30

          The fundamental tendencies are the antahkarana’s hope, thought, desire, and resoluteness (KD, p. 36), and upasana is itself a form of study: until one is complete in one’s own dharma, upasana continues in the form of study (KD, p. 38), and the attainments of the best upasana are the completeness of study (KD, p. 37). Its root sense is attaining togetherness through proper means, which requires diligence and practice (KD, p. 30). It is of three kinds: kutastha upasana, undertaken for complete realisation; rupastha upasana, undertaken to approach a form endowed with magnificence; and atmastha upasana, which is reflection itself and to which every human being is bound (KD, p. 31). The deities of the traditions are reinterpreted within the same scheme as bodiless jeevans — elemental, deific, and divine selves — ranked by awakening (KD, p. 31), and the upasana directed to each yields base learning, sub-learning, or learning accordingly (KD, p. 32).

          Upasana is education and orderliness in its own nature (KD, p. 42); its chief utility lies in immersion in universal values (KD, p. 35); at the root of all upasanas the aim is undivided society and universal orderliness (KD, p. 35); and upasanas exist only for the capacity for awakening, not for decline (KD, p. 36). Its success condition is stated: upasana must contain the aim, the seeker, and the means together, and their integrality brings success while their separation brings failure (KD, p. 38). Its disciplines include the classical restraints: truthfulness, non-violence, non-possession, and non-stealing are practised as restraint (KD, p. 39); gratitude is the beginning-to-end basis of restraint; and a mantra is any meaningful word whose meaning and nature are attained by consistently remembering that meaning (KD, p. 40). Upasana that takes a symbol for its goal is judged secondary and unsocial to the extent of its incompleteness, because in it the human goal is neither clarified nor attained (KD, p. 42).

          The purpose and fulfilment of practice include living in relationships. Wisdom and jeevan-value — resolution and prosperity — are the direct results of upasana, and sociality is naturally contained in them; the dignity of human jeevan is the impetus to behave with one’s equal and to practise towards greater awakening, and this is real upasana (KD, p. 37). The samadhi route is the reverse case, in which the seeker cannot take part in family or society while practising (§1.3). Family Relationships and Values §1.4.2 draws the family-scale consequence: the practice begins in the relationships one is born into.

          1.8 Yog, dhyan, and the vocabulary of discipline

          Yog in this darshan is union, of two kinds, unification and togetherness (MVD, p. 42). It names how units come together rather than a technique of posture or breath, but it is not only a term of physical ontology. Mediative behaviour, thought, and realisation constitute pre-existing yog, continuously available and ever present, while the development and decline of a unit depend on the unions it enters, development-oriented or decline-oriented (MVD, p. 159). Realisation happens only of pre-existing yog — the omnipresent reality from which no entity is ever separate — while proximity and accumulation belong only to attainable yog (MVD, p. 302). Yog is therefore necessary for realisation (MVD, p. 302); every human is awakened only through yog (MVD, p. 304); and the realisation of the self is attained through yog in the tradition, meaning the union of body and jeevan and union with awakened humans for awakening (MVD, p. 301). This is why humans seek association with those more awakened than themselves (MVD, p. 274). A reader carrying the modern sense of “yoga” into these passages will find no postures and no breath-control. What they will find is an account of the company and orientation through which awakening happens.

          Dhyan, concentration, focuses the faculties on understanding and on expressing what is understood. Nagraj describes the inherited method he pursued as limiting thought to one point towards its cessation (AVD, pp. 164, 219). The darshan gives its own positive account of attention:

          “Concentration involves focusing mun, vritti, chitta, and buddhi for understanding, and focusing them for the purpose of evidence after understanding and realisation.” — MVD, p. 284

          The object of concentration is the comprehension of meaning and its evidence, and all humans possess the capacity for concentration (MVD, pp. 284–285). Concentration in this sense belongs to the inward regulation (antarniyaman) through which atma-bodh happens, and atma-bodh is the highest accomplishment of concentration (MVD, p. 286).

          The ascetic vocabulary is reworked the same way. Dispassion is inclination towards the ideal without inclination elsewhere, and a chain of identities joins it to non-attachment, non-doership, non-accumulation, fearlessness and prosperity, heavenliness, humility, supreme detachment (par-vairagya), yog, realisation in omnipresence, mediation, authenticity, and liberation from delusion (MVD, p. 157). Supreme detachment is liberation from the flaws of excess, deficiency, and omission that stand against awakening (MVD, pp. 157–158). Non-accumulation means resolution and prosperity rather than poverty, and heaven becomes the heavenliness of just living (MVD, p. 160). Initiation (diksha) is accepting a definite method of behaviour and conduct as an unbroken pledge (MVD, p. 309). The ideal (ishta) of devotional practice becomes self-driven reverence, trust, and dedication towards coexistence together with complete knowledge (MVD, p. 80). And samyama has, besides the founder’s method of §1.4, the ordinary sense of restraint: being regulated in humane thought, behaviour, and vocation (MVD, p. 337).

          Devotion belongs to this vocabulary too. Bhakti is the activity of becoming liberated from fear and delusion, the expression, communication, and exposition of prayer joined with seva, and an expression of total dedication (MVD, p. 339), where dedication is the continuous endeavour of attaining and evidencing complete awakening (MVD, p. 340). The expression, communication, and exposition of values with evidence, maintained in human tradition upon awakening, is love and devotion (MVD, p. 307). Devotedness (anurag) and love (prem) towards the ideal in the form of coexistence are among the mandatory conditions of realisation (MVD, p. 317; §1.12). The darshan’s practice vocabulary is therefore wider than study, experiment, and practice: it keeps upasana, sadhana, abhyas, dhyan, yog, samyama, bhakti, diksha, and vairagya, and gives each a content defined by understanding and conduct. Gupta’s explanation of nididhyasan places the unfolding of cognition, understanding, resolve, and realisation within this course of practice (§1.11). What it rejects is set out in §1.15.

          1.9 The ten activities of jeevan and the 122 conducts

          Jeevan is a constitutionally complete atom whose nucleus is atma and whose four orbits are buddhi, chitta, vritti, and mun (MVD, p. 78; AVD, p. 87). These five are its inexhaustible strengths (bal), and hope, thought, desire, resoluteness (ritambhara), and authenticity () are its inexhaustible powers (shakti) (MVD, pp. 75, 84; AVD, p. 151). Each faculty has two activities, one of reflection and one of projection: realisation and authenticity in atma, enlightenment and resolve in buddhi, contemplation and visualisation in chitta, deliberation and analysis in vritti, and taste and selection in mun (JV, pp. 73, 92–93). Every jeevan continuously operates through these ten activities, whether in delusion or in awakening (JV, p. 73). Constitutional completeness supplies the enduring capacities; awakening concerns their effectiveness through understanding (JV, pp. 73–74).

          In delusion only four and a half of them are effective. Selection and taste in mun, analysis in vritti, visualisation in chitta, and half of deliberation — deliberation confined to what is pleasant, healthy, and profitable — are sufficient for the five senses and the four instincts, and what they produce does not become the content of contemplation (JV, pp. 73–74; AVD, p. 152; MVD, p. 79). They come into play in that order from infancy (JV, p. 94). Awakening the remaining five and a half, which happens through understanding, is what a humane mentality requires, and awakening is the evidence of all ten in behaviour (JV, p. 74). Upon awakening, jeevan evidences all ten through the realisation-based way (MVD, p. 79).

          The ten are the generic activities. In awakening their detailed content becomes evident as 122 activities:

          “Mun, vritti, chitta, buddhi and atma are integral in jeevan. In awakened jeevan, its faculties or activities that become evident in the tradition in the form of strength & power or state & motion are as follows: atma has two faculties, buddhi has four, chitta has sixteen, vritti has thirty-six, and mun has sixty-four. Therefore, 122 activities are found in human jeevan in the form of strength & power, that is, in state & motion. Each person can demonstrate all of the above activities.” — MVD, p. 323

          The 122 are tabulated as the conducts of awakened jeevan, sixty-one pairs of a reflective and a projective activity, and each is defined (AVD, pp. 91–94; MVD, pp. 328–348). They include most of what the contemplative and devotional traditions treat as spiritual attainments. In atma the pair is realisation in existence and the authenticity of ultimate bliss. In buddhi the pairs are bliss and definiteness, and existence and fearlessness. In chitta they include realised articulation (shruti) and memory (smriti), contentment and abundance, love and non-otherness, and reverence (shraddha) and devoutness (pujyata). In vritti they include learning and intelligence, peace and kindness, grace and compassion, uprightness and forgiveness, gratitude and humility, truth and dharma, justice and sensitivity, absolute-oneness and intrepidity, and restraint and law. In mun they include devotion and immersion, care and generosity, respect and cordiality, and affection and dedication (AVD, pp. 91–94; MVD, pp. 323–340). Bliss, love, reverence, devotion, forgiveness, gratitude, restraint, and absolute-oneness have defined content in the activity of awakened jeevan, and many are directed to other people. Realised articulation, for example, gives language to the understanding of reality and communicates it (MVD, pp. 324, 329).

          This is the point at which the account of practice becomes specific. Practice develops understanding and makes effective activities that jeevan already has. The fulfilled condition is itself called a state of realisation, kaivalya or tadatmya (MVD, pp. 317–318). Knowledge of jeevan is its constitution together with the inspection, examination, and survey of its activities (AVD, pp. 86–87), which makes one’s own activities a field of study. The systematic study of the awakened conducts is the subject of a separate treatise, Manav Sanchetnavadi Manovigyan (AVD, p. 151).

          1.10 How the activities are activated and refined

          The defect practice addresses is located with unusual precision. Under delusion, hope in mun is based on sensation, thought in vritti follows that hope, desire in chitta follows that thought, and buddhi, turned away from atma, supports deluded visualisation and desire (MVD, pp. 204, 275). In awakening the direction reverses: hope in mun accords with vritti, thought in vritti with chitta, desire in chitta with the enlightenment of buddhi, and the enlightenment of buddhi with atma’s realisation in existence; the resulting reflection and projection form the awakened jeevan cycle (MVD, p. 275). Practice reorients these relations through understanding. A circuit is a useful analogy for their mutual influence, while the change concerns the effective expression of a constitutionally complete jeevan (JV, pp. 73–74; The Epistemology of Coexistence §1.4).

          The realisation-oriented way (anubhav-gami vidhi) regulates hope in accordance with thought, directs thought towards fulfilling desire, and governs desire by the resolve towards realisation in atma. In the realisation-based way (anubhav-moolak vidhi), accomplished realisation is expressed through enlightenment, contemplation, deliberation, and the taste of values (MVD, pp. 276–277). The former describes practice directed towards fulfilment; the latter describes expression from accomplished understanding.

          Awakening begins with the strength of contemplation, whereas the bondage of delusion runs on the powers of hope, thought, and desire (AVD, p. 151). Study supplies contemplation with its content. As definitive understanding grows, the perspectives of justice, dharma, and truth become active in the deliberation of vritti; their direct recognition follows in chitta, enlightenment and acceptance in buddhi, and their realisation in atma, confirmed in contemplation (AVD, pp. 86, 223–224; MVD, p. 126). Each faculty reaches its complete development by receiving the signal of the one within it. Mun does so by acquiring the receptivity for just behaviour on accepting the signals of vritti; vritti by accepting the signals of chitta, that is, by the ability to form dharma-based thought; chitta by the capacity for creative visualisation according to the signals of buddhi in the presence of truth; buddhi by accepting the signals of atma; and atma in realisation in coexistence (MVD, pp. 322–323). These relations have their marks. When mun receives the signal of vritti, just behaviour and affection follow; when vritti receives that of chitta, peace; when chitta receives that of buddhi, contentment and self-governed living; and when buddhi receives that of atma, enlightenment of the self, atma-bodh, and resolve in truth (MVD, pp. 282–283). Concurrence of mun and vritti is happiness, of vritti and chitta peace, of chitta and buddhi contentment, and of buddhi and atma bliss; the eternal concurrence of atma with the omnipresent is ultimate bliss (MVD, p. 327).

          The marks have definite content in living. Peace (shanti) is behaviour based on resolved thought; contentment (santosh) is the absence of the sentiment of lacking; and bliss (anand) is complete enlightenment of mediative activity, or atma-bodh (MVD, pp. 323–325). Their significance lies in established understanding and its continuity in conduct. A passing pleasant feeling alone does not establish these attainments.

          The conditions of these relations are named as well. The reflection of hope succeeds in the absence of crime, of thought in the absence of injustice, of desire in the absence of attachment, and of resolve in the absence of ignorance. Each requires its support: the influence of orderliness for behaviour without crime, of social conduct for thought without injustice, of study and for desire without attachment, and inward regulation or concentration for a buddhi without ignorance. Only then does reflection succeed; otherwise it fails (MVD, p. 284). The channelling of jeevan’s energies towards awakening runs through curiosity in mun, enthusiasm in vritti, delight in chitta, elation and immersion in buddhi, and realisation in atma, and it requires the inward regulation of those energies (MVD, p. 77).

          Refinement is the work of upasana (§1.7), and its object is definite: the fundamental tendencies of hope, thought, desire, and resoluteness, which are refined when they are no longer wasted. The awakening of activity-power is the right use, rather than the waste, of the powers exercised through the senses; the awakening of desire-power is the right use of the fundamental tendencies; and the awakening of knowledge-power is right enlightenment together with complete realisation. This awakening of the three powers is the supreme attainment of human jeevan and the purpose of upasana (KD, p. 36). What refinement removes is delusion’s excess, deficiency, and omission in visualisation and its over-, under-, and mis-evaluation (MVD, p. 286; AVD, p. 55), and the level of awakening a person has reached is judged by their attachment or non-attachment to the body’s instincts and the objects of the senses (MVD, p. 203).

          Once awakened, realisation flows outward through the same faculties. Its enlightenment is in buddhi; its awareness (prateeti) arises where buddhi meets chitta; its comprehension (abhas) where chitta meets vritti; and its perception (bhas) where vritti meets mun, showing as the taste of values and the selection of relationships (MVD, p. 99). Just behaviour is the perception of truth, resolved thought its comprehension, and contemplation its awareness (MVD, p. 316). This outward course is how the 122 conducts become evident in relationships (§1.9), and it is why awakening is evidenced through the combined activity of all five faculties (MVD, p. 203).

          1.11 Recognition, understanding, realisation, and evidence

          Study leads from direct recognition (sakshatkar) to definitive understanding (bodh) and realisation (anubhav), expressed as evidence (praman). Nagraj describes realisation attained after direct recognition and definitive understanding, with contemplation following from that enlightenment (JV, p. 62). Their principal faculty correspondences are direct recognition in chitta, enlightenment in buddhi, and realisation in atma (MVD, pp. 126, 307). Complete enlightenment in buddhi coincides with realisation in atma, and atma-bodh and realisation in coexistence become effective together (MVD, pp. 207, 286–287). The account combines a developmental direction with the integrated fulfilment of jeevan’s faculties.

          Gupta names the unfolding into established understanding and realisation nididhyasan: direct cognition (sakshatkar), conception in buddhi and resolve, and thereafter realisation in atma. Its fulfilment is establishment in the understanding (samajh) and realisation (anubhav) of coexistence, instilled in jeevan as an indelible impression (sanskar) (RG). This gives the developmental account a definite meaning in practice: what is listened to and reasoned through becomes directly cognised, understood, resolved, and realised. The direction towards fulfilment includes the concurrence of complete bodh and anubhav described above.

          Formation develops through study, practice in behaviour, and practice in work (MVD, p. 187). Realisation-oriented conceptions in buddhi become expressed in thought and action; reflection on action returns through inference into conception. Realisation-based enlightenment is the completeness of sanskar (MVD, p. 218). The account thus connects establishment in understanding with the practice through which a learner’s conceptions develop.

          The relation between the stages is defined through definitive understanding:

          “Knowing, believing & recognising itself is definitive understanding. Attaining the point of satisfaction in these itself is realisation. Expressing it in actions & behaviour, and in systems, is authenticity.” — AVD, p. 224

          Realisation is the point at which definitive understanding reaches satisfaction, and authenticity is its expression in conduct and orderliness. The connected account gives realisation ultimate evidential standing: evidence is understanding, understanding becomes manifest as resolution, work, and behaviour, and evidence continues as awakened tradition (MVD, p. 12). The working transcript of a recorded session presents sakshatkar–bodh–anubhav–praman as a four-stage sequence, describes the latter stages as following successful sakshatkar (03:03–03:30), and calls praman the power to bring about bodh in another (04:43). It places the weight on sakshatkar as the success of study (12:54). The distinct evidential limits of this oral source are recorded in the Editorial Notes.

          The fourth stage is not a report issued after the fact. Evidence is awakened tradition (MVD, p. 12), and only awakened humans are recognisable in the seer status (MVD, p. 33). In the same session anubhav is given the second name drashtapad, the seat of the seer, and praman the name jagriti, awakening (13:09, 17:30, 21:13).

          Establishment in the seer status is also the printed definition of tadavalokan, continuous observance of the ideal, and that definition belongs to the darshan’s reinterpretation of devotional union. In the knowledge order, pursuit of the ideal (ishta-sevan) aims at oneness with it, accordance with it, abiding near it, and continuous observance of it; the ideal is self-driven reverence, trust, and dedication towards coexistence together with complete knowledge; and the way to that accordance is realisation itself (MVD, p. 80):

          “Realisation in coexistence itself is the way of absolute-accordance and absolute-resonance (tadakar), because law, regulation, balance, justice, dharma, and truth become evident as a result of realisation.” — MVD, p. 80

          The prefix tat- in these terms indicates awakening (MVD, p. 80), and awakening is the human ideal (MVD, pp. 306–307). The vocabulary of becoming one with a chosen deity is kept, its object becomes awakening in coexistence, and the means of accordance becomes study: through study one comes into absolute-resonance and absolute-accordance with understanding (MVD, p. 150), just as the inner faculties, brought into absolute-resonance with an aim, become intense in that aim (KD, p. 40).

          1.12 Two paths, preconditions, and the teacher

          For a person who is not the discoverer there are two alternatives:

          “Such a process is of two kinds: One - Exploration. Two - Following, Emulation, Study.” — MVD, p. 280

          Exploration is an inventive realisation which the seeker studies comprehensively, from the extreme of decline to the highest development and awakening. It is the route of a seeker accomplished in samadhi, who establishes themself in just behaviour and dharma-based thought through practice and then develops the capacity for deific and divine humaneness and for realisation of the omnipresent (MVD, pp. 280–281). The second path has two programmes: following or emulating just behaviour, and being and abiding in resolved, dharma-based thought. Firmness and dedication in these two give the authority for realisation of truth (MVD, p. 282).

          The darshan also settles which path is for whom. It asks why its author, having reached awakening by the traditional methods, does not recommend them, and replies that its own method is practicable. The silence method yields only self-limited happiness, no one can be assured of success within a lifetime, and silence is against the natural manifestation of jeevan (AVD, pp. 72–74). The two ways it offers are exploration, and inquiry together with study (AVD, p. 225). Study with inquiry is thus the practicable route offered to learners; exploration remains a named alternative rather than a prerequisite for each learner.

          The conditions of realisation are demanding. Realisation in knowledge requires the capacity, ability, and receptivity for delusion-free knowledge, non-otherness, devotedness, and love towards the ideal in the form of coexistence, and these are achieved through just behaviour, resolved thought, and full dedication in obedience to a realised human being (MVD, p. 317). Delusion-free knowledge is knowing, understanding, and imparting the understanding of realities as they are; non-otherness is its continuity; devotedness is the continuity of non-otherness, an earnest endeavour for freedom from delusion; and love is the continuity of non-otherness together with devotedness, the combined expression of kindness, grace, and compassion (MVD, p. 317). Dharma, unlike form, property, and essential nature, can be realised only through study and adherence to the step-by-step instructions of one who has attained realisation (MVD, p. 273).

          The teacher’s role is correspondingly large. The authentic human is the teacher in whose proximity study takes place (MVD, p. 150); in study imparted by the guru through the realisation-based way, justice, dharma, and truth are accepted in the student’s buddhi upon direct recognition (MVD, p. 99); awakened humans are the natural guides of the unawakened, and humans aspire to awaken through their own inspiration and through the inspiration, grace, and compassion of those more awakened (MVD, p. 287); and the teacher is the bearer and carrier of the evidence of awakening, with the literature as its guideline (AVD, p. 261). Alongside these statements stand the principles of §1.6: each person examines any presentation and accepts or rejects it (AVD, p. 226), and commands remain information until they become studiable (AVD, p. 225). The Epistemology of Coexistence §1.6 records the transmission requirements in outline, and §6.4 examines how the two sides of the teacher’s role fit together.

          1.13 How realisation is evidenced

          The darshan names realisation, behaviour, and experiment as evidence and calls realisation itself the ultimate evidence (MVD, p. 12). This status belongs to accomplished understanding; a person’s report of an inner experience must be assessed through the content understood and its expression in living. The awakened human, not scripture or instrument, is the basis of evidence (SB, p. 5). Understanding must be conveyable: “The proof of our comprehension lies in our ability to convey it to others” (JV, p. 26), and making just behaviour, resolved thought, and realisation in truth understandable, knowable, and practicable through language is the work of realised humans (MVD, p. 316). Conduct must change: integrality is justice, dharma, and truth-based behaviour, thought, and realisation together (MVD, p. 303); the absence of mistakes and crimes in behaviour characterises complete awakening (MVD, p. 285); and the ability to provide justice is the evidence of liberation from bondage (AVD, pp. 172–173). Work with nature must succeed, since experimentation is the endeavour continued until natural abundance is made useful (MVD, p. 56). And the change must be reproducible in tradition: an awakened human evidences awakening by paving the path for the deluded (MVD, p. 273), and awakening completeness is evidenced in participation in the whole of orderliness together with expression, communication, and exposition (AVD, pp. 259–260).

          The darshan grades what these marks show. Humans are perceived as animalistic, demonic, humane, deific, and divine according to their awakening (MVD, p. 314). Those who have realised the truth and live by justice and dharma are awakened, those whose thought and behaviour accord with justice and dharma are half-developed, and those who accord with justice alone are partially developed (MVD, p. 160). The darshan also states the criterion that is meant to make these marks evidence of truth rather than of competence alone: truth is what is uniformly realised everywhere (MVD, p. 35), and the authenticity of realisation is the same in, by, and for all human beings (AVD, p. 61).

          These marks express accomplished understanding and can also be used to assess a claim to attainment. The four-stage formulation presents praman as the fulfilment of the same process (§1.11); §6.3 considers what its observable marks establish.

          1.14 The circle the darshan states itself

          The dependencies of realisation are set out without concealing that they close:

          “The ability of realisation in knowledge depends on human awakening; human awakening depends on sanskars; development of sanskars depends on environment, study & endeavour; environment, study & endeavour depend on humaneness and higher humaneness; humaneness and higher humaneness depend on the ability of realisation in knowledge.” — MVD, p. 290

          These dependencies concern the ability for realisation in knowledge, together with awakening, formation, study, and conduct. They describe mutually supporting conditions for development. The account includes development before complete awakening: resolve can govern desire, thought, and hope before atma-bodh, while full awakening places their activity in the regulation of atma (MVD, pp. 278–279). Learning can begin through study and participation before complete awakening: the outward expressed values can be learnt before their counterparts are established in state (Axiology Value Theory §§1.4, 1.8), and belief can follow knowing and knowing can follow belief (MVD, p. 12). The question of how a learner evaluates the teaching and progresses towards establishment is examined separately in §6.2.

          1.15 What the darshan rejects

          The account is defined as much by exclusion as by prescription, but what it excludes are paths and their assumptions, not the vocabulary of practice. Mystery-based idealistic contemplation failed to accomplish the study of the human being, as did materialism-centred ideology (MVD, p. 3). In place of devotion–renunciation, isolation, accumulation, comfort-seeking, and hedonism, the darshan presents coexistence and, within orderliness, resolution, prosperity, and fearlessness (SB, p. 5). Renunciation, non-accumulation, detachment, and devotion, pursued as ideals, have drawn immense courage and dedication and produced no undivided society (JV, p. 105), and the character of renunciation and of devotion-ism is itself individualistic (SB, p. 238). Idealism takes ideal persons as its evidence, with devotion and renunciation as the criterion for recognising them (AVD, p. 62). Faith, defined as accepting the existence of something without knowing it (AVD, p. 17), rests at its root on the expectation that wishes shaped by fear and temptation will be fulfilled (SB, p. 238), and adoration pleads with an omnipotent unknown to turn inability into ability and remains individual-centred (AVD, p. 233). Seeking refuge in God as the real father and mother yields no human (JV, p. 57). Instruction by sermon risks delusion (AVD, p. 225), upasana that takes the symbol for the goal is secondary and unsocial (KD, p. 42), and the absorbed state he attained left his foundational questions unanswered (§1.3).

          What survives these exclusions is not a bare minimum. Devotion, reverence, dispassion, restraint, concentration, and union are kept and redefined by understanding and conduct (§1.8), and most of them reappear among the conducts of awakened jeevan (§1.9). The path that remains is study of a stated system, the refinement of tendency, the activation of all ten activities, and evidence in relationship and conduct. It removes the traditional shortcuts, including the one the founder was told was the only way.

          1.16 Method, evidence, and what Madhyasth Darshan establishes

          Separating the kinds of claim matters here more than in most of these studies, because the founder’s report does evidential work throughout.

          Some claims are first-person historical report: that samadhi was attained without the event of knowing the unknown; that samyama was redesigned, directed at space, and produced realisation of coexistence (MVD, pp. 4, 7; JV, pp. 12–14; AVD, p. 72). These are testimony about how the system arose — evidence of the reported route, without independently establishing either the ontology realised or its reproducibility.

          Some are definitional within the system: spirituality as understanding of the omnipresent (AVD, p. 236); realisation as realisation in coexistence and as the satisfaction of definitive understanding (MVD, p. 316; AVD, p. 224); mystery as failure to attain or communicate understanding (MVD, p. 273); upasana as refinement of the fundamental tendencies (KD, p. 30); concentration as focusing the faculties for understanding and evidence (MVD, p. 284); bhakti as liberation from fear and delusion expressed in dedication (MVD, p. 339); yog as union (MVD, p. 42).

          Some are structural claims about jeevan: ten activities, four and a half effective in delusion, and 122 conducts in awakening (JV, p. 73; MVD, pp. 79, 323). They depend on the darshan’s account of the jeevan atom, examined in The Ontology of Coexistence and The Epistemology of Coexistence.

          Some are methodological prescriptions that could be followed and reported on: the two paths and their programmes (MVD, pp. 280–282), the conditions and supports of reflection (MVD, p. 284), study together with inquiry (AVD, p. 225), and experimentation continued to success (MVD, p. 56).

          A further epistemic argument can be drawn from the report: attaining absorption does not by itself establish the truth of an account of existence. The lack of an answer in one practitioner’s experience supports that distinction; it does not establish that every absorbed state lacks awareness or that first-person experience can never have evidential value.

          The account of the learner’s method also draws on a translator’s explanation supplied for this study: Rakesh Gupta’s interpretation of overlapping shravan and manan, practice in living, and nididhyasan as the unfolding into established understanding and realisation (§§1.6, 1.11; RG). It is attributed separately from the printed primary passages and the recorded session.

          A further class enters with §§1.11 and 1.12: recorded oral teaching from a 2010 session at Amarkantak. It connects with the printed account of realisation as the satisfaction of definitive understanding and of study as the practicable route, and it states some things not located in the printed texts reviewed here, such as the names drashtapad and jagriti for the third and fourth stages and the automatic succession of stages after sakshatkar. Its stronger wording requires separate verification; a related printed definition does not verify an oral sentence. The Editorial Notes set out how this material is used.

          2. The Advaita Vedanta Answer

          Advaita Vedanta understands realisation as immediate knowledge that one’s own self is , the nondual reality. Liberation is the freedom disclosed by that knowledge; practice does not manufacture a free self. Yet preparation, teaching, reasoning, contemplation, and establishment in knowledge all have work to do for the learner. Shankara’s account joins these activities while distinguishing their functions. The question for comparison is therefore how practice overcomes ignorance and becomes effective in living, as well as what reality is understood.

          2.1 Moksha as eternally accomplished

          The ordinary sense of being a limited embodied agent involves adhyasa, the superimposition of self and not-self: the body’s and mind’s properties are attributed to the self, while consciousness is attributed to what is observed. Avidya, ignorance, sustains this misidentification. Realisation removes it through knowledge of the self’s identity with Brahman (BSB, Adhyasa Bhashya; 1.1.4). Brahman is known as the self through which objects are known, rather than encountered as one more object. The finite person does not enlarge into an infinite entity or merge two previously separate realities.

          Moksha, liberation, is consequently eternally accomplished. An effect produced by action would be conditioned and liable to end; knowledge instead removes the misconception that the self is bound. Shankara distinguishes an action, which depends on what a person chooses to do, from knowledge, which must answer to the reality known. Repetition of an assertion or deliberate imagining of oneself as Brahman cannot substitute for understanding (BSB 1.1.4; 4.1.2, printed p. 818). The change lies in the learner’s ignorance and understanding, while the self’s freedom is unchanged.

          This knowledge is immediate in the sense that it concerns oneself, rather than an absent object or a conclusion accepted only on another’s assurance. Shankara describes inquiry into Brahman as culminating in experience, and accepts reasoning consistent with the Upanishadic teaching as part of that inquiry (BSB 1.1.2, printed pp. 15–17). He also distinguishes learned knowledge from personal realisation and describes establishment in knowledge (BG 6.8; 18.55, commentary). Here experience must be understood through the self-knowledge being claimed: its immediacy does not make a passing feeling, mental silence, or unusual perception sufficient evidence of liberation.

          The self’s nonduality also determines the status of the world. names the world’s dependent appearance, whose claim to independent reality is overturned by knowledge. Ordinary experience and its means of knowledge retain validity within empirical dealings before knowledge of nonduality; the distinction does not license dismissing practical truth and error indiscriminately (BSB 2.1.14). likewise holds that the reality to be realised is already present, but retains a finite saturated in the omnipresent and a perpetual world (MVD, pp. 13, 210, 302–303, 317). The difference concerns the knower and the world themselves, beyond agreement that realisation does not create existence.

          2.2 Preparation through conduct, action, and devotion

          Advaita makes the learner’s preparedness part of the method. The sadhana-chatushtaya, fourfold qualification, comprises discrimination between eternal and non-eternal, dispassion towards the enjoyment of results here and hereafter, the disciplines beginning with tranquillity and self-restraint, and desire for liberation (BSB 1.1.1, printed p. 9). The six supporting disciplines are tranquillity, control of the senses, withdrawal, endurance, trust in the teaching and teacher, and collectedness of mind in Brahman (VC 19–26). These develop the ability to inquire without having inquiry repeatedly displaced by desire, distraction, or resistance.

          Karma-yoga gives action a preparatory role. The seeker carries out appropriate duties without attachment to their fruits, cultivating equanimity and purity of mind. Shankara addresses this prescription to the person for whom knowledge has not yet arisen (BG 2.47–48; 3.19, commentary). Vedic study, sacrifice, charity, austerity, and especially calmness and self-control can assist the emergence of knowledge; once knowledge has arisen, liberation does not await an additional action to complete it (BSB 3.4.25–27). Preparation may therefore involve substantial work in ordinary life while liberation itself remains unproduced.

          Devotion and meditation also enter this preparation. A graded account moves from fixing the mind on God, through repeated practice when steadiness fails, to action dedicated to God and relinquishment of its fruits (BG 12.8–11). Such disciplines differ in what they ask the learner to do, and their results must be distinguished from immediate self-knowledge. Upasana, sustained meditation on a prescribed object or conception, is an intentional activity; knowledge answers to what is real (BSB 1.1.4). Devotion is nevertheless also described in its highest form through knowledge and establishment in Brahman (BG 18.54–55). Reducing Advaita practice to intellectual study would omit these relationships.

          Preparation includes non-injury, truthfulness, freedom from anger, self-restraint, and compassion (BG 16.1–3). Qualification is not merely a test administered before a single teaching encounter: teaching can reveal obstacles which call for further discipline, reflection, and contemplation (§2.3). Shankara also gives renunciation and freedom from competing pursuits a substantial place in steadfastness in Brahman (BSB 3.4.17–20). His path cannot be equated with a programme requiring every realised person to sustain household and civic roles. Madhyasth Darshan’s conditions for realisation and its universal claim to human capacity (§§1.12–1.13) should therefore be compared with the content and accessibility of these disciplines, rather than with a rigid division between Advaita’s preparation and its subsequent teaching.

          2.3 Hearing, reflection, and sustained contemplation

          Shravana is attentive reception and understanding of the Upanishadic teaching through a teacher. Manana examines its meaning through reasoning, resolving doubts and apparent contradictions. Nididhyasana is sustained contemplation through which the mind remains with the understood truth. Shankara joins hearing from teacher and scripture, reasoning, and steadfast meditation in the realisation of the self (BU 2.4.5, commentary, printed pp. 355–356). Respectful approach and service accompany questioning a teacher who knows the truth; questions concern bondage, liberation, knowledge, and ignorance (BG 4.34, commentary). A learner thus studies what the teaching means, examines objections to it, and returns to it where habitual identification with body and mind continues to dominate.

          Sruti, the Upanishadic revelation, functions as a pramana, a means of knowledge, for the identity of self and Brahman. Reasoning serves the inquiry into that teaching’s truth and meaning; it is more than memorisation or assent, although independent speculation does not replace the teaching as its source. Shankara explicitly allows reasoning that accords with the Upanishads because the inquiry concerns an existing reality and culminates in realisation (BSB 1.1.2). The identity taught in tat tvam asi, you are that, directs attention to the self beyond the limitations attributed to the individual; neti neti, not this, not this, removes limiting determinations rather than establishing a blank nothing (CU 6.8.7, 6.16.3; BU 2.3.6 and commentary).

          These activities may require repetition. Shankara allows immediate understanding in a suitably prepared learner, but directs repeated hearing, reflection, and contemplation where misunderstanding persists. The teacher must continue to explain the meaning rather than divert the pupil into bare repetition (BSB 4.1.1–2, printed pp. 812–818). Reflection and contemplation participate in the arising of immediate knowledge (BSB 1.1.4, printed p. 43); meditativeness is specifically prescribed where dualistic ideas continue to predominate (BSB 3.4.47). This gives the triad an intelligible practical aim: overcome the particular impediment to understanding and establishment, without treating the words themselves as an automatic cause of release.

          Establishment in knowledge, jnana-nishtha, involves the continuance or maturation of understanding together with the disciplines that sustain it. Knowing Brahman and entering Brahman are not two separate achievements, as though a further event had to make the knowledge liberating (BG 18.55, commentary). The distinction between learning a formulation and realising its truth is thus essential, but a universal scheme of merely intellectual knowledge followed by a separate liberating experience would misstate the account. The place of contemplation is understood through the learner’s actual condition (§2.4).

          Gupta’s explanation gives the shared vocabulary a substantive Madhyasth meaning: overlapping shravan and manan develop coherent understanding through listening, ratiocination, and practice in living; nididhyasan unfolds through direct cognition, conception and resolve, and realisation into establishment as (§§1.6, 1.11; RG). Both accounts concern becoming established in what is understood. The Advaita triad, however, cannot be mapped directly onto the Madhyasth faculties or its sequence of attainments: its defining content is the identity of self and Brahman, whereas Gupta describes understanding and realisation of coexistence in jeevan.

          2.4 Knowledge, contemplation, and samadhi

          Shankara gives meditation a positive role in immediate self-knowledge. In his account of meditative yoga, the concentrated and purified mind directly realises the self, with a happiness beyond sensory enjoyment (BG 6.20–23, commentary). His objection to an independently sufficient Yoga philosophy concerns its metaphysical claims and a liberation detached from nondual knowledge; he accepts compatible disciplines (BSB 2.1.3, printed pp. 305–307). The governing distinction is between contemplation informed by knowledge of the self and a state whose mere occurrence is supposed to remove ignorance.

          Turiya, called the fourth in relation to waking, dream, and deep sleep, is the nondual self present throughout those conditions. Shankara explains its recognition through removal of superimposed characteristics; it is not a newly produced fourth episode to be entered and left (MU 7 and commentary, printed pp. 34–39). Deep sleep’s absence of differentiated objects does not make it liberation, because non-apprehension of reality remains there (MU, Karika 1.12–15). A temporary cessation of differentiation cannot by itself establish knowledge of nonduality.

          Gaudapada nevertheless prescribes sustained contemplative discipline. Withdrawal through discriminating knowledge is distinguished from the mind’s latency in sleep; the learner must correct distraction, rouse the mind when it sinks into sleep, recognise its latent attachments, and avoid clinging even to contemplative bliss (MU, Karika 3.32–35, 3.39–46 and Shankara’s commentary, printed pp. 138–153). Such practice aims at lucid establishment in nondual reality. Treating all stillness as ignorance would be as inaccurate as treating all stillness as realisation.

          The Vivekachudamani gives nirvikalpa-samadhi, absorption without conceptual differentiation, especially strong importance in definite realisation and the removal of ingrained impressions (VC 353–365). It also makes teacher-guided discrimination and knowledge of the identity of self and Brahman indispensable, distinguishing them from work that purifies the mind (VC 11–15, 56). Its discussion of lingering impressions and their attenuation after truth has been realised emphasises sustained establishment (VC 267–276). These formulations deserve comparison with Shankara’s accounts of knowledge and steadfastness; they cannot be reduced to an opposition between an intellectual Shankara and a meditative tradition (§5.2; Editorial Notes).

          Nagraj’s report concerns the practice he actually pursued: he groups its nididhyasan with samadhi as thoughtlessness and reports that his questions remained unanswered (AVD, p. 72). That finding does not straightforwardly refute the knowledge-directed contemplation just described. Gupta’s positive Madhyasth explanation also gives nididhyasan the content of understood and realised coexistence (RG). The comparison therefore concerns what becomes known and established through contemplation, rather than whether either tradition simply accepts or rejects meditation.

          2.5 Liberation while living and conduct after realisation

          Liberation while living, jivanmukti, is possible because freedom concerns knowledge of the self rather than the immediate disappearance of the body. Knowledge removes the attribution of agency to the self; karma whose result has already begun accounts for continued embodiment until its course is exhausted (BSB 4.1.13–15). The body’s persistence therefore does not disprove liberation. Shankara appeals to the knower’s own realisation in making this distinction (BSB 4.1.15, printed p. 840).

          The injunction to act without attachment has to be read with this change in standpoint. Shankara exempts the realised knower from obligatory action grounded in a need to attain something, while prescribing action for the purification of the seeker (BG 3.17–19, commentary). He gives two readings of Janaka and similar figures: their action either prepared them for knowledge, or continued after knowledge, under the momentum of prior action and for loka-sangraha, the holding-together of the world (BG 3.20). An enlightened person can teach through action without unsettling those still attached to it (BG 3.25–26). This supports exemplary engagement while leaving room for renunciation; it does not impose one social occupation on every liberated person.

          Conduct remains integral to preparation and to the description of established wisdom. Freedom from possessiveness, craving, fear, and anger accompanies steadiness; the knower’s characteristics also guide the seeker’s practice (BG 2.54–72, especially the commentary on 2.55). Recognising that others seek happiness and avoid pain as one does oneself gives a basis for non-injury, while friendliness, compassion, and concern for the welfare of beings are explicitly valued (BG 6.32; 12.4, 12.13). The Vivekachudamani similarly describes alertness, non-possessiveness, and equanimity in the liberated person (VC 421–434). Non-agency should not be mistaken for indifference to harm.

          Madhyasth Darshan uses the name jeevan-mukta for one freed from delusion while living, and makes nurturing and advancing society their essential nature (MVD, pp. 81, 157). Its account of kaivalya joins evidenced participation in orderliness with no further bodily journeys (AVD, p. 261). Both traditions connect liberation and conduct, but assign a different place to continuing social participation and to the enduring identity of the realised individual.

          2.6 Method, evidence, and what Advaita establishes

          Advaita offers a connected account of practice: action and discipline prepare the learner; teacher-guided hearing and reasoning ascertain the teaching’s meaning; contemplation and repeated inquiry address obstacles to realisation and establishment. These functions may overlap in a learner’s development. Their fulfilment is immediate self-knowledge, with liberation intrinsic to it. Scripture supplies the teaching, reasoning examines it, and the claimed realisation concerns the self which is already present (BSB 1.1.2–4; 4.1.1–3). First-person immediacy is therefore part of an epistemic account, rather than a licence to treat conviction alone as knowledge.

          The characteristics of wisdom give others grounds for assessing conduct and the suitability of teaching, although they do not make another person’s knowledge directly observable. Shankara treats knowledge as transformative: unchanged identification with the body and its bondage would conflict with the realisation asserted (BSB 1.1.4, printed pp. 39–43). Compassion, restraint, and equanimity accordingly matter; they also do not uniquely establish the truth of the nondual ontology. The passages reviewed offer disciplines, reasons, and recognisable characteristics without specifying a public certification procedure or making universal social order a condition of liberation. Comparison must examine that particular evidential structure alongside the Madhyasth criteria, rather than opposing private experience to a philosophy with conduct.

          3. Modern Western Philosophy and Contemplative Science

          The subject is not outside modern philosophy’s range; it has been worked on from several directions, and the results are unusually relevant to the claims in §1.

          3.1 Philosophy as spiritual exercise

          Hadot’s Philosophy as a Way of Life argued that ancient philosophy was not primarily a body of doctrine but a set of spiritual exercises — attention, meditation on death, examination of conscience, memorisation of precepts — whose aim was the transformation of the practitioner. Doctrine served practice rather than the reverse. Foucault’s late lectures on the care of the self and askesis develop the same material toward techniques of self-formation.

          This displaces the assumption that “spiritual practice” names something religious and other than philosophy. It also supplies the closest Western structural analogue to the darshan’s position: study whose point is transformation, doctrine that is not idle, exercises that are not withdrawal from the world. Hadot’s ancients, like this darshan, treat the philosophical life as continuous with conduct in the city rather than as preparation for leaving it.

          3.2 Religious and mystical experience: perennialism against constructivism

          James’s Varieties of Religious Experience established the field by treating such experiences as data with describable marks — ineffability, noetic quality, transiency, passivity — and by taking their fruits as the test of their worth. Stace’s Mysticism and Philosophy argued for a common core across traditions, distinguishing extrovertive from introvertive mysticism and treating the latter as a contentless unity underlying diverse interpretations. Katz’s “Language, Epistemology, and Mysticism” reversed this: there are no unmediated experiences, and a tradition’s concepts shape the experience itself, not merely its report. Forman and others replied by defending a pure consciousness event stripped of content (SEP Mysticism).

          The dispute bears directly on the darshan’s negative finding. Nagraj reports a state in which hope, thought, and desire were silent, the awareness of “I am” remained, his foundational questions remained unanswered, and he found nothing to present as evidence (MVD, p. 7; JV, pp. 12–13). That is close to what perennialists describe as a pure consciousness event, while a constructivist can ask how his Vedic and tantric training shaped both the experience and its interpretation. That the same report can be read as evidence for either position shows how little a phenomenological description settles.

          3.3 The epistemology of religious experience

          Alston’s Perceiving God is the most developed defence of the idea that experience of God can be a source of justified belief, arguing that “mystical perceptual practice” is not shown unreliable merely because it lacks the cross-checking available to sense perception, and that demanding such checks begs the question. The standard objections are the absence of independent verification, the diversity of incompatible reports across traditions, and the difficulty of specifying reliability conditions (SEP Religious Experience).

          sits at an odd angle to this literature. It shares the demand to explain what a contemplative attainment establishes: Nagraj’s report distinguishes the happiness and silence of absorption from answers to his questions. But it then claims realisation with statable content and offers conveyability, conduct, and experiment as the cross-checks Alston is accused of lacking. Whether those checks are the right kind is §4.4’s question.

          3.4 Transformative experience

          Paul’s Transformative Experience formalises a problem the contemplative traditions have always faced without naming it. Some experiences are epistemically transformative: they provide knowledge of what an experience is like that was unavailable beforehand. They can also be personally transformative, changing preferences and values. Choosing whether to undergo such an experience challenges ordinary expected-value reasoning because relevant knowledge, and sometimes the values used to assess outcomes, emerge through the experience itself.

          This is precisely the structure of a call to practice. The darshan’s demand that understanding be conveyable before it is accepted is an attempt to escape it — to make the case in terms available to the unawakened. Paul’s analysis suggests why that is hard: if realisation changes what one values and how one understands experience, prior assessment may lack relevant knowledge while retrospective assessment reflects changed commitments. That asymmetry complicates appraisal without making every prior reason or later judgment worthless. Advaita’s qualification requirement (§2.2) approaches preparedness through disciplines that shape the learner’s disposition towards inquiry.

          3.5 Contemplative science and its reckoning

          Empirical work on meditation has identified measurable differences and has also undergone a methodological correction. Electroencephalography identified differences in gamma activity between long-term practitioners and controls during meditation and at baseline (Lutz et al. 2004). Such group differences alone do not isolate the causal contribution of training. Van Dam and colleagues’ “Mind the Hype” documented problems including inconsistent definitions, weak controls, small samples, and publication bias. Lindahl and colleagues catalogued difficult and sometimes destabilising experiences among Western Buddhist practitioners; their purposive sampling identifies kinds of difficulty rather than estimating prevalence. Thompson challenges the philosophical conclusions sometimes drawn from contemplative neuroscience (Thompson 2020).

          The distinction relevant here is between evidence of change and evidence for the content of a resulting belief. Measures of attention, affect, or self-report do not by themselves establish that an ontology is true. Empirical inquiry can nevertheless test specified consequences of a claim, including accuracy, behaviour, and successful transmission. None of the studies cited here directly evaluates Madhyasth Darshan’s course of study or establishes its account of realisation.

          3.6 Method, evidence, and what modern work establishes

          Modern philosophy establishes that transformation-oriented practice belongs to philosophy’s own history rather than outside it; that no description of a contemplative state settles whether its content is given or constructed; that the demand for cross-checking is reasonable but not obviously decisive against experiential justification; and that appraising transformative experience from outside is structurally difficult. Contemplative science establishes that practice has measurable effects, that the field’s early claims outran its methods, and that adverse effects are real. None of it establishes what realisation is or whether anyone has attained it.

          4.3 The status of the absorbed state

          Nagraj’s report that his foundational questions remained unanswered in samadhi does not refute Shankara’s account of knowledge-directed contemplation. Both distinguish attainment of a state from understanding reality. Advaita’s distinction between sleep, mental dissolution, contemplative pleasure, and lucid self-knowledge gives this agreement specific content (§2.4). also names accomplished realisation a state, kaivalya (§1.5); the word state alone does not identify the disputed claim. What matters is whether an attainment supplies understanding, and what that understanding establishes.

          The disagreement concerns the reality known and the means and fulfilment of knowledge. Advaita joins Upanishadic teaching, reasoning, contemplation, and establishment in the identity of self and . Madhyasth Darshan joins study, definitive understanding, its satisfaction in realisation, and evidence in conveyability, conduct, and experiment. Gupta’s account of nididhyasan names this unfolding towards establishment in coexistence (RG). Nagraj’s retrospective description of the inherited nididhyasan as thoughtlessness (AVD, p. 72) should therefore remain a report of that practice, rather than a definition imposed on either positive account. The two traditions share a concern with understanding through contemplation; their ontological conclusions and specified forms of fulfilment remain different.

          4.4 What could count as a check

          All three positions face the cross-checking problem and answer differently. Advaita gives first-person knowledge a role that is not made dependent on public ratification, while describing its characteristics in living (§2.6). Alston argues that demanding sense-perception-style checks of mystical practice begs the question. The darshan supplies observable expressions through which a claim to realisation can be checked, including more than conveyability: evidence in behaviour and in experiment with nature, a criterion of truth as what is uniformly realised by everyone, and the requirement that the reality each word names be found in existence (MVD, pp. 12, 35; AVD, pp. 61, 235).

          These proposed checks reach beyond coherence to just conduct, successful work with nature, and agreement among those who have studied. Their results bear less directly on the ontology conveyed — the atom, the saturation of every unit in space — because a learner examines each claim through the darshan’s categories. A corresponding limitation applies to Advaita: equanimity, compassion, and clarity of teaching are relevant evidence of a person’s dispositions, but do not uniquely establish self–Brahman identity. The comparison therefore concerns the scope and independence of each tradition’s checks. Madhyasth Darshan specifies a broader programme of publicly expressed competence and social fulfilment; demonstrating that this programme better verifies its ontology requires further argument and evidence (§6.3).

          4.5 Transformation and the appraisal problem

          Paul’s analysis (§3.4) raises a question for all three approaches: how can a learner assess a transformation before fully undergoing it? Advaita supplies preparatory disciplines, practice through action and devotion, reasoned inquiry, and characteristics of steadiness which can guide the seeker (§§2.2–2.5). Hadot’s ancients likewise connect exercises with ordinary living. Madhyasth Darshan requires that understanding be conveyable, that each learner examine what is presented, and that belief follow knowing (MVD, p. 12; AVD, pp. 225–226). It also names marks of development in the learner’s faculties — affection, peace, contentment, bliss — with defined content in understanding and conduct (MVD, pp. 282–283, 323–325, 327). These offer ways to assess progress, although no tradition’s accessible preparatory stages automatically verify its final account of reality.

          The residual difficulty is that the darshan also asks for full dedication in obedience to a realised teacher (MVD, p. 317) and holds that correct evaluation occurs only through awakening (MVD, p. 203; Axiology Value Theory §1.7). Those two commitments reintroduce from the other side the asymmetry conveyability was meant to remove.

          4.6 Practice as private or social

          Madhyasth Darshan makes relationship and social order explicit in the purpose, practice, and fulfilment of awakening. Real upasana is behaving with one’s equal and practising towards greater awakening, with sociality naturally contained in its results (KD, p. 37); upasana is education and orderliness in its own nature (KD, p. 42). Many awakened conducts — kindness, grace, compassion, forgiveness, respect, affection, generosity — concern others (§1.9). This permits solitary reflection while requiring understanding to become effective in social living. Nagraj contrasts it with the pursuit of samadhi he describes as preventing participation in family or society (AVD, p. 165).

          Advaita also gives practice relational content. Non-injury, empathy, compassion, and the welfare of beings are substantial commitments, and loka-sangraha gives enlightened action an exemplary social purpose (§2.5). At the same time, Shankara preserves renunciation and exempts the knower from obligatory action undertaken for personal attainment. The difference concerns whether a specified programme of continuing social participation constitutes liberation’s fulfilment, rather than whether concern for others is present at all. Hadot supplies a further comparison through philosophy lived in the city and school, while the empirical studies cited here chiefly assess individual practitioners and outcomes.

          5. Critical Review

          5.1 Madhyasth Darshan — a demanding standard, resting on one report

          Strengths.

          • The distinction in §1.3 is transferable: the attainment of absorption does not by itself establish that the practitioner’s questions have been answered or their ontology verified.
          • It is made from the inside, by a practitioner who reached the state he then declined to credit — which insulates it from the objection that critics of contemplative claims have not done the practice.
          • The refusal of ineffability is disciplined and consistent. Knowledge is held to be manifest and effable and its evidence accessible through behaviour, so the tradition produces statable doctrine open to examination.
          • The account of what practice changes is specific. The ten activities, the four and a half that are effective in delusion, and the 122 conducts of awakened give practice definite targets and definite marks, faculty by faculty. Gupta’s explanation further connects the learner’s listening and reasoning with practice in living and the unfolding into established understanding and realisation (RG).
          • Failure has stated conditions. Reflection fails in the presence of crime, injustice, attachment, or ignorance, and each has a named support; upasana fails when aim, seeker, and means come apart (MVD, p. 284; KD, p. 38).
          • Practice includes conduct in relationships as part of its fulfilment, so social obligation belongs within its account of awakening.
          • The traditional vocabulary of devotion and discipline is kept and given a public content rather than discarded, which lets the darshan meet those traditions on their own terms.
          • The dependency circle is stated in the darshan’s own words (MVD, p. 290) rather than concealed.

          Weaknesses.

          • The founding exploration is available through one practitioner’s first-person report, including his design of the productive method (JV, p. 13). That historical episode cannot be independently repeated as the same event; the stated claims and proposed course of study remain open to examination.
          • The public marks test competence, conduct, and successful work more directly than they test the ontology conveyed (§§4.4, 6.3).
          • The texts pull in two directions on the teacher: dedicated obedience and adherence to instruction on one side, each learner’s examination and acceptance or rejection on the other (§6.4).
          • The inverted samadhi–dhyan–dharana sequence and its object, space, are reported, not argued. Why realisation should follow from them is not explained.
          • The printed passages and Gupta’s explanation specify the content and direction of practice, but leave its duration and the assessment of incomplete attainment less specified (§6.6). The treatise devoted to the systematic study of the 122 conducts is not yet available in translation.

          joins demanding criteria for understanding and conduct to a founding exploration known through testimony; assessing the transmission of its understanding is therefore central.

          5.2 Advaita Vedanta — knowledge and the characteristics of wisdom

          Strengths.

          • The argument that an eternally free self cannot be produced by action challenges accounts that make liberation a newly created condition.
          • Distinguishing sleep, dissolution, contemplative pleasure, and knowledge resists the inference from an unusual state to liberation, while retaining a positive role for meditation.
          • The qualification structure, karma-yoga, devotion, and repeated inquiry specify work for the learner rather than assuming realisation is available on demand.
          • Shravana–manana–nididhyasana connects teaching, reasoning, and contemplation with immediate self-knowledge and establishment. It gives realisation both understood content and implications for living.
          • Non-injury, compassion, and exemplary conduct prevent the account from being reduced to an isolated person’s claim about a private attainment.

          Weaknesses.

          • First-person knowledge and described characteristics do not by themselves provide an outside observer with a decisive procedure for distinguishing realisation from confident self-ascription.
          • The unique authority assigned to sruti needs defence for a reader who does not already accept it. Reasoning and an appeal to immediate self-knowledge make the position more than an appeal to authority, but do not by themselves settle disagreement over its nondual interpretation.
          • The relative work of teaching, contemplation, and stable establishment requires careful specification. Shankara’s statements about knowledge’s immediate efficacy and the strong contemplative prescriptions of the Vivekachudamani cannot be turned into a single mandatory timetable for every learner. The sources reviewed leave room for different accounts of obstacles and their removal; a complete treatment of subsequent Advaita schools lies beyond this study.

          Advaita gives a developed argument for liberation through knowledge of an already free self, while leaving the public adjudication of another person’s attainment less specified than the disciplines and characteristics of the knower.

          5.3 Modern philosophy and science — clear questions, differing accounts of the goal

          Strengths.

          • Hadot recovered transformation-oriented practice as philosophy’s own history, which reframes the whole subject.
          • The perennialist–constructivist dispute isolates exactly the question that first-person reports cannot settle.
          • Alston forces critics to state why cross-checking should be required rather than assuming it.
          • Paul’s analysis explains why transformative practice can be difficult to appraise from present knowledge and values.
          • Contemplative science has corrected itself in public — “Mind the Hype” and the adverse-effects literature are the field policing its own claims.

          Weaknesses.

          • No convergence on whether contemplative states can justify belief, and each position’s answer follows from prior commitments.
          • The cited measurements of change do not by themselves establish the truth of contemplative claims; that requires tests directed at the particular content claimed.
          • The literature is overwhelmingly built on Buddhist-derived and theistic practices, so its categories fit a darshan that rejects absorption as a goal and rebuilds devotion around understanding rather poorly.

          Modern philosophy and science clarify the problems of interpretation, justification, transformation, and measurement, without supplying a settled account of realisation shared across these approaches.

          6. Open problems for Madhyasth Darshan

          6.1 The inverted sequence

          The samadhi–dhyan–dharana order is given as the process for samyama (MVD, p. 7), undertaken to test samadhi (JV, p. 13) and directed at space (AVD, p. 72), without any statement of why that order on that object should work. Three readings are possible: that the absorbed state quiets the sensory circuit so directed attention can operate without interference; that the descent is incidental and the work was done by sustained attention regardless of order; or that the sequence records one person’s route without general significance. The darshan’s own vocabulary suggests a fourth. If dhyan is concentration for understanding (MVD, p. 284) and dharana is innateness (MVD, p. 43), the sequence would run from the silence of the faculties, through concentration for understanding, to the apprehension of each thing’s innateness in the space that saturates it. That reading is not stated in the texts reviewed, and none of the four is argued there.

          6.2 Entry into the circle

          The dependency passage concerns the ability for realisation in knowledge, awakening, , environment, study, and humaneness (MVD, p. 290). Following, emulation, and study provide a stated path (MVD, pp. 280–282), outward expressions of values can be learnt before establishment in state, and belief may precede knowing provided knowing follows (MVD, p. 12). These supply an entry into learning, and the printed texts recommend study with inquiry as the practicable route without requiring every learner to repeat the founder’s exploration (AVD, pp. 72–74, 225).

          The remaining question concerns warrant and transmission. The system studied originates in the founder’s exploration, and the exploratory route is not recommended to others. A public account should therefore show how learners can examine the teaching, identify an error in its presentation or application, and verify their progress through understanding and conduct. The texts supply the criterion that each person examine and accept or reject what is presented (AVD, p. 226), but they do not describe a procedure by which a community of learners corrects a teacher’s presentation.

          6.3 What the evidential marks establish

          The darshan’s evidential marks are richer than conveyability: realisation, behaviour, and experiment (MVD, p. 12); the absence of mistakes and crimes in behaviour (MVD, p. 285); the ability to provide justice (AVD, pp. 172–173); truth as what is uniformly realised by everyone (MVD, p. 35; AVD, p. 61); and the requirement that the reality each word names be found in existence (AVD, p. 235). Each tests something real. Conveyability tests coherence and pedagogical competence, conduct tests whether the understanding governs living, and experiment tests whether it succeeds in work with nature.

          The difficulty lies in what they are meant to confirm. Praman is the fourth stage of the same sequence as sakshatkar (§1.11), so the marks are presented as consequences of realisation. They also provide observers with grounds for assessing a claim to it. Conduct and successful work are compatible with an understanding whose ontology is mistaken, since people live justly and produce well on many metaphysics. Finding each named reality in existence presupposes that existence is presented in the darshan’s categories. And agreement among learners trained in one system is not independent confirmation of that system, as the constructivist argument of §3.2 would predict. What would settle the question is a check on the content of sakshatkar that does not already rely on the understanding sakshatkar is supposed to deliver. The automaticity claimed in §1.11 concerns successful sakshatkar, so it does not imply that a mistaken self-ascription would produce its fruits. The diagnostic question is how a learner or observer distinguishes successful recognition from confident fluency and partial changes in conduct before the full criteria are met.

          The darshan’s strongest reply is the invariance criterion. If realisation is the same in all who reach it and is of what is uniformly present everywhere, divergent realisations would expose error. That reply needs a public record of independent realisers whose accounts can be compared, and the texts reviewed do not provide one.

          6.4 The teacher and the learner’s own examination

          The texts pull in two directions. On one side, realisation requires full dedication in obedience to a realised human being (MVD, p. 317), and can be realised only through study and adherence to the step-by-step instructions of one who has attained realisation (MVD, p. 273). On the other, each person examines any presentation and accepts or rejects it (AVD, p. 226); a tradition’s commands remain information until they become studiable in logic and behaviour (AVD, p. 225); instruction by sermon risks delusion (AVD, p. 225); and belief is to follow knowing (MVD, p. 12). These are consistent if the teacher’s instructions govern the conduct of study while their content is established only by the learner’s own examination and conduct. The texts do not say this in so many words, and the practical difficulty remains that a student cannot certify a teacher as realised without the understanding the teacher is supposed to impart.

          Nagraj’s recorded remarks point the same way. He describes his own presentation as proceeding by goodwill and friendship, with no dadagiri — no bullying — the bullying in question being the older way of saying “you will ask nothing” (26:50). He treats self-examination as the test of teaching, asking whether one says, does, and lives the same thing (31:15–32:48), and of his critical method he says that he reviewed ideas and did not go about reviewing persons (41:14). Read provisionally, these remarks favour examination rather than coercion. The goodwill passage still requires an audio check, so it does not independently resolve the relationship between the printed requirements of dedication and examination.

          6.5 How failure is diagnosed

          The texts state the conditions under which the practice fails. Reflection fails when crime, injustice, attachment, or ignorance is present and its corresponding support — orderliness, social conduct, study and sanskar, inward regulation — is absent (MVD, p. 284); upasana fails when aim, seeker, and means are separated (KD, p. 38); and humans decline, while wanting awakening, for lack of understanding of the reflection and projection among their own faculties (MVD, p. 291). What is not stated is how these conditions are to be read from outside in a particular learner, how long a successful course of study takes, or what a practitioner should conclude from years of study without sakshatkar.

          The provisional claim of automatic succession in the recorded session sharpens the gap. If bodh, anubhav, and praman follow once sakshatkar succeeds (03:03), a failure downstream calls into question either the diagnosis of successful sakshatkar or the claimed automatic succession. The printed conditions of reflection identify possible failures in the relations between faculties. The account also places responsibility for successful study on teachers, guardians, teaching, and educational content and procedure together (MVD, p. 270). Failure therefore calls for examination of the learning conditions as well as the learner. The material reviewed does not supply a documented series of unsuccessful courses of study and their diagnoses against which the proposed conditions could be tested.

          6.6 The detail of practice

          For a study of spiritual practice the corpus contains more procedural content than a first reading suggests: the two programmes of the second path (MVD, p. 282), the four supports of reflection (MVD, p. 284), concentration for understanding (MVD, p. 284), the marks of each faculty’s development (MVD, pp. 282–283, 322–323, 327), the refinement of the fundamental tendencies through upasana and restraint (KD, pp. 30–40), and the inventory of 122 conducts (§1.9). Its central instruction is study, including listening, reflection, and understanding meaning (MVD, p. 257), and the recorded session names sakshatkar as study succeeding (12:54). Focused contemplative attention also has an explicit place: dhyan is concentration for understanding meaning and evidencing it (MVD, pp. 284–285).

          Gupta’s explanation adds a connected account of the learner’s work: listening with the intention to understand, ratiocination that joins different threads into a coherent view and conclusions for living, and nididhyasan as the unfolding into direct cognition, conception and resolve, realisation, and indelible establishment as sanskar (RG). It supplies a developmental direction without separating listening and reasoning into non-overlapping periods.

          Together these sources identify learner activities, conditions, developmental relations, and responsibilities. What remains less specified is a curriculum with durations and independently applicable milestones: the depth of study needed by a particular learner, how partial progress towards establishment is assessed, and how unsuccessful teaching or study is corrected. The treatise the darshan names for the systematic study of the awakened conducts, Manav Sanchetnavadi Manovigyan (AVD, p. 151), may supply part of this, but it has not yet been translated in this collection.

          6.7 Adverse effects

          The contemplative-science literature documents reported difficulties associated with practice (§3.5), and Nagraj’s own regime of twelve to eighteen hours daily for years (MVD, p. 7) is intensive by any standard. The darshan does caution against the route he took: the method of silence is against the natural manifestation of (AVD, p. 74), it leaves the practitioner without a programme of living and unable to take part in family and society (AVD, pp. 164–165, 220), and the tradition’s own stories of bodies becoming inactive in samadhi are noted (AVD, p. 164). For its own recommended route it makes a strong claim: the progress of awakening involves no pain, and all pain belongs to wrongful activity and decline (KD, p. 36). The Buddhist-practice findings do not directly test the Madhyasth route, and their sampling does not establish a rate of harm. The darshan’s own claim calls for evidence from its course of study: how difficulties are identified, whether they arise, and how teachers and learners respond.

          6.8 Translation and source coverage

          Much of the positive account rests on material available here only in working translation. The upasana chapter of Manav Karm Darshan is a machine-assisted working translation; for this study its key passages were compared with the Hindi source pages and found to support the readings used (Editorial Notes). The English of Adhyatmvad, the darshan’s own treatment of spirituality, is a work-in-progress translation not yet peer reviewed, and Manav Sanchetnavadi Manovigyan, the treatise on the 122 conducts, has not been translated. The account of liberation, of study as practice, and of the activation of the faculties should be rechecked as those translations mature.

          7. Conclusion

          ’s account of practice is organised around a refusal. Its founder pursued the method he was told was the only way to know the unknown, reached the state it aims at, and reported that knowing the unknown did not occur: his foundational questions remained unanswered and he could not present evidence of the attainment. What replaces it is not a sparse residue but a full positive account. Spirituality is the understanding of the omnipresent reality in which every unit is saturated, and its accomplishment is atma-bodh together with realisation in coexistence. Realisation is the satisfaction of a definitive understanding of existence, , and humane conduct, and it is evidenced in behaviour, experiment, and tradition. Practice is study — overlapping listening and ratiocination, accompanied by practice in living — together with refinement of the fundamental tendencies, inward regulation of the faculties, and concentration for understanding and evidence. Gupta describes nididhyasan as the unfolding through direct cognition, conception and resolve, and realisation, becoming established in coexistence as an indelible impression in jeevan (RG). What practice aims at is the awakening of all ten activities of jeevan, of which only four and a half are effective in delusion, and their evidence in 122 conducts that include devotion, reverence, love, forgiveness, gratitude, and restraint. The traditional vocabulary of devotion, discipline, and liberation — bhakti, dhyan, yog, vairagya, jeevan-mukta, kaivalya — is kept and redefined by understanding and conduct. What is rejected is the path of mystery, faith without knowing, renunciation as an ideal, and the absorbed state as a goal.

          The comparison with Advaita requires a full account of its practice. Advaita joins ethical preparation, action and devotion, teacher-guided inquiry, reasoning, contemplation, and establishment in immediate self-knowledge. Both traditions hold that the reality to be realised is already present, distinguish understanding from the mere attainment of absorption, and connect realisation with conduct. They part on what the self and the world are — self– identity and a dependently appearing world against a finite jeevan saturated in the omnipresent and a perpetual world — and on the authority, content, and specified fulfilment of knowledge. Advaita includes compassion and exemplary social action while preserving renunciation and non-agency. Madhyasth Darshan makes continuing participation in orderliness, conveyance, work, and tradition integral to its account of evidence. Assessing the ontology in either case requires more than observing beneficial conduct.

          Modern philosophy turns out to have a great deal to say. Hadot shows that transformation-oriented practice is philosophy’s own inheritance rather than something outside it. The perennialist–constructivist dispute shows that the founder’s report of a silent state can be read as evidence for either view, which is a caution about how much any such report can settle. Alston shows that the demand for cross-checking needs defending rather than assuming. Paul explains why transformative practice complicates appraisal from present knowledge and values — the difficulty the darshan’s conveyability requirement is designed to escape and does not entirely escape. And contemplative science distinguishes measurable differences and reported changes from the further tests needed to assess a claim to knowledge.

          The darshan’s transferable contribution is the demand to distinguish contemplative attainment from what that attainment establishes. Nagraj makes the distinction from within the practice he pursued; its force lies in asking what has been understood and how that understanding becomes evident. Its positive account is more specific than a first reading suggests, since it names the faculties practice must activate, the conditions under which each step succeeds or fails, and the conducts by which awakening is to be recognised. Its cost is that its public marks test conduct and competence more directly than the ontology they are meant to confirm, that the teacher’s authority and the learner’s own examination stand in tension, and that its positive foundation rests on a single report and a method its author devised for himself. A reader who wants to test the darshan should test it where it asks to be tested: in whether study of its stated system brings the faculties into the order it describes, and whether the conduct that follows is the conduct it predicts.

          Editorial Notes

          The founder’s report as evidence

          §§1.3–1.4 rest on autobiographical passages in MVD, JV, and AVD. This study treats them as first-person historical testimony about how the darshan arose — evidence of the reported route, without independently establishing either the ontology realised or its reproducibility — and says so in §1.16 and §5.1 rather than letting them function as demonstration. The Epistemology study takes the same line at its §1.6.

          Recorded oral teaching as a source

          §§1.11, 1.12, 6.4, 6.5, and 6.6 draw on a recorded session of January 2010 at Amarkantak, Sakshatkar – Bodh – Anubhav – Praman, under four constraints. It never outweighs a printed text; where the two differ the text governs. It is cited by timestamp, so a reader can tell at a glance which claims rest on it. It is machine-transcribed Hindi that no Hindi speaker has yet checked against the audio, and the transcript records per-segment which passages are secure. And it is occasion-bound teaching, answering the people in the room rather than composed for publication.

          The printed definitions establish realisation as the point of satisfaction in definitive understanding (AVD, p. 224) and give reasons for preferring the practicable study route (AVD, pp. 72–74, 225). They are related to, but do not verify, the transcript’s stronger statements that anubhav adds no content beyond what sakshatkar makes firm (22:28) and that exploration is impractical for all (34:07). The transcript explicitly reserves these passages, and the assertion that there is no other method (14:12), for audio verification before released reliance. §§1.11–1.12 therefore rest their corresponding claims on the printed formulations.

          The names drashtapad for anubhav and jagriti for praman (13:09, 17:30, 21:13), and the automatic succession of stages after sakshatkar (03:03–03:30), were not located in the printed passages reviewed. They remain attributed to the working transcript, and §6.5 examines automaticity conditionally. Form, property, and essential nature can be understood while can only be realised (MVD, p. 273), so the printed account should not be reduced to an idle repetition of recognition. The goodwill-versus-coercion passage (26:50) used in §6.4 also remains provisionally transcribed; it cannot independently settle the teacher-authority question. The transcript, translation, and per-segment reliability marks are published as Sakshatkar – Bodh – Anubhav – Praman (2010, Amarkantak); the analysis behind these sections is in the companion research note alongside this file.

          Study functions and developmental order

          MVD p. 257 names shravan, manan, and arth-bodh as elements of successful teacher-guided study. P. 126 maps affirmation of coexistence to manan in , tulan in , sakshatkar in , and bodh in . It does not prescribe completing manan before beginning tulan. P. 295 also describes manan as receiving justice-based thought from vritti. §§1.6 and 1.11 therefore distinguish the learner’s activities, faculty correspondences, and developmental direction. The ordered account in JV p. 62 is retained together with the explicit concurrence of complete bodh and anubhav (MVD, pp. 207, 286–287).

          This study uses manan as ratiocination in the account of the learner’s method, following Gupta’s explanation: coherent synthesis and logical conclusions about reality and living, accompanied by practice. MVD p. 126’s acceptance-related manan in mun is retained as a narrower faculty correspondence. The relation between these uses should not be compressed into either a compulsory manan–tulan timetable or a definition of all ratiocination as acceptance alone. Shravan, listening, is distinct from shruti, articulation of understanding (MVD, pp. 324, 329).

          Gupta’s explanation of the method

          Rakesh Gupta’s explanation was supplied for this study on September 27, 2026. It is adopted here as a translator’s interpretation of the teaching method, separately attributed from Nagraj’s printed works and the 2010 recording. The supplied wording is:

          shravan and manan overlap. shravan is listening with the intent of understanding the meaning. manan is ratiocination. it requires one to build a coherent view from different threads of listening and reach the logical conclusion about the way reality is, and how one should live therefore. it is accompanied with practice in living. nidhidhyasan is the unfolding of the direct-cognition (sakshatkar), conception in buddhi and resolve, and thereafter realisation in atma. It is to become established with the understanding (samajh) and realisation (anubhav) of coexistence, which gets instilled in jeevan as indelible impression (sanskar). — RG

          The date records when the explanation was supplied for this study, not when Gupta originally stated it. The quotation retains the supplied spelling nidhidhyasan; analytical prose uses nididhyasan. Its direction from cognition through conception and resolve to realisation is preserved alongside the printed statement that complete bodh and anubhav become effective together (MVD, pp. 207, 286–287). No separately timed interval between their completed forms is asserted. The indelibility in Gupta’s explanation concerns accomplished establishment in understanding and realisation; formative conceptions remain open to refinement through study and living (MVD, pp. 187, 218).

          AVD pp. 71–72 uses nididhyasan in Nagraj’s retrospective description of inherited prescriptions. Gupta supplies the positive teaching-method sense used in §§1.6 and 1.11; it is not attributed to that historical passage. Likewise, the comparison with BU 2.4.5 concerns becoming established through meaningful understanding, while keeping the respective accounts of self, reality, and fulfilment distinct (§2.3).

          Tadroop-tadakar and the seer status

          The definition of tadroop-tadakar vidhi on MVD p. 80 belongs to the account of pursuing the ideal (ishta-sevan), whose four goals are oneness, accordance, abiding near, and continuous observance; establishment in the seer status is given there as the definition of the last of these, tadavalokan. This study therefore reads the sentence “Realisation in coexistence itself is the way of absolute-accordance and absolute-resonance” as the darshan’s reinterpretation of devotional union with a chosen ideal, not as a general thesis that method and realisation are identical, and it cites the recorded session rather than p. 80 for naming anubhav as drashtapad.

          Samadhi in three vocabularies

          Samadhi names different things in the traditions compared here, and this study does not harmonise them. In MVD, JV, and AVD it is the state Nagraj attained without resolving his foundational questions, and in which mun, vritti, chitta, and buddhi have no remaining motion. The Advaita texts distinguish knowledge-directed contemplation from sleep, mental dissolution, and attachment to contemplative pleasure; Shankara’s positive account of meditation and the Vivekachudamani’s strong emphasis on absorption must both be retained (§2.4). In the modern literature absorption also lacks a single referent across studies. The expression “absorbed state” therefore does not establish that all the cited practices or attainments are equivalent.

          Advaita sources, realisation, and establishment

          The Advaita comparison centres on Shankara’s commentaries on the Brahma Sutra, Brihadaranyaka Upanishad, and Bhagavad Gita. The Mandukya volume includes Gaudapada’s Karika and Shankara’s commentary; references distinguish the Upanishad’s verses from the Karika’s chapter and verse numbers. The Vivekachudamani is used as a traditionally attributed pedagogical witness. Its formulations are identified separately; neither its date nor the resolution of its disputed attribution is inferred from its emphasis on samadhi. This study does not survey all subsequent Advaita schools or settle their accounts of the relative efficacy of hearing, reflection, and contemplation.

          “Immediate self-knowledge” preserves the connection between understanding and realisation. The English translations use experience and personal realisation in contexts including BSB 1.1.2 and BG 6.8, while denying that liberation is a produced result additional to knowledge (BSB 1.1.4; BG 18.55). It would therefore be misleading either to oppose knowledge to all experience or to require a second, transient experience after completed liberating knowledge. Repetition, contemplative discipline, and establishment are discussed in relation to obstacles in the learner; the account of impressions after realisation in VC 267–276 is retained without imposing that work’s formulation as an identical sequence across every source.

          Printed BSB page numbers refer to the 1965 translation’s tenth impression (2009); the MU scan is the fourth impression (2000) of the revised second edition. The repository’s BG copy is a reflowed English compilation and its VC copy an English transcription: these are cited by chapter and verse, or by verse, rather than treating their PDF positions as pages in a print edition.

          Samyama, dharana, and dhyan

          Samyama appears in two senses: the method of §1.4, and restraint in humane thought, behaviour, and vocation as one of the 122 conducts (MVD, p. 337). §1.4 glosses the reversed sequence in the received sense of its terms and notes that the darshan’s own definitions give dharana as innateness (MVD, p. 43) and dhyan as concentration for understanding (MVD, p. 284). Whether those definitions apply to the words in the autobiographical passage is not stated in the texts reviewed; §6.1 records the reading they suggest without adopting it.

          Sadhana, upasana, and the practice vocabulary

          The darshan keeps and redefines the traditional names of practice rather than replacing them (§1.8). MVD and JV use sadhana for the practice Nagraj pursued and moved beyond, while AVD redefines it as effort towards self-improvement whose fulfilment is awakening (AVD, pp. 233–234). This study uses sadhana in that redefined sense where AVD is under discussion, and otherwise prefers study, upasana, and practice for the darshan’s recommended course. Upasana is used in its redefined sense, with the traditional sense reported where the sources report it (MVD, p. 4; JV, p. 9). The same convention is followed in Family Relationships and Values.

          Moksha and kaivalya

          AVD defines moksha as freedom from the bondage of delusion and evidence of awakening (p. 84), and again places moksha in awakening (p. 167). Yet its account of kaivalya says that absoluteness lies in awakening “and not in moksha” (pp. 260–261). These passages do not establish two successive achievements called liberation and its positive completion. The contrast may address an inherited use of moksha, but the material reviewed does not settle that interpretation. §1.5 retains the supported positive account of freedom from delusion, realisation, and participation in orderliness; the unexplained contrast remains a textual question.

          The 122 activities and their inventory

          §1.9 names representative pairs from the 122 activities. The translation renders the members of several pairs differently in different places, and §1.9 follows MVD’s definitions — devotedness for anurag, devoutness for pujyata, and fearlessness for dhruti, defined as the absence of fear (MVD, p. 324). The complete tabulation, its bal–shakti assignments, and the points at which the tabulated assignment and the definitions diverge are recorded in The Sixty-One Activity Pairs of Jeevan; this study does not depend on resolving those points.

          Working translations and page conventions

          MVD page numbers follow the printed page footers of the bilingual edition; where a Hindi passage and its English translation fall on facing pages, the page of the English is cited. The upasana chapter of Manav Karm Darshan is available here as a machine-assisted working translation. For this study the Hindi of KD printed pp. 30, 35, 36, 37, and 42 was compared with the rendered source pages and supports the translation at the points used. On p. 37 the Hindi reads जिसमें सामाजिकता स्वाभाविक रूप से समाहित रहती है, whose relative most naturally attaches to the direct fruit of upasana rather than to upasana itself; the same page defines real upasana as behaving with one’s equal and practising towards greater awakening, which supports §1.7’s relational purpose and fulfilment of practice, without excluding solitary reflection. AVD is cited from the page numbers of Sanjeev Chopra’s bilingual working translation (2023 edition), which has not yet been peer reviewed. Manav Sanchetnavadi Manovigyan has no English translation in this collection.

          Modern sources selected for relevance, not coverage

          §3 does not survey the philosophy of religion or the psychology of meditation. It selects the four bodies of work that bear on claims made in §1 — practice as philosophy, the content of contemplative states, the justification of experiential belief, and the appraisal of transformative experience — together with the methodological correction in contemplative science. Fuller treatment of religious experience and divinity belongs to God, Divinity, and the Sacred.
