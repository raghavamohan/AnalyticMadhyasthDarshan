**The question:** What does Madhyasth Darshan mean by spirituality and realisation, what practice leads to realisation, and how would anyone know it had occurred? How does its answer compare with Advaita Vedanta's path of knowledge, and with what modern philosophy and contemplative science have established about practice and religious experience?

This study examines *anubhav* — realisation — and the practice Madhyasth Darshan holds leads to it. The darshan presents its account of these matters as *anubhavatmak adhyatmvad*, realisation-centred spiritualism (AVD, p. 2), and the account is compared here with **Advaita Vedanta**, whose path of knowledge is its nearest and most demanding rival, and with **modern Western philosophy and contemplative science**, which turn out to have a great deal to say about this subject and not what one might expect.

The relevance of modern philosophy is worth stating in advance, because the natural assumption is that spiritual practice lies outside its range. It does not. Ancient philosophy was itself organised around spiritual exercises, as Hadot's work established; the epistemology of religious experience is a developed field; the perennialist–constructivist dispute is exactly the question of whether contemplative states have content independent of the tradition that trains them; L. A. Paul's account of transformative experience formalises the problem of appraising a state one has not undergone; and contemplative science has produced both suggestive findings and a serious methodological reckoning. Each bears directly on claims the darshan makes.

[The Epistemology of Coexistence](../The-Epistemology-of-Coexistence/The-Epistemology-of-Coexistence.pdf) §1.6 states the transmission and evidence conditions in outline and §§7.1–7.5 records the resulting evidential problems; [The Ontology of Coexistence](../The-Ontology-of-Coexistence/The-Ontology-of-Coexistence.pdf) §1.14 sets out realisation in coexistence as an activity of *jeevan*; and [*The Sixty-One Activity Pairs of Jeevan*](../The-Epistemology-of-Coexistence/Research-Note-Activity-Pair-Inventory.pdf) records the 122 activities of *jeevan* pair by pair. This study takes the practice itself as its subject: what is done, which activities of *jeevan* it makes effective and how, what is claimed to result, and what would count as knowing that it had.

## 1. The Madhyasth Darshan Answer

Madhyasth Darshan holds that realisation is the understanding of existence as coexistence — of every unit saturated in one omnipresent reality — reached by *jeevan* through study and the refinement of its own activities, and evidenced in behaviour, work, and the capacity to convey it. It denies that any absorbed state is itself a knowing, and it says so on the strength of its founder's report that he attained such a state and found no knowing in it. That denial separates the darshan most sharply from the traditions it emerged from. What replaces the absorbed state is a positive account: spirituality as the understanding of the omnipresent, realisation as the awakening of all ten activities of *jeevan* and their evidence in 122 named conducts, and liberation as release from delusion.

### 1.1 Spirituality as the understanding of the omnipresent

The darshan uses the word *adhyatma*, spirit, for the omnipresent reality itself. Every unit in existence — atom, plant, animal, human — is saturated in one all-pervading, non-active reality, and the darshan's name for its account of spirituality comes from that fact:

> **“One of the names I have given to this omnipresent reality is ‘adhyatma’ (spirit). This scripture has been named as ‘Realisation Centred Spiritualism’ because all the units are seen to be submerged, soaked & surrounded in this omnipresent reality.”**
> — AVD, p. 236

The same reality is called Omnipotence (*satta*), Brahma, space, and knowledge. Knowledge itself is the omnipresent Omnipotence, referred to as space, and it is called *paramatma* for being subtler than *atma* (MVD, p. 35); omnipresence is what the word God refers to (MVD, p. 163). Humans have the opportunity to understand, study, and experiment with three aspects of existence — the spiritual, which is the omnipresent; the deific, which is the intellectual or sentient; and the metaphysical, which is insentient nature — and every man and woman has the right to realisation and experimentation in coexistence (MVD, p. 285). The spiritual accomplishment is named precisely: *atma-bodh*, the enlightenment of the self, is itself the spiritual accomplishment (MVD, p. 286), and upon definitive understanding, as *buddhi* turns towards *atma*, *atma-bodh* and Brahma-realisation — realisation in coexistence — come into effect together (MVD, pp. 286–287).

Two consequences follow for practice. Spirituality is not a domain of experience set apart from ordinary life: the omnipresent is present in everything, so understanding it is understanding the whole of existence and oneself within it. And spiritual knowledge is not ineffable. The idealist scriptures and mystery-based contemplation hold knowledge to be unmanifest and ineffable; this darshan holds that knowledge is manifest, effable, and understandable through study, and that its evidence becomes accessible to all through behaviour (MVD, p. 4).

The founder's own questions set the terms of the account. How could the world be false if it arose from Brahma, the truth? How could Brahma be the cause of both bondage and liberation? And what counts as evidence — the word or its bearer, the credible saying or its originator, the scripture or its author (MVD, pp. 4–6)? The darshan's answers to these three questions organise what follows: Brahma is truth and the world is perpetual (MVD, p. 13); bondage is delusion and liberation is its removal (§1.5); and the awakened human alone is the basis of evidence (SB, p. 5).

### 1.2 What realisation is

Realisation has a definite object and a definite location. It is realisation in the ultimate truth which is in the form of coexistence, distinguished from contemplation, the awareness of truth (MVD, p. 316). Its object is not something absent that practice must bring into being. Omnipotence in the form of omnipresence has been uniformly inherent in every entity since eternity, and its realisation is the goal of humans (MVD, p. 317); truth is present even before awakening (MVD, p. 303); and while union with any finite thing is attained and lost, no entity or activity is ever without the pre-existing union with the omnipresent, which can only be realised (MVD, p. 302). What study and practice develop is the capacity, ability, and receptivity for that realisation, which is the right of every human being (MVD, p. 302). Realisation is an activity of *atma* (MVD, pp. 204, 307), and its effect is transmitted to *buddhi*, *chitta*, *vritti*, and *mun* (MVD, p. 318).

Its content is given as three kinds of knowledge — of existence as coexistence, of *jeevan*, and of humane conduct (MVD, pp. 13, 127, 300; KD, p. 30). Complete enlightenment is the enlightenment of these three, and it is called enlightenment of the self (MVD, p. 300): an awakened human has the awareness of their own being and of the being of the entire existence (MVD, p. 84). Awakening is correspondingly defined as knowing, believing, recognising, and fulfilling *jeevan* and existence as they are (AVD, p. 259). Realisation is thus neither a feeling nor a vision. It is the understanding, obtained through the natural progression of things, of their states and motions, their emergence and their results (MVD, p. 77).

Each kind of knowledge has determinate content. To understand any existing thing is to understand its *rup*, *gun*, *swabhav*, and *dharma* — form, property, essential nature, and innateness (MVD, p. 273). *Rup* is determined by shape, volume, and density; *gun* is the generative, degenerative, and mediative; *swabhav* differs across the four orders; and *dharma* is cumulative across them — existence in the material order, growth along with existence in the biological, the hope of living along with both in the animal, and happiness along with all three in the knowledge order (MVD, pp. 50–51). Form, property, and essential nature can be understood; *dharma* can only be realised (MVD, p. 273). Visualisation of all four, together with generative, degenerative, and mediative knowledge, is the innateness of *chitta*, where *sakshatkar*, direct recognition, takes place (MVD, pp. 126, 203–204).

Knowledge of humane conduct is specified by justice, *dharma*, and truth. Deciding justice and injustice, *dharma* and *adharma*, truth and untruth is the innateness of *vritti* (MVD, pp. 203–204), and the three are levels of realisation itself: justice is the manifestation of realisation in behaviour, *dharma* the manifestation of realisation in thought, and coexistence the realisation in *atma* that is the ultimate truth (MVD, p. 156). Correct evaluation occurs only through awakening; otherwise over-, under-, or mis-evaluation takes place (MVD, p. 203). Knowledge reaches conduct through law, regulation, and balance:

> **“Knowledge itself is law; law itself is regulation; regulation itself is balance; balanced living itself is justice in humans; living within the universal orderliness with justice itself is dharma, i.e., resolution; realisation in coexistence itself is dharma and truth.”**
> — MVD, p. 174

The six of *niyam*, *niyantran*, *santulan*, *nyaya*, *dharma*, and *satya* — law, regulation, balance, justice, *dharma*, and truth — are evidenced in the fruition of realisation (MVD, p. 80). It follows that a person cannot have realised and still misjudge, or have realised and still live unregulated, and the darshan states the observable mark: absence of mistakes and crimes in behaviour characterises complete awakening, and it is observed in human interactions (MVD, p. 285). This is what makes the account testable in principle; §6.3 examines how much the test establishes.

What is realised is not private. The study is in, by, and for realisation in coexistence (MVD, p. 35), and what is realised can be stated, taught, and lived. Whatever cannot be conveyed has not, on this account, been realised.

*Anubhav* is one stage of four — *sakshatkar*, *bodh*, *anubhav*, *praman* — whose relations §1.11 sets out. Where this study says "realisation" without qualification it means *anubhav*; where the distinction between the stages matters, the Hindi terms are used.

### 1.3 *Samadhi* without knowing

The account of practice begins with a negative finding reported in the first person. Shri A. Nagraj records that he was assured by the scholars and sages of his time that the only way to know the unknown is *samadhi*, accepted this, went to Amarkantak, and practised from 1950 with maturity in the 1960s (MVD, p. 7). The practice was traditional. He followed the Agama-Tantra method of *upasana*, which seeks *samadhi* by visualising the deities in every part of the body (*nyas*), until one day he found himself free of their imagined proximity: the awareness of "I am" remained, there were no other thoughts, and there was no thought of achieving, having, or doing anything (JV, p. 12). His practice also followed the instructions available for *nididhyasan*, *samadhi*, control of the mind, and the method of observing (AVD, p. 72). The result is stated without hedging:

> **“In 1970, I accepted that I had attained samadhi. In this state, my hopes, thoughts, and desires were silent, and it was clear that the event of ‘knowing the unknown’ did not occur.”**
> — MVD, p. 7

The state recurred for twelve to eighteen hours a day (MVD, p. 7). It answered none of his questions: he received no answers from understanding Vedanta and none after achieving *samadhi* (JV, p. 13), and the thoughtless state, whether called *samadhi*, *nididhyasan*, complete enlightenment, or *nirvana*, did not supply them (AVD, p. 72). Nor could the state serve as evidence of itself:

> **“There was nothing to describe in the state of samadhi; how could I present its evidence?”**
> — JV, p. 13

Two distinct claims are being made, and they should not be run together. The first is phenomenological: in *samadhi* hope, thought, and desire fall silent — *mun*, *vritti*, *chitta*, and *buddhi* have no remaining motion (AVD, p. 220) — and no knowing occurs. The state is not nothing. It yields what the traditions call self-limited happiness (*svantah sukh*), free of fear, temptation, and the pain of lack (AVD, p. 220), and Nagraj counts himself among the very few who have reached it (AVD, p. 222). The second claim is evidential: because there is nothing in the state to describe, it cannot serve as evidence of anything, including of itself. The second is the more consequential, and it is a demand for public checkability made from inside a contemplative tradition rather than against it.

The darshan draws a practical conclusion as well. No programme of living arises in the thoughtless state, so the person's usefulness becomes zero (AVD, p. 220); during the pursuit of *samadhi* the seeker cannot take part in family, society, or the system of living (AVD, p. 165); and the method of silence is completely against the natural manifestation of *jeevan*, whose activity never ceases (AVD, p. 74).

### 1.4 *Samyama*, and an inverted ladder

What Nagraj reports as productive is *samyama*, and he undertook it to test the *samadhi* he could not otherwise evidence (JV, p. 13). Patanjali's definition — *dharana*, *dhyana*, and *samadhi* practised on one object — is cited and then departed from:

> **“I could not agree with what was written about samyama. Hence, I devised my own design and method of samyama and subsequently carried it out.”**
> — JV, p. 13

The design he adopted inverts the received order:

> **“Of my own accord, I accepted the sequence of samadhi, dhyan, and dharana as the process for samyama. Two years later, having accomplished samyama, I accepted the evidence of having attained samadhi.”**
> — MVD, p. 7

Its object is stated separately. In the thoughtless state, together with the acceptance of existence, all realities were seen to be ceaselessly immersed in space, and this prompted *samyama* on space itself; soon after, existence presented itself in its true form, as coexistence, and *jeevan* was seen within it (AVD, p. 72). Seeing, here, means understanding (JV, p. 13; AVD, p. 87). The reported outcome is realisation that all existence is in the form of coexistence, presented in literary form for study (MVD, p. 7) — a state in which the understanding can be imparted, together with sight of the *jeevan* atom and of the three stages of wanting to live, to be happy, and to evidence oneself (JV, pp. 13–14).

Patanjali's sequence ascends from holding to absorption. This one runs from absorption back through attention to *dharana*, a word the darshan elsewhere defines as innateness, that which cannot be separated from a thing (MVD, p. 43). The darshan presents *samyama* not as traditional authority but as a method its author devised because the received one did not satisfy him, and it does not recommend the route to others (§1.12). §6.1 takes up what the texts do and do not explain about the sequence.

### 1.5 Mystery, delusion, and liberation

The darshan names its target *rahasya*, mystery, and defines it as a deficiency of understanding rather than a property of reality:

> **“Not revealing understanding or not being able to do so, or not being able to attain it, is a mystery. The incapacity for this is ‘mysteriousness’.”**
> — MVD, p. 273

Mystery is also the incompleteness in understanding the absolute state and activity, and its eradication comes only through the holistic view of reality, which yields certitude (MVD, p. 209). Beneath mystery lies delusion, belief different from reality (MVD, p. 291), whose root is the assumption that the body is *jeevan* (MVD, pp. 80–81; AVD, p. 88). Delusion is at the root of all unhappiness (MVD, p. 318), and it shows as excess, deficiency, and omission in visualisation and as over-, under-, and mis-evaluation in judgement (MVD, p. 286; AVD, p. 55).

The hope for liberation is found in all humans. The darshan distinguishes liberation from unhappiness and liberation from delusion, notes that all study, worship, experimentation, behaviour, vocation, practice, and dedication have been undertaken for these, and presents its own proposal as one for liberation from delusion (MVD, p. 149). It gives the old question of bondage and liberation a definite answer. What is bound, what binds, and what is freed is *jeevan* itself (AVD, pp. 139, 150). Bondage takes three forms: bondage of hope, directed at sensory gratification; bondage of thought, which takes one's own thoughts to be superior; and bondage of desire, which takes the maximisation of physical creation to be superior (AVD, pp. 155, 167). To be free from the bondage of delusion is *moksha*, and this is the evidence of awakening (AVD, p. 84).

The liberated condition is described in the traditions' own vocabulary. The complete acceptance and realisation of the mortality of the body, the immortality and continuity of *jeevan*, and the laws of work and behaviour is seedless (*nirbeej*) thought; behaviour in accordance with it is seedless behaviour; and this is liberation from delusion (MVD, p. 81). The *jeevan-mukta* — one liberated while living, who has moved irreversibly out of animal consciousness and delusion — does not suffer from the recollection of the past or apprehension of the future and has no resistance with the present (MVD, p. 81), and nurturing and advancing human society is their essential nature (MVD, p. 157). The state of realisation is named *kaivalya*, absoluteness, in which there is liberation from suffering, resentment, fear, and the bondage of delusion (MVD, pp. 317–318). *Kaivalya* is evidenced as participation in the whole of orderliness together with expression, communication, and exposition; the positive form of liberation from bondage is awakening completeness, and *kaivalya* lies in awakening, not in *moksha* (AVD, pp. 260–261). For one who is evidence in that state, no further bodily journeys are needed (AVD, p. 261).

The goal of practice is therefore neither an encounter nor a dissolution of the self. It is the removal of a cognitive deficit, delusion, from which the ending of suffering follows. A deluded *jeevan* keeps yearning for a happiness the five senses cannot supply (MVD, p. 274), and the accomplishment of mental wellbeing or bliss is itself liberation from mystery (MVD, p. 301).

### 1.6 Study as practice

The positive method is study, and the darshan states it in the vocabulary of worship:

> **“Study itself is worship. The authentic human is the teacher, in whose proximity study takes place.”**
> — MVD, p. 150

Through study one attains the authority of innateness and independence by coming into absolute-resonance and absolute-accordance with understanding (MVD, p. 150). Study is defined by what it discriminates: understanding the difference between the laws necessary and unnecessary for humaneness is study, and implementing the necessary ones at all levels is evidence and accomplishment (MVD, p. 161). The journey to realisation begins with methodical study, whose field is definitive understanding (*avdharana*) (AVD, p. 223). Dedication carries study to realisation: study for the human goal in the light of justice, *dharma*, and truth occurs through dedication, and dedication leads to realisation (MVD, p. 150).

Study proceeds by two means that correct each other, inquiry while studying and study while inquiring (AVD, p. 225). Instruction by sermon always carries a possibility of delusion, and a tradition's commands remain sermons or information until they become studiable, that is, until their intent is understood in logic and behaviour (AVD, p. 225). Each person examines any presentation with their own imagination and freedom of action and accepts or rejects it on that basis (AVD, p. 226). The darshan's formula for the relation between accepting and knowing is short: believe what is known; know what is believed (MVD, p. 12).

The older vocabulary of practice is kept and redefined around study. *Sadhana* is effort towards self-improvement, whose point of fulfilment is awakening (AVD, pp. 233–234); practice (*abhyas*) is carrying the awakening and prosperity so attained into behaviour and teaching as comprehensive resolution (AVD, p. 234); and the triad of aim, seeker, and means is reconstituted, with every human the seeker of awakening, awakening the aim, and the method of study the means (AVD, p. 224; MVD, p. 322). Experimentation is the endeavour, continued until it succeeds, to meet a human need by instilling usefulness and aesthetic value in natural abundance (MVD, p. 56). Humans have undertaken studies, experiments, explorations, and practices for realisation, reflected in humaneness and higher humaneness (MVD, p. 302), and the study and practice of the mediative activity, *atma*, as presented in this darshan, leads to integrality — justice, *dharma*, and truth-based behaviour, thought, and realisation together (MVD, p. 303).

### 1.7 *Upasana* redefined

The inherited sense of *upasana* is devotional practice towards deities, or ritual (MVD, p. 4; JV, p. 9). In this darshan its content is different altogether:

> **“Upasana is an important part of human jeevan. It is the process of refining and transforming the fundamental tendencies, and this study also transforms sanskar and essential nature.”**
> — KD, p. 30

The fundamental tendencies are the *antahkarana*'s hope, thought, desire, and resoluteness (KD, p. 36), and *upasana* is itself a form of study: until one is complete in one's own *dharma*, *upasana* continues in the form of study (KD, p. 38), and the attainments of the best *upasana* are the completeness of study (KD, p. 37). Its root sense is attaining togetherness through proper means, which requires diligence and practice (KD, p. 30). It is of three kinds: *kutastha upasana*, undertaken for complete realisation; *rupastha upasana*, undertaken to approach a form endowed with magnificence; and *atmastha upasana*, which is reflection itself and to which every human being is bound (KD, p. 31). The deities of the traditions are reinterpreted within the same scheme as bodiless *jeevans* — elemental, deific, and divine selves — ranked by awakening (KD, p. 31), and the *upasana* directed to each yields base learning, sub-learning, or learning accordingly (KD, p. 32).

*Upasana* is education and orderliness in its own nature (KD, p. 42); its chief utility lies in immersion in universal values (KD, p. 35); at the root of all *upasanas* the aim is undivided society and universal orderliness (KD, p. 35); and *upasanas* exist only for the capacity for awakening, not for decline (KD, p. 36). Its success condition is stated: *upasana* must contain the aim, the seeker, and the means together, and their integrality brings success while their separation brings failure (KD, p. 38). Its disciplines include the classical restraints: truthfulness, non-violence, non-possession, and non-stealing are practised as restraint (KD, p. 39); gratitude is the beginning-to-end basis of restraint; and a *mantra* is any meaningful word whose meaning and nature are attained by consistently remembering that meaning (KD, p. 40). *Upasana* that takes a symbol for its goal is judged secondary and unsocial to the extent of its incompleteness, because in it the human goal is neither clarified nor attained (KD, p. 42).

The consequence is that practice in this darshan has no private phase. Wisdom and *jeevan*-value — resolution and prosperity — are the direct results of *upasana*, and sociality is naturally contained in them; the dignity of human *jeevan* is the impetus to behave with one's equal and to practise towards greater awakening, and this is real *upasana* (KD, p. 37). The *samadhi* route is the reverse case, in which the seeker cannot take part in family or society while practising (§1.3). [Family Relationships and Values](../Family-Relationships-And-Values/Family-Relationships-And-Values.pdf) §1.4.2 draws the family-scale consequence: the practice begins in the relationships one is born into.

### 1.8 *Yog*, *dhyan*, and the vocabulary of discipline

*Yog* in this darshan is union, of two kinds, unification and togetherness (MVD, p. 42). It names how units come together rather than a technique of posture or breath, but it is not only a term of physical ontology. Mediative behaviour, thought, and realisation constitute pre-existing *yog*, continuously available and ever present, while the development and decline of a unit depend on the unions it enters, development-oriented or decline-oriented (MVD, p. 159). Realisation happens only of pre-existing *yog* — the omnipresent reality from which no entity is ever separate — while proximity and accumulation belong only to attainable *yog* (MVD, p. 302). *Yog* is therefore necessary for realisation (MVD, p. 302); every human is awakened only through *yog* (MVD, p. 304); and the realisation of the self is attained through *yog* in the tradition, meaning the union of body and *jeevan* and union with awakened humans for awakening (MVD, p. 301). This is why humans seek association with those more awakened than themselves (MVD, p. 274). A reader carrying the modern sense of "yoga" into these passages will find no postures and no breath-control. What they will find is an account of the company and orientation through which awakening happens.

*Dhyan* is redefined in the same spirit. The meditation the traditions recommend aims to limit thought to one point until thought ceases (AVD, pp. 164, 219). The darshan gives concentration a different object:

> **“Concentration involves focusing mun, vritti, chitta, and buddhi for understanding, and focusing them for the purpose of evidence after understanding and realisation.”**
> — MVD, p. 284

The object of concentration is the comprehension of meaning and its evidence, and all humans possess the capacity for concentration (MVD, pp. 284–285). Concentration in this sense belongs to the inward regulation (*antarniyaman*) through which *atma-bodh* happens, and *atma-bodh* is the highest accomplishment of concentration (MVD, p. 286).

The ascetic vocabulary is reworked the same way. Dispassion is inclination towards the ideal without inclination elsewhere, and a chain of identities joins it to non-attachment, non-doership, non-accumulation, fearlessness and prosperity, heavenliness, humility, supreme detachment (*par-vairagya*), *yog*, realisation in omnipresence, mediation, authenticity, and liberation from delusion (MVD, p. 157). Supreme detachment is liberation from the flaws of excess, deficiency, and omission that stand against awakening (MVD, pp. 157–158). Non-accumulation means resolution and prosperity rather than poverty, and heaven becomes the heavenliness of just living (MVD, p. 160). Initiation (*diksha*) is accepting a definite method of behaviour and conduct as an unbroken pledge (MVD, p. 309). The ideal (*ishta*) of devotional practice becomes self-driven reverence, trust, and dedication towards coexistence together with complete knowledge (MVD, p. 80). And *samyama* has, besides the founder's method of §1.4, the ordinary sense of restraint: being regulated in humane thought, behaviour, and vocation (MVD, p. 337).

Devotion belongs to this vocabulary too. *Bhakti* is the activity of becoming liberated from fear and delusion, the expression, communication, and exposition of prayer joined with *seva*, and an expression of total dedication (MVD, p. 339), where dedication is the continuous endeavour of attaining and evidencing complete awakening (MVD, p. 340). The expression, communication, and exposition of values with evidence, maintained in human tradition upon awakening, is love and devotion (MVD, p. 307). Devotedness (*anurag*) and love (*prem*) towards the ideal in the form of coexistence are among the mandatory conditions of realisation (MVD, p. 317; §1.12). The darshan's practice vocabulary is therefore wider than study, experiment, and practice: it keeps *upasana*, *sadhana*, *abhyas*, *dhyan*, *yog*, *samyama*, *bhakti*, *diksha*, and *vairagya*, and gives each a content defined by understanding and conduct. What it rejects is set out in §1.15.

### 1.9 The ten activities of *jeevan* and the 122 conducts

*Jeevan* is a constitutionally complete atom whose nucleus is *atma* and whose four orbits are *buddhi*, *chitta*, *vritti*, and *mun* (MVD, p. 78; AVD, p. 87). These five are its inexhaustible strengths (*bal*), and hope, thought, desire, resoluteness (*ritambhara*), and authenticity (*pramanikta*) are its inexhaustible powers (*shakti*) (MVD, pp. 75, 84; AVD, p. 151). Each faculty has two activities, one of reflection and one of projection: realisation and authenticity in *atma*, enlightenment and resolve in *buddhi*, contemplation and visualisation in *chitta*, deliberation and analysis in *vritti*, and taste and selection in *mun* (JV, pp. 73, 92–93). Every *jeevan* continuously operates through these ten activities, whether in delusion or in awakening (JV, p. 73).

In delusion only four and a half of them are effective. Selection and taste in *mun*, analysis in *vritti*, visualisation in *chitta*, and half of deliberation — deliberation confined to what is pleasant, healthy, and profitable — are sufficient for the five senses and the four instincts, and what they produce does not become the content of contemplation (JV, pp. 73–74; AVD, p. 152; MVD, p. 79). They come into play in that order from infancy (JV, p. 94). Awakening the remaining five and a half, which happens through understanding, is what a humane mentality requires, and awakening is the evidence of all ten in behaviour (JV, p. 74). Upon awakening, *jeevan* evidences all ten through the realisation-based way (MVD, p. 79).

The ten are the generic activities. In awakening their detailed content becomes evident as 122 activities:

> **“Mun, vritti, chitta, buddhi and atma are integral in jeevan. In awakened jeevan, its faculties or activities that become evident in the tradition in the form of strength & power or state & motion are as follows: atma has two faculties, buddhi has four, chitta has sixteen, vritti has thirty-six, and mun has sixty-four. Therefore, 122 activities are found in human jeevan in the form of strength & power, that is, in state & motion. Each person can demonstrate all of the above activities.”**
> — MVD, p. 323

The 122 are tabulated as the conducts of awakened *jeevan*, sixty-one pairs of a reflective and a projective activity, and each is defined (AVD, pp. 91–94; MVD, pp. 328–348). They include most of what the contemplative and devotional traditions treat as spiritual attainments. In *atma* the pair is realisation in existence and the authenticity of ultimate bliss. In *buddhi* the pairs are bliss and definiteness, and existence and fearlessness. In *chitta* they include revelation and memory, contentment and abundance, love and non-otherness, and reverence (*shraddha*) and devoutness (*pujyata*). In *vritti* they include learning and intelligence, peace and kindness, grace and compassion, uprightness and forgiveness, gratitude and humility, truth and *dharma*, justice and sensitivity, absolute-oneness and intrepidity, and restraint and law. In *mun* they include devotion and immersion, care and generosity, respect and cordiality, and affection and dedication (AVD, pp. 91–94; MVD, pp. 323–340). Bliss, love, reverence, devotion, forgiveness, gratitude, restraint, and absolute-oneness are thus not states to be attained in withdrawal. They are named conducts of awakened *jeevan*, and many of them are directed to other people.

This is the point at which the account of practice becomes specific. Practice is not aimed at producing a state; it is aimed at making effective activities that *jeevan* already has. Knowledge of *jeevan* is its constitution together with the inspection, examination, and survey of its activities (AVD, pp. 86–87), which makes one's own activities a field of study. The systematic study of the awakened conducts is the subject of a separate treatise, *Manav Sanchetnavadi Manovigyan* (AVD, p. 151).

### 1.10 How the activities are activated and refined

The defect practice addresses is located with unusual precision. Under delusion, hope in *mun* is based on sensation, thought in *vritti* follows that hope, desire in *chitta* follows that thought, and *buddhi*, turned away from *atma*, supports deluded visualisation and desire (MVD, pp. 204, 275). In awakening the direction reverses: hope in *mun* accords with *vritti*, thought in *vritti* with *chitta*, desire in *chitta* with the enlightenment of *buddhi*, and the enlightenment of *buddhi* with *atma*'s realisation in existence; the resulting reflection and projection form the awakened *jeevan* cycle (MVD, p. 275). Practice is therefore the repair of a circuit rather than the accumulation of states ([The Epistemology of Coexistence](../The-Epistemology-of-Coexistence/The-Epistemology-of-Coexistence.pdf) §1.4).

Awakening begins with the strength of contemplation, whereas the bondage of delusion runs on the powers of hope, thought, and desire (AVD, p. 151). Study supplies contemplation with its content. As definitive understanding grows, the perspectives of justice, *dharma*, and truth become active in the deliberation of *vritti*; their direct recognition follows in *chitta*, enlightenment and acceptance in *buddhi*, and their realisation in *atma*, confirmed in contemplation (AVD, pp. 86, 223–224; MVD, p. 126). Each faculty reaches its complete development by receiving the signal of the one within it. *Mun* does so by acquiring the receptivity for just behaviour on accepting the signals of *vritti*; *vritti* by accepting the signals of *chitta*, that is, by the ability to form *dharma*-based thought; *chitta* by the capacity for creative visualisation according to the signals of *buddhi* in the presence of truth; *buddhi* by accepting the signals of *atma*; and *atma* in realisation in coexistence (MVD, pp. 322–323). Each step has its mark. When *mun* receives the signal of *vritti*, just behaviour and affection follow; when *vritti* receives that of *chitta*, peace; when *chitta* receives that of *buddhi*, contentment and self-governed living; and when *buddhi* receives that of *atma*, enlightenment of the self, *atma-bodh*, and resolve in truth (MVD, pp. 282–283). Concurrence of *mun* and *vritti* is happiness, of *vritti* and *chitta* peace, of *chitta* and *buddhi* contentment, and of *buddhi* and *atma* bliss; the eternal concurrence of *atma* with the omnipresent is ultimate bliss (MVD, p. 327).

The conditions of each step are named as well. The reflection of hope succeeds in the absence of crime, of thought in the absence of injustice, of desire in the absence of attachment, and of resolve in the absence of ignorance. Each requires its support: the influence of orderliness for behaviour without crime, of social conduct for thought without injustice, of study and *sanskar* for desire without attachment, and inward regulation or concentration for a *buddhi* without ignorance. Only then does reflection succeed; otherwise it fails (MVD, p. 284). The channelling of *jeevan*'s energies towards awakening runs through curiosity in *mun*, enthusiasm in *vritti*, delight in *chitta*, elation and immersion in *buddhi*, and realisation in *atma*, and it requires the inward regulation of those energies (MVD, p. 77).

Refinement is the work of *upasana* (§1.7), and its object is definite: the fundamental tendencies of hope, thought, desire, and resoluteness, which are refined when they are no longer wasted. The awakening of activity-power is the right use, rather than the waste, of the powers exercised through the senses; the awakening of desire-power is the right use of the fundamental tendencies; and the awakening of knowledge-power is right enlightenment together with complete realisation. This awakening of the three powers is the supreme attainment of human *jeevan* and the purpose of *upasana* (KD, p. 36). What refinement removes is delusion's excess, deficiency, and omission in visualisation and its over-, under-, and mis-evaluation (MVD, p. 286; AVD, p. 55), and the level of awakening a person has reached is judged by their attachment or non-attachment to the body's instincts and the objects of the senses (MVD, p. 203).

Once awakened, realisation flows outward through the same faculties. Its enlightenment is in *buddhi*; its awareness (*prateeti*) arises where *buddhi* meets *chitta*; its comprehension (*abhas*) where *chitta* meets *vritti*; and its perception (*bhas*) where *vritti* meets *mun*, showing as the taste of values and the selection of relationships (MVD, p. 99). Just behaviour is the perception of truth, resolved thought its comprehension, and contemplation its awareness (MVD, p. 316). This outward course is how the 122 conducts become evident in relationships (§1.9), and it is why awakening is evidenced through the combined activity of all five faculties (MVD, p. 203).

### 1.11 The ladder: *sakshatkar*, *bodh*, *anubhav*, *praman*

Realisation is not a single act. It is the third rung of a four-stage sequence — *sakshatkar*, *bodh*, *anubhav*, *praman* — whose members are linked so tightly that each is said to be the next: realisation is the ultimate evidence, evidence is understanding, understanding is manifest, the manifest is resolution and work and behaviour, work and behaviour is evidence, evidence is awakened tradition, and awakened tradition is coexistence (MVD, p. 12). The stages fall on the faculties of §1.9: affirmation of truth in the form of coexistence through study is *manan* in *mun*, *tulan* in *vritti* by the qualitative method, *sakshatkar* in *chitta*, and *bodh* in *buddhi* (MVD, p. 126). Realisation-based *bodh* issues in resolve, *sakshatkar*, realisation-based contemplation and thought, and finally evidence (MVD, p. 99). Nagraj describes the same order in the first person: realisation attained after revelation (*sakshatkar*) and definitive understanding (*avdharna bodh*), contemplation following from that enlightenment (JV, p. 62).

The relation between the stages is defined through definitive understanding:

> **“Knowing, believing & recognising itself is definitive understanding. Attaining the point of satisfaction in these itself is realisation. Expressing it in actions & behaviour, and in systems, is authenticity.”**
> — AVD, p. 224

Realisation on this definition adds no new object to what definitive understanding already holds. It is the point at which that understanding reaches satisfaction, and authenticity is its expression in conduct and in orderliness. In a recorded session Nagraj puts the same point directly. Asked what further is understood in *anubhav* beyond *bodh*, he answers that nothing is: what occurs in *sakshatkar* becomes firm (22:28). He adds that if *sakshatkar* succeeds the remaining three stages follow of themselves, as acceptance or rejection follows ordinary seeing (03:03–03:30), and that *praman* is the power to bring about *bodh* in another (04:43). The weight of the sequence therefore falls on *sakshatkar*, which he calls the success of study (12:54).

The fourth stage is not a report issued after the fact. Evidence is awakened tradition (MVD, p. 12), and only awakened humans are recognisable in the seer status (MVD, p. 33). In the same session *anubhav* is given the second name *drashtapad*, the seat of the seer, and *praman* the name *jagriti*, awakening (13:09, 17:30, 21:13).

Establishment in the seer status is also the printed definition of *tadavalokan*, continuous observance of the ideal, and that definition belongs to the darshan's reinterpretation of devotional union. In the knowledge order, pursuit of the ideal (*ishta-sevan*) aims at oneness with it, accordance with it, abiding near it, and continuous observance of it; the ideal is self-driven reverence, trust, and dedication towards coexistence together with complete knowledge; and the way to that accordance is realisation itself (MVD, p. 80):

> **“Realisation in coexistence itself is the way of absolute-accordance and absolute-resonance (tadakar), because law, regulation, balance, justice, dharma, and truth become evident as a result of realisation.”**
> — MVD, p. 80

The prefix *tat-* in these terms indicates awakening (MVD, p. 80), and awakening is the human ideal (MVD, pp. 306–307). The vocabulary of becoming one with a chosen deity is kept, its object becomes awakening in coexistence, and the means of accordance becomes study: through study one comes into absolute-resonance and absolute-accordance with understanding (MVD, p. 150), just as the inner faculties, brought into absolute-resonance with an aim, become intense in that aim (KD, p. 40).

### 1.12 Two paths, preconditions, and the teacher

For a person who is not the discoverer there are two alternatives:

> **“Such a process is of two kinds: One - Exploration. Two - Following, Emulation, Study.”**
> — MVD, p. 280

Exploration is an inventive realisation which the seeker studies comprehensively, from the extreme of decline to the highest development and awakening. It is the route of a seeker accomplished in *samadhi*, who establishes themself in just behaviour and *dharma*-based thought through practice and then develops the capacity for deific and divine humaneness and for realisation of the omnipresent (MVD, pp. 280–281). The second path has two programmes: following or emulating just behaviour, and being and abiding in resolved, *dharma*-based thought. Firmness and dedication in these two give the authority for realisation of truth (MVD, p. 282).

The darshan also settles which path is for whom. It asks why its author, having reached awakening by the traditional methods, does not recommend them, and replies that its own method is practicable. The silence method yields only self-limited happiness, no one can be assured of success within a lifetime, and silence is against the natural manifestation of *jeevan* (AVD, pp. 72–74). The two ways it offers are exploration, and inquiry together with study (AVD, p. 225). In the recorded session Nagraj adds that for everyone to undertake *samadhi–samyama* and exploration is impractical, and that study is the method by which everyone can arrive (34:07).

The conditions of realisation are demanding. Realisation in knowledge requires the capacity, ability, and receptivity for delusion-free knowledge, non-otherness, devotedness, and love towards the ideal in the form of coexistence, and these are achieved through just behaviour, resolved thought, and full dedication in obedience to a realised human being (MVD, p. 317). Delusion-free knowledge is knowing, understanding, and imparting the understanding of realities as they are; non-otherness is its continuity; devotedness is the continuity of non-otherness, an earnest endeavour for freedom from delusion; and love is the continuity of non-otherness together with devotedness, the combined expression of kindness, grace, and compassion (MVD, p. 317). *Dharma*, unlike form, property, and essential nature, can be realised only through study and adherence to the step-by-step instructions of one who has attained realisation (MVD, p. 273).

The teacher's role is correspondingly large. The authentic human is the teacher in whose proximity study takes place (MVD, p. 150); in study imparted by the guru through the realisation-based way, justice, *dharma*, and truth are accepted in the student's *buddhi* upon direct recognition (MVD, p. 99); awakened humans are the natural guides of the unawakened, and humans aspire to awaken through their own inspiration and through the inspiration, grace, and compassion of those more awakened (MVD, p. 287); and the teacher is the bearer and carrier of the evidence of awakening, with the literature as its guideline (AVD, p. 261). Alongside these statements stand the principles of §1.6: each person examines any presentation and accepts or rejects it (AVD, p. 226), and commands remain information until they become studiable (AVD, p. 225). [The Epistemology of Coexistence](../The-Epistemology-of-Coexistence/The-Epistemology-of-Coexistence.pdf) §1.6 records the transmission requirements in outline, and §6.4 examines how the two sides of the teacher's role fit together.

### 1.13 How realisation is evidenced

Because the darshan refuses to let an inner state serve as its own credential (§1.3), it needs public marks, and it names three kinds of evidence: realisation, behaviour, and experiment (MVD, p. 12). The awakened human, not scripture or instrument, is the basis of evidence (SB, p. 5). Understanding must be conveyable: "The proof of our comprehension lies in our ability to convey it to others" (JV, p. 26), and making just behaviour, resolved thought, and realisation in truth understandable, knowable, and practicable through language is the work of realised humans (MVD, p. 316). Conduct must change: integrality is justice, *dharma*, and truth-based behaviour, thought, and realisation together (MVD, p. 303); the absence of mistakes and crimes in behaviour characterises complete awakening (MVD, p. 285); and the ability to provide justice is the evidence of liberation from bondage (AVD, pp. 172–173). Work with nature must succeed, since experimentation is the endeavour continued until natural abundance is made useful (MVD, p. 56). And the change must be reproducible in tradition: an awakened human evidences awakening by paving the path for the deluded (MVD, p. 273), and awakening completeness is evidenced in participation in the whole of orderliness together with expression, communication, and exposition (AVD, pp. 259–260).

The darshan grades what these marks show. Humans are perceived as animalistic, demonic, humane, deific, and divine according to their awakening (MVD, p. 314). Those who have realised the truth and live by justice and *dharma* are awakened, those whose thought and behaviour accord with justice and *dharma* are half-developed, and those who accord with justice alone are partially developed (MVD, p. 160). The darshan also states the criterion that is meant to make these marks evidence of truth rather than of competence alone: truth is what is uniformly realised everywhere (MVD, p. 35), and the authenticity of realisation is the same in, by, and for all human beings (AVD, p. 61).

On the darshan's own architecture these marks are not tests applied from outside to an inner event. *Praman* is the fourth stage of realisation itself (§1.11). §6.3 considers what follows for their evidential force.

### 1.14 The circle the darshan states itself

The dependencies of realisation are set out without concealing that they close:

> **“The ability of realisation in knowledge depends on human awakening; human awakening depends on sanskars; development of sanskars depends on environment, study & endeavour; environment, study & endeavour depend on humaneness and higher humaneness; humaneness and higher humaneness depend on the ability of realisation in knowledge.”**
> — MVD, p. 290

These dependencies connect realisation, formation, study, and conduct. Learning can begin through study and participation before complete awakening: the outward expressed values can be learnt before their counterparts are established in state ([Axiology Value Theory](../Axiology-Value-Theory/Axiology-Value-Theory.pdf) §§1.4, 1.8), and belief can follow knowing and knowing can follow belief (MVD, p. 12). The question of how a learner evaluates the teaching and progresses towards establishment is examined separately in §6.2.

### 1.15 What the darshan rejects

The account is defined as much by exclusion as by prescription, but what it excludes are paths and their assumptions, not the vocabulary of practice. Mystery-based idealistic contemplation failed to accomplish the study of the human being, as did materialism-centred ideology (MVD, p. 3). In place of devotion–renunciation, isolation, accumulation, comfort-seeking, and hedonism, the darshan presents coexistence and, within orderliness, resolution, prosperity, and fearlessness (SB, p. 5). Renunciation, non-accumulation, detachment, and devotion, pursued as ideals, have drawn immense courage and dedication and produced no undivided society (JV, p. 105), and the character of renunciation and of devotion-ism is itself individualistic (SB, p. 238). Idealism takes ideal persons as its evidence, with devotion and renunciation as the criterion for recognising them (AVD, p. 62). Faith, defined as accepting the existence of something without knowing it (AVD, p. 17), rests at its root on the expectation that wishes shaped by fear and temptation will be fulfilled (SB, p. 238), and adoration pleads with an omnipotent unknown to turn inability into ability and remains individual-centred (AVD, p. 233). Seeking refuge in God as the real father and mother yields no human (JV, p. 57). Instruction by sermon risks delusion (AVD, p. 225), *upasana* that takes the symbol for the goal is secondary and unsocial (KD, p. 42), and the absorbed state itself yields no knowing (§1.3).

What survives these exclusions is not a bare minimum. Devotion, reverence, dispassion, restraint, concentration, and union are kept and redefined by understanding and conduct (§1.8), and most of them reappear among the conducts of awakened *jeevan* (§1.9). The path that remains is study of a stated system, the refinement of tendency, the activation of all ten activities, and evidence in relationship and conduct. It removes the traditional shortcuts, including the one the founder was told was the only way.

### 1.16 Method, evidence, and what Madhyasth Darshan establishes

Separating the kinds of claim matters here more than in most of these studies, because the founder's report does evidential work throughout.

Some claims are **first-person historical report**: that *samadhi* was attained and contained no knowing event; that *samyama* was redesigned, directed at space, and produced realisation of coexistence (MVD, pp. 4, 7; JV, pp. 12–14; AVD, p. 72). These are testimony about how the system arose — strong evidence of what one careful practitioner found, and no evidence that others would find the same.

Some are **definitional within the system**: spirituality as understanding of the omnipresent (AVD, p. 236); realisation as realisation in coexistence and as the satisfaction of definitive understanding (MVD, p. 316; AVD, p. 224); mystery as incapacity to attain or convey understanding (MVD, p. 273); *upasana* as refinement of the fundamental tendencies (KD, p. 30); concentration as focusing the faculties for understanding and evidence (MVD, p. 284); *bhakti* as liberation from fear and delusion expressed in dedication (MVD, p. 339); *yog* as union (MVD, p. 42).

Some are **structural claims about *jeevan***: ten activities, four and a half effective in delusion, and 122 conducts in awakening (JV, p. 73; MVD, pp. 79, 323). They depend on the darshan's account of the *jeevan* atom, examined in [The Ontology of Coexistence](../The-Ontology-of-Coexistence/The-Ontology-of-Coexistence.pdf) and [The Epistemology of Coexistence](../The-Epistemology-of-Coexistence/The-Epistemology-of-Coexistence.pdf).

Some are **methodological prescriptions** that could be followed and reported on: the two paths and their programmes (MVD, pp. 280–282), the conditions and supports of reflection (MVD, p. 284), study together with inquiry (AVD, p. 225), and experimentation continued to success (MVD, p. 56).

And one is a **negative epistemic claim of general force**: that a contentless absorbed state cannot serve as evidence. This is the darshan's most transferable contribution, and it does not depend on accepting anything else in the system.

A further class enters with §§1.11 and 1.12: **recorded oral teaching** from a 2010 session at Amarkantak. It corroborates printed doctrine at several points — that realisation adds nothing to what *sakshatkar* recognises, and that exploration is not the general route — and it states some things not located in the printed texts reviewed here, such as the names *drashtapad* and *jagriti* for the third and fourth stages and the automatic succession of stages after *sakshatkar*. The Editorial Notes set out how this material is used.

## 2. The Advaita Vedanta Answer

Advaita Vedanta holds that liberation is not produced by practice at all, because it is not a state to be attained. What practice does is remove the ignorance that obscures what is already the case. Every feature of its path follows from that, and it makes Advaita the sharpest possible contrast to a darshan whose founder went looking for an event and reported not finding one.

### 2.1 *Moksha* as eternally accomplished

Shankara's *Brahma Sutra Bhashya* treats liberation as eternally accomplished, obscured by *avidya*, and disclosed by knowledge (BSB 1.1.4). The self is not altered by realisation; the superimposition of self and not-self is removed, which is the subject of the *Adhyasa Bhashya* prefacing the commentary. Because the goal is not produced, practice cannot be its efficient cause — a point Advaita presses against ritualist and yogic accounts alike (BSB 1.1.4, 2.1.3).

This is where the traditions are closest. Both hold that the reality to be realised is already present: for Advaita the self was never other than Brahman, and for Madhyasth Darshan no unit is ever separate from the omnipresent reality whose realisation is the human goal (MVD, pp. 302–303, 317). Both deny that an achieved state is the goal. They differ over what the reality is, what the knower is, and what the world is. Advaita identifies the self with Brahman and treats the world as *mithya*. Madhyasth Darshan holds that *atma* — the seer, the self, the seat of knowledge — is the nucleus of a finite *jeevan* unit saturated in the omnipresent (MVD, p. 210), that Brahma is truth and the world is perpetual (MVD, p. 13), and that realisation requires a capacity that must be developed and evidenced (MVD, p. 302).

### 2.2 Qualification before instruction

Advaita is exacting about what a seeker must bring. The *sadhana-chatushtaya* comprises discrimination between eternal and non-eternal (*viveka*), dispassion (*vairagya*), the six treasures beginning with tranquillity and self-restraint, and the intense desire for liberation (*mumukshutva*); the *Vivekachudamani* sets out these qualifications and the supporting virtues (vv. 17–20, 69, 82). The Gita's list of divine qualities serves the same preparatory function (16.1–16.3).

The role is precise: these qualify a person to receive teaching and do not themselves constitute knowledge. Advaita therefore has a two-stage structure — become fit, then be taught — with an explicit account of what fitness consists in. Madhyasth Darshan also states preconditions — the capacity for delusion-free knowledge, non-otherness, devotedness, and love towards the ideal, achieved through just behaviour, resolved thought, and dedicated obedience to a realised human (MVD, p. 317) — but it places them before realisation rather than before instruction, and it holds that the capacity for realisation is the right of every human being (MVD, pp. 285, 302).

### 2.3 Hearing, reflection, and sustained contemplation

The path proper is *shravana*, *manana*, and *nididhyasana* — hearing the teaching from a qualified teacher, reasoning it through until doubt is removed, and sustained contemplation until it is no longer merely understood but immediate. The Brihadaranyaka's instruction to Maitreyi is the classical locus (BU 2.4.5). *Sruti* is the means of knowledge for what perception and inference cannot reach, with reasoning subordinate and confirmatory.

The negative discipline is *neti neti* — not this, not this (BU 2.3.6) — and the identification is *tat tvam asi* (CU 6.8.7). What removes ignorance is a knowledge event mediated by language, which is why Advaita can be uncompromising about scripture while remaining unimpressed by absorption.

### 2.4 The disputed status of *samadhi*

Advaita's own position on absorbed states is more divided than popular accounts suggest, and the division matters for the comparison. Shankara subordinates *yoga* to knowledge: *samadhi* may steady the mind but does not by itself remove ignorance, since what removes ignorance is knowledge and nothing else (BSB 2.1.3). Later texts within the tradition are warmer — the *Vivekachudamani* praises absorption and treats it as near-indispensable, and its authorship is disputed for this among other reasons. The *Mandukya* material on the fourth state, *turiya*, describes a condition beyond waking, dream, and deep sleep (MU, vv. 3–7), which is not the same as recommending a technique for reaching it.

Nagraj's finding in §1.3 bears on Advaita practice as he encountered it. His doubts began with the Vedanta literature on bondage and liberation, his practice followed the instructions for *nididhyasan* as well as for *samadhi*, and he counts the resulting thoughtless state under both names (AVD, p. 72). On Shankara's account *nididhyasana* is sustained contemplation of the meaning of the teaching, not thoughtlessness, and that *samadhi* contains no knowing event is what one should expect. The finding therefore does not straightforwardly refute Advaita, and the disagreement turns partly on what *nididhyasana* is. Where the traditions genuinely part is on what does the work of knowing: for Advaita, *sruti*-mediated knowledge culminating in immediacy; for the darshan, study culminating in definitive understanding, its satisfaction in realisation, and its evidence in conduct.

### 2.5 After realisation

The *jivanmukta* continues to act, and the Gita supplies the account: prescribed action (3.8) performed without attachment (3.9, 3.19), and action for the holding-together of the world (3.20, 3.25–26). Conduct expresses realisation without being its cause or its evidence in the darshan's sense. Advaita does not require the liberated person to establish an order, transmit a system, or produce social outcomes, and it does not treat failure to do so as counting against realisation.

Madhyasth Darshan uses the same name, *jeevan-mukta*, for one freed from delusion while living, and gives it the opposite emphasis. The *jeevan-mukta* is free of suffering over past and future, and nurturing and advancing society is their essential nature (MVD, pp. 81, 157); the one who is evidence in *kaivalya* has no need of further bodily journeys and remains an inspiration after the body dies (AVD, p. 261).

### 2.6 Method, evidence, and what Advaita establishes

Advaita's method is scriptural testimony for the supersensible, disciplined reasoning against it, and contemplation to make it immediate, with qualification as a precondition. Its enduring achievements here are two: the argument that nothing produced can be the final good, hence no state is liberation; and a rigorous account of how a knowledge event, rather than an experience, could remove ignorance. What it does not offer, by its own lights, is public evidence of realisation. The criterion is the knower's own conviction: when someone knows in his heart that he has realised Brahman while still holding the body, no one else can deny it (BSB 4.1.15). The Gita's characteristics of the one steady in wisdom describe such a knower (BG 2.55–72), but Shankara presents them as disciplines for the seeker rather than as tests of another's knowledge (BG 2.55, commentary). The tradition accepts this internal criterion rather than treating it as a gap.

## 3. Modern Western Philosophy and Contemplative Science

The subject is not outside modern philosophy's range; it has been worked on from several directions, and the results are unusually relevant to the claims in §1.

### 3.1 Philosophy as spiritual exercise

Hadot's *Philosophy as a Way of Life* argued that ancient philosophy was not primarily a body of doctrine but a set of *spiritual exercises* — attention, meditation on death, examination of conscience, memorisation of precepts — whose aim was the transformation of the practitioner. Doctrine served practice rather than the reverse. Foucault's late lectures on the care of the self and *askesis* develop the same material toward techniques of self-formation.

This displaces the assumption that "spiritual practice" names something religious and other than philosophy. It also supplies the closest Western structural analogue to the darshan's position: study whose point is transformation, doctrine that is not idle, exercises that are not withdrawal from the world. Hadot's ancients, like this darshan, treat the philosophical life as continuous with conduct in the city rather than as preparation for leaving it.

### 3.2 Religious and mystical experience: perennialism against constructivism

James's *Varieties of Religious Experience* established the field by treating such experiences as data with describable marks — ineffability, noetic quality, transiency, passivity — and by taking their fruits as the test of their worth. Stace's *Mysticism and Philosophy* argued for a common core across traditions, distinguishing extrovertive from introvertive mysticism and treating the latter as a contentless unity underlying diverse interpretations. Katz's "Language, Epistemology, and Mysticism" reversed this: there are no unmediated experiences, and a tradition's concepts shape the experience itself, not merely its report. Forman and others replied by defending a pure consciousness event stripped of content (SEP Mysticism).

The dispute bears directly on the darshan's negative finding. Nagraj reports a state in which hope, thought, and desire were silent, the awareness of "I am" remained, nothing further occurred, and nothing could be described (MVD, p. 7; JV, pp. 12–13). That is close to what perennialists describe as a pure consciousness event, and it is precisely what a constructivist would predict a Vedically and tantrically trained practitioner to reach and to find unhelpful. That the same report can be read as evidence for either position shows how little a phenomenological description settles.

### 3.3 The epistemology of religious experience

Alston's *Perceiving God* is the most developed defence of the idea that experience of God can be a source of justified belief, arguing that "mystical perceptual practice" is not shown unreliable merely because it lacks the cross-checking available to sense perception, and that demanding such checks begs the question. The standard objections are the absence of independent verification, the diversity of incompatible reports across traditions, and the difficulty of specifying reliability conditions (SEP Religious Experience).

Madhyasth Darshan sits at an odd angle to this literature. It agrees with the critics that a contemplative state without describable content cannot be evidence — indeed it says so more sharply than the critics do. But it then claims realisation with statable content and offers conveyability, conduct, and experiment as the cross-checks Alston is accused of lacking. Whether those checks are the right kind is §4.4's question.

### 3.4 Transformative experience

Paul's *Transformative Experience* formalises a problem the contemplative traditions have always faced without naming it. Some experiences are *epistemically* transformative: one cannot know what they are like, or what one will value afterwards, without having had them. Decisions about whether to undergo them therefore cannot be made by ordinary expected-value reasoning from present values, because the relevant values are among the things that change.

This is precisely the structure of a call to practice. The darshan's demand that understanding be conveyable before it is accepted is an attempt to escape it — to make the case in terms available to the unawakened. Paul's analysis suggests why that is hard: if realisation changes what one counts as evidence and what one values, the pre-realisation assessment cannot be authoritative and the post-realisation assessment cannot be neutral. Advaita's qualification requirement (§2.2) is the same problem handled by admission criteria rather than by argument.

### 3.5 Contemplative science and its reckoning

Empirical work on meditation has established real effects and has also undergone a methodological correction that matters more than the effects. Neuroimaging has found trait and state differences in long-term practitioners (Lutz et al. 2004), and clinical trials support moderate benefits for some conditions. But Van Dam and colleagues' "Mind the Hype" documented systematic problems across the field — inconsistent definitions of mindfulness, weak or absent active controls, small samples, and publication bias — and Lindahl and colleagues' study of the varieties of contemplative experience catalogued a wide range of adverse and destabilising effects in Western Buddhist practitioners, effects that traditional literatures acknowledge and modern secular programmes had largely omitted. Thompson has argued that findings of this kind cannot carry the epistemic conclusions sometimes drawn from them (Thompson 2020).

Two conclusions bear on this study. Contemplative practice demonstrably changes people, so a tradition claiming transformation is not making an empty claim. And nothing in this literature reaches the epistemic question: measuring that a practice alters attention, affect, or self-report says nothing about whether the resulting understanding is true. The science can corroborate the darshan's claim that practice transforms and is structurally unable to corroborate its claim that the transformation is a knowing.

### 3.6 Method, evidence, and what modern work establishes

Modern philosophy establishes that transformation-oriented practice belongs to philosophy's own history rather than outside it; that no description of a contemplative state settles whether its content is given or constructed; that the demand for cross-checking is reasonable but not obviously decisive against experiential justification; and that appraising transformative experience from outside is structurally difficult. Contemplative science establishes that practice has measurable effects, that the field's early claims outran its methods, and that adverse effects are real. None of it establishes what realisation is or whether anyone has attained it.

## 4. Comparison

### 4.1 Criteria for comparison

The three positions are compared on eight questions: what the goal is; whether it is already present; what practice produces; what the decisive means is; the status of absorbed states; what counts as evidence of attainment; the place of conduct and relationship; and what liberation and the world afterwards are.

### 4.2 Cross-tradition summary

| Question | Madhyasth Darshan | Advaita Vedanta | Modern philosophy and science |
|---|---|---|---|
| The goal | Realisation of existence as coexistence — of all units saturated in the omnipresent; liberation from delusion | *Moksha* — recognition of what is eternally the case | No settled goal; transformation of the practitioner is the studied phenomenon |
| Is the goal already present | Yes — the omnipresent is inherent in every unit; only its realisation is to be attained | Yes — the self was never other than Brahman | Not addressed |
| What practice produces | The capacity, ability, and receptivity for realisation; the awakening of all ten activities of *jeevan* and their evidence in 122 conducts | The removal of ignorance; the goal is unproduced | Measurable change; whether it produces knowledge is untouched |
| Decisive means | Study, which is worship; refinement of the fundamental tendencies; inward regulation and concentration for understanding; *sakshatkar* as the success of study | *Sruti*-mediated knowledge made immediate by contemplation | Spiritual exercises (Hadot); no consensus on epistemic means |
| Absorbed states | Silent; self-limited happiness at most; no knowing; cannot serve as evidence; not recommended | Steadying at most; Shankara subordinates them to knowledge, later texts warmer | Contested between perennialist and constructivist readings |
| Evidence of attainment | Realisation, behaviour, and experiment; conveyability, absence of mistakes and crimes, the ability to provide justice, reproducibility in tradition — as the final stage of realisation, not external checks | The knower's own conviction, which no one else can deny; the marks of the one steady in wisdom describe the knower and serve the seeker as disciplines, not as tests | Behavioural and neural measures of change; no measure of truth |
| Conduct and relationship | Constitutive — sociality contained in *upasana*; many awakened conducts are directed to others | Expressive — *loka-sangraha*, neither cause nor evidence | Central for Hadot's ancients; peripheral in contemplative science |
| Liberation and the world afterwards | *Jeevan-mukta* nurturing society; *kaivalya* as evidenced participation in orderliness, with no further bodily journeys; the world real, to be ordered through undivided society | *Jivanmukti*; the world *mithya* at the final level; engagement continues without ultimate standing | Not addressed |

### 4.3 The status of the absorbed state

This is where the comparison is most instructive, because the apparent clash is smaller than it looks and the real one is elsewhere. Nagraj's report that *samadhi* contained no knowing event is not a refutation of Shankara, who holds that knowledge and not absorption removes ignorance. Both are against the same target: the idea that an achieved state is itself the goal. The report does reach an Advaita practice as Nagraj understood it, since he grouped *nididhyasana* with *samadhi* as states of thoughtlessness (AVD, p. 72); an Advaitin would reply that this is not what *nididhyasana* is.

The genuine divergence is over what replaces the absorbed state. Advaita replaces it with a knowledge event whose means is scriptural testimony made immediate by contemplation, and accepts that the criterion of success is internal. The darshan replaces it with study leading to definitive understanding and its satisfaction in realisation, and insists the criterion of success is public — conveyability, conduct, and experiment. So the traditions agree in their negative diagnosis and in holding that the reality to be realised is already present, and differ completely on what a positive means may consist in and on whether realisation owes anyone public evidence.

### 4.4 What could count as a check

All three positions face the cross-checking problem and answer differently. Advaita accepts an internal criterion and is consistent in doing so. Alston argues that demanding sense-perception-style checks of mystical practice begs the question. The darshan tries to supply external checks, and it supplies more than conveyability: evidence in behaviour and in experiment with nature, a criterion of truth as what is uniformly realised by everyone, and the requirement that the reality each word names be found in existence (MVD, pp. 12, 35; AVD, pp. 61, 235).

These checks establish more than coherence. They test just conduct, successful work with nature, and agreement among those who have studied. What they test less directly is the ontology conveyed — the *jeevan* atom, the saturation of every unit in space — because a learner checks each claim against existence as the darshan's own categories present it. This is not a reason to prefer Advaita's internal criterion, which only the knower can apply. It is a reason to say that the darshan's demand for evidence is a real advance in kind, whose force depends on how independent its checks are of the understanding they are meant to confirm (§6.3).

### 4.5 Transformation and the appraisal problem

Paul's analysis (§3.4) applies unevenly across the three, and the unevenness is illuminating. Advaita handles the problem by qualification: the *sadhana-chatushtaya* screens for those already disposed, so the appraisal question is not put to the unprepared. Hadot's ancients handle it by making the exercises continuous with ordinary life, so each step is appraisable from the one before. Madhyasth Darshan takes the second route and pushes it further. It requires that understanding be conveyable to the unawakened at every stage, that each learner examine what is presented, and that belief follow knowing (MVD, p. 12; AVD, pp. 225–226), and it names the marks of each step in the learner's own faculties — affection, peace, contentment, bliss (MVD, pp. 282–283, 327). These are its strongest anti-esoteric commitments.

The residual difficulty is that the darshan also asks for full dedication in obedience to a realised teacher (MVD, p. 317) and holds that correct evaluation occurs only through awakening (MVD, p. 203; [Axiology Value Theory](../Axiology-Value-Theory/Axiology-Value-Theory.pdf) §1.7). Those two commitments reintroduce from the other side the asymmetry conveyability was meant to remove.

### 4.6 Practice as private or social

On this the darshan is the outlier, and it is a genuine contribution. Advaita's path is compatible with solitude and its qualification list is a list of personal virtues. Contemplative science studies individuals and measures individual outcomes. Here real *upasana* is behaving with one's equal and practising towards greater awakening, and sociality is naturally contained in its results (KD, p. 37); *upasana* is education and orderliness in its own nature (KD, p. 42); and many of the 122 conducts it activates — kindness, grace, compassion, forgiveness, respect, affection, generosity — are directed to others (§1.9). Relationship is therefore not a field of application but the medium of the practice, and the darshan says so against the *samadhi* route, whose seeker cannot take part in family or society (AVD, p. 165). Hadot's ancients are the nearest analogue — philosophy practised in the city, with the school as its setting — though even there the exercises are individual and the community supports them rather than constituting them.

## 5. Critical Review

### 5.1 Madhyasth Darshan — a demanding standard, resting on one report

**Strengths.**

- The negative claim in §1.3 is a real epistemic advance and transferable to any tradition: a state with nothing describable in it cannot be evidence, including of itself.
- It is made from the inside, by a practitioner who reached the state he then declined to credit — which insulates it from the objection that critics of contemplative claims have not done the practice.
- The refusal of ineffability is disciplined and consistent. Knowledge is held to be manifest and effable and its evidence accessible through behaviour, so the tradition produces statable doctrine open to examination.
- The account of what practice changes is specific. The ten activities, the four and a half that are effective in delusion, and the 122 conducts of awakened *jeevan* give practice definite targets and definite marks, faculty by faculty.
- Failure has stated conditions. Reflection fails in the presence of crime, injustice, attachment, or ignorance, and each has a named support; *upasana* fails when aim, seeker, and means come apart (MVD, p. 284; KD, p. 38).
- Practice has no private phase; sociality is contained in it, which removes the standard problem of reconciling withdrawal with social obligation.
- The traditional vocabulary of devotion and discipline is kept and given a public content rather than discarded, which lets the darshan meet those traditions on their own terms.
- The dependency circle is stated in the darshan's own words (MVD, p. 290) rather than concealed.

**Weaknesses.**

- The founding evidence is a single first-person report, and the productive method is one the founder states he designed himself after rejecting the received account (JV, p. 13). Neither can be independently examined.
- The public marks test competence, conduct, and successful work more directly than they test the ontology conveyed (§§4.4, 6.3).
- The texts pull in two directions on the teacher: dedicated obedience and adherence to instruction on one side, each learner's examination and acceptance or rejection on the other (§6.4).
- The inverted *samadhi*–*dhyan*–*dharana* sequence and its object, space, are reported, not argued. Why realisation should follow from them is not explained.
- Nothing in the texts reviewed says how long practice takes, or how a learner can tell from within that the point of satisfaction is genuine; and the treatise devoted to the systematic study of the 122 conducts is not yet available in translation.

Madhyasth Darshan sets the most exacting evidential standard of the three positions here and grounds it on the least examinable foundation.

### 5.2 Advaita Vedanta — rigorous about the goal, internal about verification

**Strengths.**

- The argument that nothing produced can be liberation is powerful and unanswered by accounts that make the goal an achievement.
- Subordinating absorption to knowledge anticipates the darshan's negative finding by more than a millennium.
- The qualification structure is explicit about what a seeker must already possess rather than assuming availability on demand.
- *Shravana*–*manana*–*nididhyasana* is a genuine account of how a knowledge event, not an experience, could remove ignorance.

**Weaknesses.**

- The criterion of realisation is internal, so the tradition cannot distinguish realisation from confident self-ascription.
- Dependence on *sruti* limits the argument's reach to those who grant the source's authority.
- The world's *mithya* status leaves conduct expressive rather than evidential. The marks of the one steady in wisdom describe the knower, but nothing in a liberated person's life is offered as a test of the claim.
- The internal dispute over *samadhi* between Shankara and later texts is unresolved, which weakens appeals to "the Advaita position" on practice.

Advaita is the more rigorous of the two traditions about what the goal cannot be, and it answers the question of how anyone else could tell by holding that no one else can.

### 5.3 Modern philosophy and science — sharp on the problems, empty on the goal

**Strengths.**

- Hadot recovered transformation-oriented practice as philosophy's own history, which reframes the whole subject.
- The perennialist–constructivist dispute isolates exactly the question that first-person reports cannot settle.
- Alston forces critics to state why cross-checking should be required rather than assuming it.
- Paul's analysis explains why calls to practice cannot be appraised on the appraiser's current values.
- Contemplative science has corrected itself in public — "Mind the Hype" and the adverse-effects literature are the field policing its own claims.

**Weaknesses.**

- No convergence on whether contemplative states can justify belief, and each position's answer follows from prior commitments.
- The science measures change and cannot in principle reach the epistemic question, a limit sometimes obscured in how findings are reported.
- The literature is overwhelmingly built on Buddhist-derived and theistic practices, so its categories fit a darshan that rejects absorption as a goal and rebuilds devotion around understanding rather poorly.

Modern work states the problems in this area more clearly than either tradition and offers no account of what realisation would be.

## 6. Open problems for Madhyasth Darshan

### 6.1 The inverted sequence

The *samadhi*–*dhyan*–*dharana* order is given as the process for *samyama* (MVD, p. 7), undertaken to test *samadhi* (JV, p. 13) and directed at space (AVD, p. 72), without any statement of why that order on that object should work. Three readings are possible: that the absorbed state quiets the sensory circuit so directed attention can operate without interference; that the descent is incidental and the work was done by sustained attention regardless of order; or that the sequence records one person's route without general significance. The darshan's own vocabulary suggests a fourth. If *dhyan* is concentration for understanding (MVD, p. 284) and *dharana* is innateness (MVD, p. 43), the sequence would run from the silence of the faculties, through concentration for understanding, to the apprehension of each thing's innateness in the space that saturates it. That reading is not stated in the texts reviewed, and none of the four is argued there.

### 6.2 Entry into the circle

MVD, p. 290 states the mutual dependence of realisation, awakening, *sanskar*, environment, study, and humaneness. Following, emulation, and study provide a stated path (MVD, pp. 280–282), outward expressions of values can be learnt before establishment in state, and belief may precede knowing provided knowing follows (MVD, p. 12). These supply an entry into learning, and the printed texts settle that study, not the discoverer's exploration, is the route for everyone else (AVD, pp. 72–74, 225).

The remaining question concerns warrant and transmission. The system studied originates in the founder's exploration, and the exploratory route is not recommended to others. A public account should therefore show how learners can examine the teaching, identify an error in its presentation or application, and verify their progress through understanding and conduct. The texts supply the criterion that each person examine and accept or reject what is presented (AVD, p. 226), but they do not describe a procedure by which a community of learners corrects a teacher's presentation.

### 6.3 What the evidential marks establish

The darshan's evidential marks are richer than conveyability: realisation, behaviour, and experiment (MVD, p. 12); the absence of mistakes and crimes in behaviour (MVD, p. 285); the ability to provide justice (AVD, pp. 172–173); truth as what is uniformly realised by everyone (MVD, p. 35; AVD, p. 61); and the requirement that the reality each word names be found in existence (AVD, p. 235). Each tests something real. Conveyability tests coherence and pedagogical competence, conduct tests whether the understanding governs living, and experiment tests whether it succeeds in work with nature.

The difficulty lies in what they are meant to confirm. *Praman* is the fourth stage of the same sequence as *sakshatkar* (§1.11), so the marks are entailed by realisation rather than applied to it from outside. Conduct and successful work are compatible with an understanding whose ontology is mistaken, since people live justly and produce well on many metaphysics. Finding each named reality in existence presupposes that existence is presented in the darshan's categories. And agreement among learners trained in one system is not independent confirmation of that system, as the constructivist argument of §3.2 would predict. What would settle the question is a check on the content of *sakshatkar* that does not already rely on the understanding *sakshatkar* is supposed to deliver. Someone mistaken in believing that *sakshatkar* has occurred would, on the automatic reading of §1.11, acquire conveyable fluency and the conduct that goes with it, and the marks alone would not show the mistake.

The darshan's strongest reply is the invariance criterion. If realisation is the same in all who reach it and is of what is uniformly present everywhere, divergent realisations would expose error. That reply needs a public record of independent realisers whose accounts can be compared, and the texts reviewed do not provide one.

### 6.4 The teacher and the learner's own examination

The texts pull in two directions. On one side, realisation requires full dedication in obedience to a realised human being (MVD, p. 317), and *dharma* can be realised only through study and adherence to the step-by-step instructions of one who has attained realisation (MVD, p. 273). On the other, each person examines any presentation and accepts or rejects it (AVD, p. 226); a tradition's commands remain information until they become studiable in logic and behaviour (AVD, p. 225); instruction by sermon risks delusion (AVD, p. 225); and belief is to follow knowing (MVD, p. 12). These are consistent if the teacher's instructions govern the conduct of study while their content is established only by the learner's own examination and conduct. The texts do not say this in so many words, and the practical difficulty remains that a student cannot certify a teacher as realised without the understanding the teacher is supposed to impart.

Nagraj's recorded remarks point the same way. He describes his own presentation as proceeding by goodwill and friendship, with no *dadagiri* — no bullying — the bullying in question being the older way of saying "you will ask nothing" (26:50). He treats self-examination as the test of teaching, asking whether one says, does, and lives the same thing (31:15–32:48), and of his critical method he says that he reviewed ideas and did not go about reviewing persons (41:14). This is evidence about the founder's stance rather than a doctrinal statement about the teacher's role, but it places authority in what can be examined rather than in who says it.

### 6.5 How failure is diagnosed

The texts state the conditions under which the practice fails. Reflection fails when crime, injustice, attachment, or ignorance is present and its corresponding support — orderliness, social conduct, study and *sanskar*, inward regulation — is absent (MVD, p. 284); *upasana* fails when aim, seeker, and means are separated (KD, p. 38); and humans decline, while wanting awakening, for lack of understanding of the reflection and projection among their own faculties (MVD, p. 291). What is not stated is how these conditions are to be read from outside in a particular learner, how long a successful course of study takes, or what a practitioner should conclude from years of study without *sakshatkar*.

The automatic reading of the ladder sharpens the gap. If *bodh*, *anubhav*, and *praman* follow once *sakshatkar* succeeds (03:03), every failure downstream must be redescribed as a failure of *sakshatkar*, and therefore as study not yet having succeeded. The printed conditions of reflection partly answer this, since they name where in the circuit the failure lies, but they are stated for the faculties rather than for an observable course of study, and the corpus supplies no record of failures and their diagnosis against which the claim could be tested.

### 6.6 The detail of practice

For a study of spiritual practice the corpus contains more procedural content than a first reading suggests: the two programmes of the second path (MVD, p. 282), the four supports of reflection (MVD, p. 284), concentration for understanding (MVD, p. 284), the marks of each faculty's development (MVD, pp. 282–283, 322–323, 327), the refinement of the fundamental tendencies through *upasana* and restraint (KD, pp. 30–40), and the inventory of 122 conducts (§1.9). Its central instruction is study, and *sakshatkar* is the name of study succeeding (12:54). The absence of a meditative technique is principled: there is no procedure beyond study, refinement, and conduct to describe.

What remains missing is a curriculum and a timescale. Nothing in the texts or the session says in what order a learner should study the stated system, how the success of study is to be recognised before its fruits appear in conduct, or how long it should take. The treatise the darshan names for the systematic study of the awakened conducts, *Manav Sanchetnavadi Manovigyan* (AVD, p. 151), may supply part of this, but it has not yet been translated in this collection.

### 6.7 Adverse effects

The contemplative-science literature documents destabilising effects of intensive practice (§3.5), and Nagraj's own regime of twelve to eighteen hours daily for years (MVD, p. 7) is intensive by any standard. The darshan does caution against the route he took: the method of silence is against the natural manifestation of *jeevan* (AVD, p. 74), it leaves the practitioner without a programme of living and unable to take part in family and society (AVD, pp. 164–165, 220), and the tradition's own stories of bodies becoming inactive in *samadhi* are noted (AVD, p. 164). For its own recommended route it makes a strong claim: the progress of awakening involves no pain, and all pain belongs to wrongful activity and decline (KD, p. 36). That claim is testable against what the empirical literature knows, and a modern presentation of the darshan that recommends study and concentration owes readers that comparison.

### 6.8 Translation and source coverage

Much of the positive account rests on material available here only in working translation. The *upasana* chapter of *Manav Karm Darshan* is a machine-assisted working translation; for this study its key passages were compared with the Hindi source pages and found to support the readings used (Editorial Notes). The English of *Adhyatmvad*, the darshan's own treatment of spirituality, is a work-in-progress translation not yet peer reviewed, and *Manav Sanchetnavadi Manovigyan*, the treatise on the 122 conducts, has not been translated. The account of liberation, of study as practice, and of the activation of the faculties should be rechecked as those translations mature.

## 7. Conclusion

Madhyasth Darshan's account of practice is organised around a refusal. Its founder pursued the method he was told was the only way to know the unknown, reached the state it aims at, and reported that no knowing occurred in it and that there was nothing in it to describe or present as evidence. What replaces it is not a sparse residue but a full positive account. Spirituality is the understanding of the omnipresent reality in which every unit is saturated, and its accomplishment is *atma-bodh* together with realisation in coexistence. Realisation is the satisfaction of a definitive understanding of existence, *jeevan*, and humane conduct, and it is evidenced in behaviour, experiment, and tradition. Practice is study — study itself is worship — together with the refinement of the fundamental tendencies, the inward regulation of the faculties, and concentration for understanding. What practice aims at is the awakening of all ten activities of *jeevan*, of which only four and a half are effective in delusion, and their evidence in 122 conducts that include devotion, reverence, love, forgiveness, gratitude, and restraint. The traditional vocabulary of devotion, discipline, and liberation — *bhakti*, *dhyan*, *yog*, *vairagya*, *jeevan-mukta*, *kaivalya* — is kept and redefined by understanding and conduct. What is rejected is the path of mystery, faith without knowing, renunciation as an ideal, and the absorbed state as a goal.

Against Advaita the disagreement is narrower and deeper than it first appears. Both hold that the reality to be realised is already present, and both deny that an achieved state is the goal; Shankara's subordination of absorption to knowledge anticipates the darshan's negative finding. They part on what the self and the world are — Brahman and *mithya* against a finite *jeevan* saturated in the omnipresent and a perpetual world — on what does the work of knowing, and on whether realisation owes anyone public evidence. Advaita says it does not and is consistent; the darshan says it does, which is the more demanding position and the one that can fail.

Modern philosophy turns out to have a great deal to say. Hadot shows that transformation-oriented practice is philosophy's own inheritance rather than something outside it. The perennialist–constructivist dispute shows that the founder's report of a silent state can be read as evidence for either view, which is a caution about how much any such report can settle. Alston shows that the demand for cross-checking needs defending rather than assuming. Paul explains why a call to practice cannot be appraised on the appraiser's present values — the difficulty the darshan's conveyability requirement is designed to escape and does not entirely escape. And contemplative science establishes that practice measurably changes people while being structurally unable to establish that the change is a knowing.

The darshan's most transferable contribution is the negative claim: a contemplative state with nothing describable in it cannot serve as evidence, including of itself. That claim is made from inside the practice by someone who had done it, which is the strongest position from which to make it. Its positive account is more specific than a first reading suggests, since it names the faculties practice must activate, the conditions under which each step succeeds or fails, and the conducts by which awakening is to be recognised. Its cost is that its public marks test conduct and competence more directly than the ontology they are meant to confirm, that the teacher's authority and the learner's own examination stand in tension, and that its positive foundation rests on a single report and a method its author devised for himself. A reader who wants to test the darshan should test it where it asks to be tested: in whether study of its stated system brings the faculties into the order it describes, and whether the conduct that follows is the conduct it predicts.

## Appendix: Quick Glossary

### Terms in §1

- ***Adhyatma*** — spirit; the darshan's name for the omnipresent reality in which all units are saturated, from which *anubhavatmak adhyatmvad*, realisation-centred spiritualism, takes its name (AVD, pp. 235–236). The spiritual aspect of existence, as distinct from the deific (intellectual) and the metaphysical (insentient) (MVD, p. 285).
- ***Atma-bodh*** — enlightenment of the self; the spiritual accomplishment, reached through inward regulation and coming into effect together with Brahma-realisation, realisation in coexistence (MVD, pp. 283, 286).
- ***Sakshatkar*** — direct recognition, in *chitta* (MVD, p. 126); also rendered "revelation" (JV, p. 62). On oral testimony, the success of study (§1.11).
- ***Bodh*** — enlightenment; the stage in *buddhi* following *sakshatkar* (MVD, p. 126).
- ***Avdharana*** — definitive understanding: knowing, believing, and recognising (AVD, p. 224).
- ***Anubhav*** — realisation. Realisation in the ultimate truth which is in the form of coexistence (MVD, p. 316), an activity of *atma*; attaining the point of satisfaction in definitive understanding (AVD, p. 224). In the recorded session, also called *drashtapad*, the seat of the seer (§1.11).
- ***Praman*** — evidence; the fourth stage. Evidence is threefold — realisation, behaviour, experiment — and evidence is awakened tradition (MVD, p. 12). Its expression in conduct and orderliness is authenticity (*pramanikta*) (AVD, p. 224).
- ***Tadroop*, *tadakar*, *tatsannidhya*, *tadavalokan*** — absolute-accordance, absolute-resonance, absolute-connectedness, and absolute-observance with the ideal; the goals of pursuing the ideal, reached through realisation in coexistence. *Tadavalokan* is establishment in the seer status in coexistence, and the *tat-* prefix denotes awakening (MVD, p. 80).
- ***Ishta*** — the ideal: self-driven reverence, trust, and dedication towards coexistence together with complete knowledge (MVD, p. 80); awakening is the human ideal (MVD, pp. 306–307).
- ***Bal*, *shakti*** — the inexhaustible strengths (*mun*, *vritti*, *chitta*, *buddhi*, *atma*) and powers (hope, thought, desire, resoluteness, authenticity) of *jeevan* (MVD, pp. 75, 275; AVD, p. 151).
- **Ten activities** — taste and selection (*mun*), deliberation and analysis (*vritti*), contemplation and visualisation (*chitta*), enlightenment and resolve (*buddhi*), realisation and authenticity (*atma*) (JV, pp. 73, 92–93).
- **Four and a half activities** — the activities effective in delusion: selection, taste, analysis, visualisation, and deliberation confined to the pleasant, healthy, and profitable (JV, p. 73; AVD, p. 152).
- **122 conducts** — the detailed activities of awakened *jeevan*: two in *atma*, four in *buddhi*, sixteen in *chitta*, thirty-six in *vritti*, and sixty-four in *mun* (MVD, p. 323; AVD, p. 91).
- ***Ritambhara*** — resoluteness; the entire backdrop for the manifestation of the natural grandeur of truth, a resolve suffused with truth; distinct from *sankalp*, resolve (MVD, p. 76).
- ***Antarniyaman*** — inward regulation of *jeevan*'s powers, required for the reception of higher-conformance signals and for *atma-bodh* (MVD, pp. 77, 283–286).
- ***Dhyan*** — concentration: focusing *mun*, *vritti*, *chitta*, and *buddhi* for understanding, and for evidence after understanding and realisation (MVD, p. 284).
- ***Manan*, *tulan*** — ratiocination in *mun* and deliberation in *vritti*, the two stages preceding *sakshatkar* (MVD, p. 126).
- ***Bhas*, *abhas*, *prateeti*** — perception, comprehension, and awareness of realisation, where *vritti* meets *mun*, *chitta* meets *vritti*, and *buddhi* meets *chitta* (MVD, p. 99).
- ***Anusandhan*** — exploration; the first of the two paths, the route of a seeker accomplished in *samadhi* (MVD, pp. 280–281).
- ***Gyan-anubhuti*** — realisation in knowledge; the same realisation considered as the knowing of coexistence (MVD, pp. 290, 316).
- ***Rahasya*** — mystery. Not revealing understanding, not being able to, or not being able to attain it (MVD, p. 273); incompleteness in understanding the absolute state and activity (MVD, p. 209).
- ***Bhram*** — delusion: belief different from reality (MVD, p. 291). Its bondage is threefold, of hope, thought, and desire (AVD, pp. 155, 167).
- ***Bhram-mukti*** — liberation from delusion; the darshan's proposal for liberation (MVD, p. 149), identified with seedless thought and behaviour (MVD, p. 81). Freedom from the bondage of delusion is *moksha* (AVD, p. 84).
- ***Jeevan-mukta*** — one liberated from animal consciousness and delusion while living, free of suffering over past and future, whose essential nature is nurturing society (MVD, pp. 81, 157).
- ***Kaivalya*** — absoluteness; the state of realisation, with liberation from suffering, resentment, fear, and the bondage of delusion (MVD, pp. 317–318), evidenced as participation in the whole of orderliness (AVD, pp. 260–261).
- ***Samadhi*** — reported as the state in which hope, thought, and desire fall silent and in which no knowing event occurs (MVD, p. 7; JV, p. 13); self-limited happiness (*svantah sukh*) (AVD, p. 220).
- ***Samyama*** — the method Nagraj devised after rejecting Patanjali's, run as *samadhi*, *dhyan*, *dharana* and directed at space (MVD, p. 7; JV, p. 13; AVD, p. 72); also, as one of the 122 conducts, restraint: being regulated in humane thought, behaviour, and vocation (MVD, p. 337).
- ***Dharana*** — in this darshan's definitions, innateness, that which cannot be separated from a thing (MVD, p. 43).
- ***Upasana*** — the refining and transforming of the fundamental tendencies, and itself a form of study (KD, pp. 30, 38); education and orderliness in its own nature (KD, p. 42); of three kinds, *kutastha*, *rupastha*, and *atmastha* (KD, p. 31).
- ***Sadhana*** — effort towards self-improvement, whose point of fulfilment is awakening (AVD, pp. 233–234).
- ***Abhyas*** — practice: carrying the awakening attained into behaviour and teaching as comprehensive resolution (AVD, p. 234).
- ***Bhakti*** — devotion: the activity of becoming liberated from fear and delusion, the expression of prayer joined with *seva*, and an expression of total dedication (MVD, p. 339).
- ***Par-vairagya*** — supreme detachment: liberation from the flaws of excess, deficiency, and omission that stand against awakening (MVD, pp. 157–158).
- ***Yog*** — union, of two kinds, unification and togetherness (MVD, p. 42); pre-existing *yog*, the union with the omnipresent that is only realised, and attainable *yog*, the unions a unit enters (MVD, pp. 159, 302).
- ***Prayog*** — experimentation: the endeavour, continued until it succeeds, to instil usefulness and aesthetic value in natural abundance (MVD, p. 56).
- ***Chintan*** — contemplation. The awareness of truth (MVD, p. 316); the strength with which awakening begins (AVD, p. 151).
- **Realisation-based way** (*anubhav-moolak vidhi*) — the mode in which an awakened *jeevan* evidences all ten of its activities, and in which teaching is conducted (MVD, pp. 79, 99).
- **Integrality** — justice, *dharma*, and truth-based behaviour, thought, and realisation together (MVD, p. 303).

### Terms in §2

- ***Moksha*** — liberation; eternally accomplished and obscured by ignorance rather than produced.
- ***Avidya*** — ignorance, whose removal is liberation.
- ***Adhyasa*** — superimposition of self and not-self, the subject of Shankara's preamble to the *Brahma Sutra Bhashya*.
- ***Sadhana-chatushtaya*** — the fourfold qualification: *viveka*, *vairagya*, the six treasures, *mumukshutva*.
- ***Shravana*, *manana*, *nididhyasana*** — hearing, reasoning, and sustained contemplation.
- ***Turiya*** — the fourth state, beyond waking, dream, and deep sleep (MU, vv. 3–7).
- ***Jivanmukta*** — one liberated while living; conduct expresses realisation without evidencing it.
- ***Loka-sangraha*** — the holding-together of the world; the ground of continued action (BG 3.20, 3.25–26).

### Terms in §3

- **Spiritual exercises** — Hadot's term for the transformative practices of ancient philosophy.
- ***Askesis*** — training or discipline of the self; Foucault's care of the self.
- **Perennialism** — the view that mystical experience has a common core across traditions (Stace).
- **Constructivism** — the view that tradition-specific concepts shape the experience itself, not only its report (Katz).
- **Pure consciousness event** — a contentless state of awareness, defended by Forman.
- **Mystical perceptual practice** — Alston's term for the doxastic practice of forming beliefs from experience of God.
- **Epistemically transformative experience** — an experience whose character and value cannot be known before undergoing it (Paul).

## Editorial Notes

### The founder's report as evidence

§§1.3–1.4 rest on autobiographical passages in MVD, JV, and AVD. This study treats them as first-person historical testimony about how the darshan arose — strong evidence of what one practitioner found, no evidence that others would find the same — and says so in §1.16 and §5.1 rather than letting them function as demonstration. The Epistemology study takes the same line at its §1.6.

### Recorded oral teaching as a source

§§1.11, 1.12, 6.4, 6.5, and 6.6 draw on a recorded session of January 2010 at Amarkantak, *Sakshatkar – Bodh – Anubhav – Praman*, under four constraints. It never outweighs a printed text; where the two differ the text governs. It is cited by timestamp, so a reader can tell at a glance which claims rest on it. It is machine-transcribed Hindi that no Hindi speaker has yet checked against the audio, and the transcript records per-segment which passages are secure. And it is occasion-bound teaching, answering the people in the room rather than composed for publication.

Printed text corroborates two of its central points. Realisation as the point of satisfaction in definitive understanding (AVD, p. 224) matches the answer that nothing further is understood in *anubhav* beyond what *sakshatkar* makes firm (22:28), and the printed reasons for not recommending the *samadhi* route to others (AVD, pp. 72–74, 225) match its statement that the route is impractical in general (34:07). Three points were not located in the printed texts reviewed: the names *drashtapad* for *anubhav* and *jagriti* for *praman* (13:09, 17:30, 21:13), and the automatic succession of stages after *sakshatkar* (03:03–03:30). §1.11 reports them as the founder's teaching. One printed passage bears against too simple a reading of the first point: form, property, and essential nature can be understood, while *dharma* can only be realised (MVD, p. 273). That passage is compatible with realisation as the point of satisfaction, but it shows that *anubhav* is not an idle repetition of *sakshatkar*. The transcript, translation, and per-segment reliability marks are published as [*Sakshatkar – Bodh – Anubhav – Praman* (2010, Amarkantak)](https://analyticmadhyasthdarshan.org/References/Madhyasth-Darshan/Nagraj-Recorded-Sessions/Sakshatkar-Bodh-Anubhav-Praman-2010-Amarkantak/Sakshatkar-Bodh-Anubhav-Praman-2010-Amarkantak.pdf); the analysis behind these sections is in the companion research note alongside this file.

### *Tadroop-tadakar* and the seer status

The definition of *tadroop-tadakar vidhi* on MVD p. 80 belongs to the account of pursuing the ideal (*ishta-sevan*), whose four goals are oneness, accordance, abiding near, and continuous observance; establishment in the seer status is given there as the definition of the last of these, *tadavalokan*. This study therefore reads the sentence "Realisation in coexistence itself is the way of absolute-accordance and absolute-resonance" as the darshan's reinterpretation of devotional union with a chosen ideal, not as a general thesis that method and realisation are identical, and it cites the recorded session rather than p. 80 for naming *anubhav* as *drashtapad*.

### *Samadhi* in three vocabularies

*Samadhi* names different things in the three traditions compared here, and this study does not harmonise them. In MVD, JV, and AVD it is the state Nagraj attained and found without knowing, and in which *mun*, *vritti*, *chitta*, and *buddhi* have no remaining motion. In Advaita its status is internally disputed, subordinated to knowledge by Shankara and praised in later texts. In the modern literature it is one of several terms for absorption, with no settled referent across studies. Where §4.3 speaks of "the absorbed state", that is this study's neutral term and not a translation of any of the three.

### *Samyama*, *dharana*, and *dhyan*

*Samyama* appears in two senses: the method of §1.4, and restraint in humane thought, behaviour, and vocation as one of the 122 conducts (MVD, p. 337). §1.4 glosses the reversed sequence in the received sense of its terms and notes that the darshan's own definitions give *dharana* as innateness (MVD, p. 43) and *dhyan* as concentration for understanding (MVD, p. 284). Whether those definitions apply to the words in the autobiographical passage is not stated in the texts reviewed; §6.1 records the reading they suggest without adopting it.

### *Sadhana*, *upasana*, and the practice vocabulary

The darshan keeps and redefines the traditional names of practice rather than replacing them (§1.8). MVD and JV use *sadhana* for the practice Nagraj pursued and moved beyond, while AVD redefines it as effort towards self-improvement whose fulfilment is awakening (AVD, pp. 233–234). This study uses *sadhana* in that redefined sense where AVD is under discussion, and otherwise prefers study, *upasana*, and practice for the darshan's recommended course. *Upasana* is used in its redefined sense, with the traditional sense reported where the sources report it (MVD, p. 4; JV, p. 9). The same convention is followed in [Family Relationships and Values](../Family-Relationships-And-Values/Family-Relationships-And-Values.pdf).

### *Moksha* and *kaivalya*

AVD defines *moksha* as freedom from the bondage of delusion (AVD, p. 84) and says that *kaivalya* lies in awakening "and not in *moksha*" (AVD, p. 261). The second statement follows the statement that the positive form of liberation from bondage is awakening completeness (AVD, pp. 260–261). §1.5 reports both. This study reads them as distinguishing release from bondage from its positive completion; the texts do not state that reading, and the English of AVD is a working translation.

### The 122 activities and their inventory

§1.9 names representative pairs from the 122 activities. The complete tabulation, its bal–shakti assignments, and the points at which the tabulated assignment and the definitions diverge are recorded in [*The Sixty-One Activity Pairs of Jeevan*](../The-Epistemology-of-Coexistence/Research-Note-Activity-Pair-Inventory.pdf); this study does not depend on resolving those points. The translation renders the members of several pairs differently in different places. §1.9 follows MVD's definitions — devotedness for *anurag*, devoutness for *pujyata*, and fearlessness for *dhruti*, defined as the absence of fear (MVD, p. 324).

### Working translations and page conventions

MVD page numbers follow the printed page footers of the bilingual edition; where a Hindi passage and its English translation fall on facing pages, the page of the English is cited. The *upasana* chapter of *Manav Karm Darshan* is available here as a machine-assisted working translation. For this study the Hindi of KD printed pp. 30, 35, 36, 37, and 42 was compared with the rendered source pages and supports the translation at the points used. On p. 37 the Hindi reads *जिसमें सामाजिकता स्वाभाविक रूप से समाहित रहती है*, whose relative most naturally attaches to the direct fruit of *upasana* rather than to *upasana* itself; the same page defines real *upasana* as behaving with one's equal and practising towards greater awakening, which is the passage §1.7 relies on for the absence of a private phase. AVD is cited from the page numbers of Sanjeev Chopra's bilingual working translation (2023 edition), which has not yet been peer reviewed. *Manav Sanchetnavadi Manovigyan* has no English translation in this collection.

### Modern sources selected for relevance, not coverage

§3 does not survey the philosophy of religion or the psychology of meditation. It selects the four bodies of work that bear on claims made in §1 — practice as philosophy, the content of contemplative states, the justification of experiential belief, and the appraisal of transformative experience — together with the methodological correction in contemplative science. Fuller treatment of religious experience and divinity belongs to *God, Divinity, and the Sacred*.

### Advaita Vedanta (primary texts)

- **BSB** — Shankara, Adi. [*Brahma Sutra Bhashya*](https://analyticmadhyasthdarshan.org/References/Advaita-Vedanta/BSB-Brahma-Sutra-Bhashya-Gambhirananda.pdf). English translation by Swami Gambhirananda. Cited: the *Adhyasa Bhashya* on superimposition (preamble; §2.1); liberation as eternally accomplished and not produced by action (1.1.4; §2.1); the subordination of *yoga* to knowledge (2.1.3; §§2.1, 2.4); the knower's conviction, which no one else can deny (4.1.15; §2.6).
- **BU** — [*Brihadaranyaka Upanishad*](https://analyticmadhyasthdarshan.org/References/Advaita-Vedanta/BU-Brihadaranyaka-Upanishad-Madhavananda.pdf). English translation by Swami Madhavananda. Cited: *neti neti* (2.3.6; §2.3); hearing, reflection, and sustained contemplation (2.4.5; §2.3).
- **CU** — [*Chandogya Upanishad*](https://analyticmadhyasthdarshan.org/References/Advaita-Vedanta/CU-Chandogya-Upanishad-Gambhirananda.pdf). English translation by Swami Gambhirananda. Cited: *tat tvam asi* (6.8.7; §2.3).
- **MU** — [*Mandukya Upanishad*](https://analyticmadhyasthdarshan.org/References/Advaita-Vedanta/MU-Mandukya-Upanishad-Gambhirananda.pdf). English translation by Swami Gambhirananda. Cited: the three states and *turiya* (vv. 3–7; §2.4).
- **BG** — [*Bhagavad Gita with the Commentary of Shankaracharya*](https://analyticmadhyasthdarshan.org/References/Advaita-Vedanta/BG-Bhagavad-Gita-Shankara-Gambhirananda.pdf). English translation by Swami Gambhirananda. Cited: the characteristics of the one steady in wisdom, presented in the commentary as disciplines for the seeker (2.55–72, with the commentary on 2.55; §2.6); prescribed action (3.8; §2.5); action without attachment (3.9, 3.19; §2.5); *loka-sangraha* (3.20, 3.25–26; §2.5); divine qualities (16.1–16.3; §2.2).
- **VC** — [*Vivekachudamani*](https://analyticmadhyasthdarshan.org/References/Advaita-Vedanta/VC-Vivekachudamani-Madhavananda.pdf) (traditionally attributed to Shankara; authorship disputed). English translation by Swami Madhavananda. Cited as a later pedagogical witness: the fourfold qualification and supporting virtues (vv. 17–20, 69, 82; §2.2); the warmer estimate of absorption (§2.4).

### Related studies in this collection

- [*The Sixty-One Activity Pairs of Jeevan*](../The-Epistemology-of-Coexistence/Research-Note-Activity-Pair-Inventory.pdf) — the pair-by-pair inventory of the 122 activities of *jeevan* drawn on in §1.9.
- [*Jeevan Architecture*](../The-Epistemology-of-Coexistence/Research-Note-Jeevan-Architecture.pdf) — the faculties, the ten and the four and a half activities, and their reception conditions, related to §§1.9–1.10.
- [The Ontology of Coexistence](../The-Ontology-of-Coexistence/The-Ontology-of-Coexistence.pdf) — realisation in coexistence as an activity of *jeevan* (§1.14) and *jeevan*'s faculties (§1.8).
- [Axiology Value Theory](../Axiology-Value-Theory/Axiology-Value-Theory.pdf) — correct evaluation and awakening (§1.7), and education, establishment, and continuity through conduct (§§1.4, 1.8).
- [Family Relationships and Values](../Family-Relationships-And-Values/Family-Relationships-And-Values.pdf) — the natal family as where *upasana* is initiated, and its feedback circuit with external behaviour (*vyavahar*) (§1.4.2).
- *God, Divinity, and the Sacred* (Ongoing) — divinity, theism, and the sacred, which §3 deliberately leaves aside.
