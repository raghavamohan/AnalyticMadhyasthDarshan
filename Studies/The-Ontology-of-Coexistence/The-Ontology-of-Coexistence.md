**The question:** What is Existence? What exists? Does what exists begin at some time? Does the individual self (*jeevan*) begin or end with the body? Is the world finally real?

This study examines those questions in Madhyasth Darshan (Co-existentialism), as presented by Shri A. Nagraj, and compares its answers with Advaita Vedanta and selected modern philosophical and scientific approaches to physical reality, consciousness, and selfhood. The core claim about time is stated in §1.4 — that *kaal* is duration of unit-activity and *satta* is timeless as ground; full treatment of time (*kaal*), *trikaalabadh*, and spacetime physics is in [*Nature of Time*](../Nature-Of-Time/Nature-Of-Time.pdf).

## 1. The Madhyasth Darshan Answer

Madhyasth Darshan holds that existence is coexistence: the eternal, inseparable presence of formless Omnipresence (*satta*) and countless formful units of nature. The units are insentient and sentient, bounded and active, while Omnipresence is all-pervasive, non-transforming, and without activity of its own. Neither is produced from the other. Units do not first exist independently and later enter coexistence; they are present only as nature saturated in Omnipresence.

A unit becomes known through its form, its effects on other units, and its participation in an ordered whole. The same account connects the composition of the natural world with sentient life and the human capacity to understand and live in coexistence.

### 1.1 Coexistence: Omnipresence and units

Omnipresence is formless, all-pervasive, transparent, permeating, non-transforming, and immeasurable. It permeates every insentient and sentient unit (*paragami*) and is transparent in their mutuality (*paradarshi*), where units are imaged and recognised (MVD p. 32). These features describe one indivisible reality. Because it permeates a unit throughout, dividing that unit leaves every part within the same pervasive reality.

Omnipresence is also named Uniform Energy (*sam-urja*), Space, Knowledge, Consciousness, Absolute Energy, and Light (*prakash*). These names describe the same pervasive reality through different relations. Realisation in this pervasive reality is described as eternal light (*shashvat prakash*; PS pp. 80, 137). Omnipresence remains actionless and without motion, wave, or pressure. Knowing, believing, and realising belong to sentient units, called *jeevan*.

Nature is present as countless formful units within Omnipresence. Every unit is bounded from six directions and remains saturated in the pervasive reality. Units are distinct from one another by their boundaries, not by separation from Omnipresence. What appears as empty distance between units is the same pervasive reality in which both are present. Omnipresence has no absent place, including where no local unit is perceived.

The atom is the root unit, constituted by a nucleus and particles in orbits. At larger scales, atoms compose molecules, cells, bodies, and planets. Such an organised whole is a composite unit while its definite composition and conduct persist. When it disintegrates, that composite form ends and its constituents enter other relations.

Omnipresence is state-complete: it remains unchanged. Nature is state-dynamic: units remain active, and material configurations combine and separate. Change belongs to units and their configurations within perpetual coexistence.

### 1.2 Saturation: the ground–unit bond

The inseparability of Omnipresence and nature is called *saturation* (*sampriktata*): every unit is soaked, submerged, and surrounded in the pervasive reality. Picture a cloth immersed in water: water is within it, it is submerged, and water surrounds it on every side. The image helps distinguish the three relations. Saturation itself is the ever-present way a unit exists in formless Omnipresence.

Because Omnipresence permeates every unit, every fragment remains saturated however often a unit is divided. Madhyasth Darshan connects this inseparability with its claim that what exists is not annihilated (SB p. 57). Division changes the configuration while leaving existent constituents. The evidential reach of this argument is examined in §6.2.

Being soaked means that every unit is endowed with energy and force. Being submerged means that it is active and stands in mutual recognition with other units. Being surrounded means that it remains regulated in definite relations. These are inseparable aspects of the unit's presence in Omnipresence (SB p. 57; PS p. 114).

A unit is always active and endowed with the energy evident in its activity. It bears force in state and expresses relative power in motion. Recognition and fulfilment become evident in its interactions according to its constitution and order. Saturation, energy, force, recognition, and participation in orderliness thus describe connected features of its continuing existence (SB pp. 57–62, 69).

Absolute energy (*nirpeksha urja*) names the pervasive reality in which units are active. Relative energy (*sapeksh urja*) becomes evident through their mutuality, including pressure, waves, effects, heat, sound, and electricity (MVD p. 40). This is the source's ontological distinction; its relation to measured physical quantities and forces requires the further comparison in §6.2.

Every unit is manifest (*prakashman*) through its saturation in Omnipresence. The permeation of units by this state-complete reality grounds their force-endowment, activeness, and manifestness (AVD pp. 115–116; SB p. 248). Thus the unit's manifest presence among other units belongs to its inseparable bond with Omnipresence. In mutual facing, units are imaged to one another, and imaging provides the basis of mutual recognition (PS p. 121; SB pp. 248–251). The same inseparable coexistence is considered here through the unit's saturation and through its manifestation in mutuality.

In insentient nature, recognition takes the form of definite responsiveness. An atomic particle recognises another according to constitution, distance, force, and relation; fulfilment is the resulting definite conduct. Heat accepted and redistributed by matter, particles maintaining definite distances in an atom, and atoms combining in definite ways illustrate this responsiveness. Imaging supplies the accompanying account of how units are manifest to one another.

Activity includes effort, motion, and result together: effort as active tendency, motion as its state of change, and result as the condition attained. Every physical and chemical activity bears all three. Force is recognised in state and power in motion; relative energy becomes evident through the mutuality of units.

Being surrounded in Omnipresence establishes regulation. Units remain regulated and conserved through their mediative organisation. In an atom, the nucleus mediates generative and degenerative tendencies and regulates relative powers. This regulation becomes evident as law and definite conduct (§1.6.1).

Nature saturated in Omnipresence is also called knowledge: coexistence itself is the complete content available to be known. Units are manifest within it; understanding that content is an activity of *jeevan*. Existence is therefore intelligible and available for study.

### 1.3 The unit and its four aspects in mutuality

Every unit is manifest (*prakashman*) with four inseparable aspects: form (*roop*), properties (*guna*), essential nature (*svabhav*), and *dharma* (PS p. 119). Form is shape, volume, and density: the real framework by which a unit is bounded and individuated. The unit in its constituted configuration is the *bimb*; its image in mutual facing is *pratibimb*, and its being imaged is *pratibimban* (PS pp. 121, 135; SB pp. 249–250). What is imaged is a real unit with its own form.

Properties (*guna*) are the relative powers of a unit, inseparable from the unit that bears them (*guni*). They produce generative, degenerative, or mediative effects when units interact. Essential nature (*svabhav*) is the usefulness or characteristic contribution of those effects in a given order and context. *Dharma* is innateness: what is inseparable from the unit and fulfilled in its activity. Existence is common to all orders; growth, hope to live, and happiness are added cumulatively in the higher orders.

In the mutuality of units, light (*prakash*) also names imaging: a unit's imaging is *prakash*, and units remain imaged on all sides (MVD p. 45; PS pp. 119, 121). In mutual facing, imaging is the basis of recognition and is indispensable for units' becoming manifest to one another. This includes insentient units; imaging is an existential relationship before it becomes a subject of human perception or understanding. The same unit bears all four aspects, while the recognition and evaluation of those aspects take the forms available in each order (SB pp. 248–252).

Imaging and influence have distinct roles in this mutuality. Through imaging a unit is manifest to another; through its relative powers it exerts effects. Both belong to the account of insentient nature. In animal living, recognition of essential nature becomes evident in friendliness and opposition; in human mutuality, essential natures are exchanged as values and *dharma* is evaluated as resolution (SB pp. 249–252). The whole unit's manifestness supplies what is to be understood, while human understanding of it requires the knowing activities of *jeevan*. Its image alone does not constitute that completed understanding.

The peepal tree illustrates the four aspects:

| Aspect | Peepal-tree example |
|---|---|
| Form (*roop*) | A bounded biological composition with roots, trunk, branches, leaves, fruit, and seeds. The constituted tree is the *bimb*, imaged in mutual facing as *pratibimb*. |
| Property (*guna*) | Relative powers become effective in mutuality: a seed in conducive soil, water, and climate participates in growth; disintegration changes the effects its constituents exert. |
| Essential nature (*svabhav*) | Effects are vitalising or devitalising according to the affected body and conditions. Their usefulness is definite in that mutuality. |
| *Dharma* | Existence and growth. The living tree grows and produces seeds that carry its composition method forward. |

Every entity's characteristic contribution is manifest, communicated, and expressed according to its state, motion, developmental standing, and order (SB p. 179). Presentation (*prakashan*), communication (*sampreshana*), and expression (*abhivyakti*) are connected aspects of this manifestness. In insentient nature, they concern the unit's manifest form, properties, and participation in mutuality. Sentient *jeevan* manifests and communicates through a body, while its inexhaustible forces and powers are active and manifest through its inward and outward activities (SB pp. 52, 63). During human awakening progression, communicating and expressing capacity, ability, and receptivity are themselves aspects of manifestness (SB p. 66). Their fulfilment in awakened living carries understood coexistence into speech, work, and behaviour (§1.14).

Atomic constitution and motion-path differentiate a unit's form and the capacities through which its relative powers operate. Atomic kind, state, and measure vary with nuclear and orbital particle constitution. As a developing atom changes constitution, its form and properties change. Structure therefore bears and constrains what the unit can express.

Those capacities become effective in definite mutualities. *Guna* concerns the effect when units come together, *svabhav* its usefulness, and *dharma* the unit's innateness and fulfilment. Understanding a unit therefore includes both its constituted form and its relations with other units.

Given the participating units, their constitutions, state, motion, distance, and environment, insentient effects and conduct are definite. Different mutualities yield different definite effects. In human living, understanding and evaluation additionally guide how relationships are fulfilled.

Each unit is a whole along with its environment. It retains its constitution and capacities while participating in definite mutualities. Its characteristic identity in orderliness—its own “ness”—is expressed through its images, effects, and usefulness to others (SB pp. 51, 250).

The four aspects also describe the unit's activity. Effort is associated with *dharma* and essential nature; motion with essential nature and properties; result with form and, in the completeness account, immortality (SB pp. 60–62). The overlap matters: essential nature concerns both effort and motion. These are interconnected descriptions of an active unit, whose developmental goals are explained through constitutional, activity, and conduct completeness (§§1.7.1, 1.11).

Truth of a unit is stated in three complementary aspects (SB pp. 250–251; MVD pp. 88, 97; YAT). Inherent truth (*vastugat satya*) is the unit's own form, properties, essential nature, and innateness. Positional truth (*vastu-sthiti satya*) is that same unit in place, time, and direction—its mutuality with other units. Existential truth (*sthiti satya*) is nature saturated in Omnipresence: coexistence as the standing condition that is not itself timed. Knowing a unit completely means knowing what it is, how it stands among others, and that it exists in the pervasive ground.

In the natural state, a unit's activity accords with its innateness and definite conduct. In the excited state, pressure from a mutuality displaces its activity from that accord and carries the possibility of decline. In physicochemical exchange, units give and take and then reinstate natural-state motion. Development means movement toward fuller order-definite expression; decline means movement away from it. Units participate according to their constitution and order; some developing atoms attain constitutional completeness.

At the human order, definite bodily composition is not sufficient for humane conduct; knowing and believing must guide recognition, evaluation, and fulfilment.

### 1.4 Time, causation, conservation, and the perpetual world

Time is the reckoning of the duration of activity (SB p. 251). Recurrent activities provide its measures. Omnipresence, being without transformation, is not temporal in the sense in which unit-activity is measured; units and configurations undergo the changes through which duration is recognised.

Omnipresence is supreme cause (*mahakaran*) in the sustaining sense: every activity exists as nature saturated in it. Particular transformations occur through active units in mutuality. Composition, decomposition, and development belong to units, while the pervasive ground remains unchanged.

Existence is perpetual. Omnipresence and the underlying reality (*vastu*) of units neither arise from non-being nor pass into non-being. Form (*roop*), composition, and association change while the realities participating in change remain. Thus the decomposition of a compound or body ends that organisation while its constituents continue. “Brahma is truth, the world is perpetual” affirms both the pervasive ground and the reality of the changing world.

General indestructibility concerns what persists through changing configurations. The persistence of one organised individual requires a further account of its constitution; Madhyasth Darshan supplies that account for *jeevan* through constitutional completeness (§1.7.1).

### 1.5 Composition: atoms, molecules, and bodies

There is a natural inclination toward coexistence throughout nature. Atomic particles assemble into atoms, atoms into molecules, and molecules into molecular forms. Compositional progression becomes evident in chemical substances, biological cells, vegetation, animal bodies, and the human body. Each level depends on definite composition method and conducive environment.

Composition retains a dependence on its constituents: iron objects remain iron, and bodies built from biological (pranic) cells remain pranic compositions. Material composition can manifest as the pranic order and return to material constituents through disintegration (SB pp. 75–77). These changes supply bodies and environments, including the human body. Development within an atom is the further line through which sentient *jeevan* becomes possible (§1.7.1).

Mixtures and compounds should be distinguished. In a mixture, components retain their respective conducts. In a compound, components combine in definite proportion and establish a new bounded unit with a new constitution, relational capacities, and order-specific conduct. Biological composition transmits its method through seed and lineage. Bodies in animal and human orders are species-conformant compositions built from pranic cells; their sentience belongs to the *jeevan* joined with the body, not to composition alone.

### 1.6 The four orders of nature

Nature is organised into four orders: material, bio or pranic, animal, and knowledge or human. Higher-order bodies contain and organise material and pranic compositions, thereby evidencing existence and growth. In the animal and knowledge orders, hope to live and happiness belong to the *jeevan*–body joint form: the body provides the expression medium, while the further *dharma* is attributed to *jeevan*. The higher orders therefore include the lower-order *dharmas* while adding a further *dharma* and a distinctive mode in which essential nature is expressed.

| Order | Principal manifestation | Order-specific expression of essential nature | Dharma, cumulatively stated |
|---|---|---|---|
| Material (*padarth-avastha*) | Atoms, molecules, physical and chemical compositions | integration–disintegration | existence |
| Bio (pranic; *pran-avastha*) | Biological cells, vegetation, bodily composition | vitalising–devitalising | existence and growth |
| Animal (*jeeva-avastha*) | Body–jeevan joint form under species-conformance | cruel–uncruel | existence, growth, and hope to live |
| Knowledge (human; *gyan-avastha*) | Human body–jeevan joint form with knowing and believing | fortitude, courage, generosity, kindness, grace, compassion | existence, growth, hope to live, and happiness |

Integration and disintegration name effects among material constituents. Vitalising and devitalising name whether a biological or chemical composition supports or obstructs bodily nourishment and protection in the relevant context. Cruelty is intrinsically relational because it means causing suffering to another; non-cruelty is its order-specific complement. Humane essential nature is evidenced through qualities fulfilled in behaviour and relationship. These are definite forms of participation, although the human can misrecognise or fail to fulfil them under delusion.

The material and bio orders are insentient. The animal and knowledge orders are joint expressions of an insentient body and a sentient unit, *jeevan*. Hope to live is the animal-order dharma, expressed through the sentient unit. The animal body provides for hope-bound, species-conformant expression; the human body provides for imagination, freedom of action, knowing, believing, and the full possibility of awakening. The bodily-medium distinction is developed in §1.8. Happiness is the human dharma because the human seeks not merely continued living but resolution and the continuity of happiness.

The five *koshas* describe capacities distributed across these orders. Material nature is described through *annamaya* and *pranamaya*; pranic nature adds *manomaya*, the selective reception of nourishment. Animal and deluded human living include *anandamaya*, the expression of happiness and unhappiness. Awakened human living includes *vigyanmaya*, the capacity to receive essential knowledge (MVD pp. 49–50). This classification begins with material nature. It therefore differs both from the five faculties of *jeevan* (§1.8) and from the Advaita account of five sheaths covering the Self (§2.6).

Two developmental lines therefore meet in the animal and human joint forms: compositional progression supplies the body, while development in the atom supplies constitutionally complete *jeevan*. Constitutional completeness and awakening are developed in §§1.7 and 1.11.

Existential progression names the order in which these manifestations become possible under conducive conditions: material, pranic, animal, and knowledge. Individual compositions participate according to their constitution and environment. Biological compositions return to material constituents when they disintegrate, while many atoms continue in physical and chemical constitution.

### 1.6.1 Regulation, law, and conformance

Regulation is inherent in nature saturated in Omnipresence. Orderliness is self-regulation (*swatah-saspurt*) in the coexisting orders. Omnipresence is the sustaining condition; the mediative nucleus regulates activities within an atom; and order-specific conformance describes the conduct evident at larger scales.

Regulation becomes evident as law because every unit has provision for recognising and fulfilling according to definite constitution. The way of existence (*niyati-vidhi*) names the four modes of conformance (*anushangita*):

| Order | Conformance mode | Character |
|---|---|---|
| Material (*padarth-avastha*) | Result- or structure-conformance (*parinam-anushangita*) | Definite |
| Bio (pranic; *pran-avastha*) | Seed-conformance (*beej-anushangita*) | Definite |
| Animal (*jeeva-avastha*) | Species-conformance (*vansh-anushangita*) | Definite in body and inherited conduct-pattern |
| Knowledge (human; *gyan-avastha*) | Sanskar-conformance (*sanskar-anushangita*) | Conduct follows acquired acceptances and dispositions, which may express delusion or awakening |

Animal conduct includes inherited species-patterns accepted from gestation and followed after birth. Human bodily composition also remains species-conformant, but human conduct is principally *jeevan*-based and sanskar-conformant. *Sanskar* concerns acquired acceptances and tendencies that guide conduct. Education and tradition can therefore reproduce delusion or support awakening; humane education–sanskar makes understanding and the disposition to live by it available.

### 1.7 Two lines of development

Compositional progression builds molecules, cells, bodies, and their environments. Development within the atom changes an atomic constitution until it becomes complete. These two lines meet in animal and human living: composition supplies the body, while a constitutionally complete sentient atom, *jeevan*, operates through it. The body's organisation provides the medium through which sentient activity can be expressed.

### 1.7.1 Constitutional completeness (T1)

Development in the atom culminates when an evolving constitution becomes satisfied. Constitution-oriented atoms are described as hungry when they can absorb particles and undigested or overfull when they carry particles they seek to expel. Through absorption and displacement, atoms return to natural-state motion or participate in further constitution. When the particles required for a particular developing constitution are integrated, that constitution becomes complete.

Constitutional completeness is an irreversible transition. Up to that point the two kinds of change are tied together: in matter, qualitative change does not occur without quantitative change in constitution, which is why atomic kind and capacity track particle number. Completeness separates them. The particle constitution is closed: qualitative activity continues, but particles no longer increase or decrease.

The atom is then *jeevan*, with inexhaustible force and power expressed as hope, thought, desire, resoluteness, and evidence. It is liberated from molecular-bondage and weight-bondage and established in hope-bondage: it no longer participates as a weighable atom bound into molecular composition, while its sentient activity is organised around hope. The relation of these source categories to contemporary physical measurement remains open (§6.2.1). Constitutional completeness concerns this transition of an insentient atom into sentient status.

T1 establishes the sentient unit with its capacity for knowing. How fully this capacity is expressed also depends on its bodily medium. Animal bodies permit hope-bound, species-conformant expression; the fully developed human nervous system provides for the complete activity of understanding. The faculties through which *jeevan* operates are described next.

![Saturation and constitutional completeness: compositional development supplies bodies, while development in the atom reaches the constitutionally complete sentient unit jeevan; the diagram does not claim that sentience is produced by biological complexity](1-saturation-samprikt.svg)

This closed constitution supplies Madhyasth Darshan's specific account of individual persistence. The *jeevan* no longer gains or loses particles and remains free from molecular- and weight-bondage. The body is a decomposable pranic composition; *jeevan* is the sentient atom that operates through it and continues when the joint form ends.

### 1.8 Jeevan: structure and faculties

*Jeevan* is the constitutionally complete sentient atom; the body is the biological instrument through which it acts. As an illustration, the body serves as a vehicle and *jeevan* as its conscious driver and knower. The body supplies sense organs, the brain (*medhas*), and capacities for physical expression. The brain receives and translates signals between *jeevan* and the body. *Jeevan* supplies hope, thought, desire, resolve, comprehension (*bodh*), authenticity, and realisation. A bodily limitation constrains expression through that medium; delusion concerns how *jeevan* identifies itself and organises its activity.

The primary texts describe five inseparable strengths or faculties in *jeevan*. Atma is the nucleus; buddhi, chitta, vritti, and mun are associated with the first through fourth orbits. Each faculty's characteristic power has paired activities in projection (*paravartan*) and reflection (*pratyavartan*) (SB p. 63).

| Faculty and characteristic power | Projection | Reflection |
|---|---|---|
| Mun — hope | selection | taste-recognition |
| Vritti — thought | analysis | deliberation |
| Chitta — desire | visualisation | contemplation; direct apprehension (*sakshatkar*) |
| Buddhi — resoluteness (*ritambhara*) | resolve | comprehension (*bodh*) |
| Atma — evidence | authenticity | realisation |

These are the ten activities of *jeevan*. Every *jeevan* operates through all ten; their effective organisation and evidence in living differ. The same inexhaustible strengths and powers underlie the equal capacity of *jeevan* in every human (SB p. 91). Equal capacity does not imply identical present understanding or bodily ability. Projection is the outward evidencing direction: understanding is expressed through resolve, thought, selection, work, and behaviour. Reflection (*pratyavartan*) is the inward knowing direction: experience and study are accepted, compared, directly apprehended, comprehended, and realised.

Reflection concerns *jeevan*'s understanding. Imaging (*pratibimban*, §1.3) is a unit's presence in mutual facing; visualisation (*chitran*) is an activity of *chitta*. At the activity level, force (*bal*) names activity in state and power (*shakti*) activity in motion. The faculty-level strengths and their characteristic powers are distinguished in the table above.

The source also places five insentient forces alongside the sentient faculties: electromagnetic force with *mun*, gravitational force with *vritti*, weak interaction with *chitta*, strong interaction with *buddhi*, and mediative force with *atma* (SB pp. 84–85). This is a further relation proposed by the text. Whether it is a structural analogy or a literal physical correspondence, and what evidence would establish it, requires the assessment in §6.2.

Study proceeds inward by the reflection circuit. Truth in the form of coexistence is accepted through tasting in *mun*, deliberated in *vritti*, directly apprehended (*sakshatkar*) through contemplation in *chitta*, and comprehended (*bodh*) in *buddhi*. Buddhi's accordance with atma culminates in realisation. Projection expresses this understanding through authenticity, resolve, visualisation, analysis, and selection into living conduct.

Tasting (*asvadan*) at *mun* has sensory, value-based, and goal-based forms (*ruchi-mulak*, *mulya-mulak*, and *lakshya-mulak*). Sensory tasting concerns sound, touch, form, taste, and smell through the body. Value-based tasting concerns fulfilment in recognised relationships. Goal-based tasting concerns the purpose of activity; awakening makes that purpose definite as resolution, prosperity, fearlessness, and coexistence. Development changes the governing orientation from sensory attraction toward relationship-fulfilment and the human goal, so what is accepted at *mun* is no longer organised by bodily sensation alone.

In animal living, the body has a brain and sensory organisation but not the fully enriched nervous system of the human body. It therefore mediates hope-bound selection, taste-recognition, and species-conformant conduct without providing for the complete evidence of understanding. This is a limitation of bodily expression, not an animal's erroneous attempt at human evaluation.

The human body supplies the developed medium required for the full circuit, but bodily provision does not itself produce awakening. In deluded human living, *jeevan* identifies itself with the body and organises only four and a half activities effectively around bodily sensitivity: selection, taste, analysis, visualisation, and deliberation restricted to pleasant–unpleasant, healthy–unhealthy, and profit–loss.

Deliberation has six perspectives: pleasant–unpleasant (*priya–apriya*), healthy–unhealthy (*hita–ahita*), profit–loss (*labh–alabh*), justice–injustice (*nyaya–anyaya*), dharma–adharma, and truth–untruth (*satya–asatya*). Only the first three govern deliberation in deluded living; justice, dharma, and truth have yet to guide it. In this restricted sense, deliberation is called a *half* activity. The remaining activities are present but lack the content and coherent direction of understanding in conduct. The human recognises and fulfils sensory aims without joining them to complete knowing and believing.

The texts distinguish sensitivity (*sanvedansheelta*) from cognisance (*sangyansheelta*). Sensitivity is responsiveness through the body and senses; cognisance is *jeevan*'s understanding of relationship, value, and orderliness. Awakening does not eliminate sensitivity. It brings sensory recognition and fulfilment under the guidance of knowing and believing, so cognisance and sensitivity operate in balance rather than as rival capacities.

The direction of faculty regulation distinguishes stages of awakening. Higher-conformance (*purvanukram*) is guidance descending from *atma* through *buddhi*, *chitta*, and *vritti* to *mun*; lower-conformance (*paranukram*) is sensory drive pushing inward from the body (MVD pp. 207–208). Cognisance regulates sensitivity in the former; sensitivity organises the faculties around the body in the latter.

In the partially awakened condition, mun and vritti are driven by chitta. In the half-awakened condition, mun, vritti, and chitta are guided by buddhi, while resolve remains without self-realisation. In awakening, atma regulates and disciplines buddhi, chitta, vritti, and mun; knowing, believing, recognising, and fulfilling become coherent. Study supplies the content of understanding; practice evidences and stabilises it in behaviour.

The seer–doer–enjoyer account describes three coordinated roles of the same *jeevan*: it knows, acts through work and behaviour, and undergoes the results. Under delusion, doing and undergoing continue while the seer-status remains incompletely evidenced. Awakening establishes *jeevan* as seer through understanding coexistence and itself as the knower; this understanding then guides doing and enjoying toward mutual satisfaction (MVD pp. 115, 288–289; KD 3.17–3.18).

### 1.9 Jeevan harmonies and experience

Happiness, peace, contentment, and bliss are *jeevan* values: harmonies between adjacent faculties, each experienced at the outer faculty of its pair. Happiness is the harmony of *mun* with *vritti*, peace of *vritti* with *chitta*, contentment of *chitta* with *buddhi*, and bliss of *buddhi* with *atma*. These are also described as four stages of happiness.

Each harmony is borne at the outer faculty of its pair: happiness at *mun*, peace at *vritti*, contentment at *chitta*, and bliss at *buddhi* (MVD pp. 328–346). For example, peace at *vritti* is the absence of resistance between desire and thought, the orientations of *chitta* and *vritti*. Naming its location and naming the faculties in harmony thus describe the same activity. Realisation at *atma* is ultimate bliss (*paramanand*), whose effect reaches the remaining faculties (MVD pp. 100–101).

The movement is not a sequence of temporary moods. It describes progressively complete coherence in the activity of *jeevan*. Curiosity in mun, enthusiasm in vritti, delight in chitta, elation and immersion in buddhi, and realisation in atma name the inward channel of energies toward awakening. Inward regulation is the discipline of the orbital faculties by mediative atma, followed by regulation of bodily activity through understanding.

The four *jeevan* values correspond to human goals in living:

| Jeevan value | Borne at | Faculty harmony | Human goal and field of evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Happiness | mun | mun–vritti | Resolution in the person |
| Peace | vritti | vritti–chitta | Resolution and prosperity in the family |
| Contentment | chitta | chitta–buddhi | Resolution, prosperity, and fearlessness in social participation |
| Bliss | buddhi | buddhi–atma | Resolution, prosperity, fearlessness, and coexistence in living |

The settings in which understanding is expressed and assessed widen cumulatively: family prosperity includes resolution, fearless social participation includes resolution and prosperity, and living in coexistence includes all three (KD 3.12, printed p. 112; JV p. 61). These are principal correspondences between inner harmony and living. They do not confine a value to one social setting or make a faculty pair the cause of an institution.

![Happiness toward bliss: harmony within jeevan becomes evident through resolution, prosperity, fearlessness, and coexistence; this is the felt and lived expression of activity and conduct completeness, not a fourth completeness transition](1-happiness-bliss.svg)

Awakening develops through repeated reflection and projection. By reflection, one understands; through projection, that understanding is made evident. Evidence widens from one issue to further issues, each accomplished understanding becoming the basis for the next projection (KD 3.12). Book knowledge alone is not the completion of this process. Study, practice, work, behaviour, result, and purpose must become coherent.

*Jeevan* remains active through bodily sleep and, according to MD, persists after bodily death. Its subsequent association with another body is also affirmed (JV pp. 20, 55). These claims rest on the doctrine of constitutional completeness; their relation to public evidence is considered in §6.2.

### 1.10 Karma: desire, activity, and result

Effort–motion–result is the structure of *kriyā*, or activity, throughout nature. In the sentient unit it is expressed through projection and reflection. When the human intends and acts through the body, the same structure is studied as desire, karma, and result.

Activity together with aspiration is *karma*; motion together with awareness is called complete activity (KD Ch. 1, printed pp. 1–2). Every karma has a result. Its five limbs are doer, cause, objective, result, and effect. These distinguish the actor, motive, intended aim, attained result, and wider consequence. The balance of desire, karma, and result is accomplishment of the desire. “Complete activity” here concerns awareness in action; completeness of understanding is developed in §1.11.

Karma is human activity under freedom of action. Deluded humans are independent while performing actions but dependent while undergoing their results. In awakened living, understanding precedes action and the attained result is evaluated and received within resolution. This is independence in both doing and undergoing: consequences remain, while their reception is free of unresolved contradiction. Living proof requires understanding to regulate conduct and complete the projection–reflection cycle.

*Prarabdha* names results still to be undergone when knowing, desiring, doing, and enjoying have differed (MVD p. 91). It locates unresolved consequences within this account of action and result. Advaita's use of the term for already-begun karma sustaining embodied life after knowledge belongs to a different account (§2.9).

### 1.11 Awakening: activity and conduct completeness

Nature saturated in state-complete Omnipresence is oriented toward development and awakening until realisation in Omnipresence. Development in its full sense includes constitutional completeness, activity completeness, conduct completeness, and their continuity. Awakening is resolution and authenticity. This direction is fulfilled according to the constitution and order of the participating unit.

The three developmental goals are immortality of result, restfulness of effort, and destination of motion. Constitutional completeness establishes the non-decomposable constitution of *jeevan*. Activity completeness settles its internal search through realisation and resolution. Conduct completeness establishes its outward activity as humane conduct and participation in universal orderliness.

| Transition | Completeness | Principal goal | What is established |
|---|---|---|---|
| T1 | Constitutional completeness | Immortality of result | Constitutionally complete sentient atom, *jeevan* |
| T2 | Activity completeness | Restfulness of effort | Realisation and resolution in awakened *jeevan* |
| T3 | Conduct completeness | Destination of motion | Continuous authentic conduct, contributing to humane tradition |

Each activity includes effort, motion, and result. The three goals identify what becomes complete in constitution, understanding, and conduct: T1 establishes the persistent sentient unit, T2 settles its search through realisation, and T3 establishes continuity of that understanding in conduct (SB p. 58).

### 1.11.1 Activity completeness (T2)

Activity completeness is restfulness of effort. The texts define restfulness as the absence of delusion. Delusion is not absence of activity: *jeevan* continues hoping, thinking, desiring, acting, and undergoing results while mistaking the body for the self. The unresolved effort is the continual search for happiness without understanding *jeevan*, coexistence, and humane conduct well enough to guide living.

Activity completeness is attained when knowing, believing, recognising, and fulfilling become coherent through realisation. The inward knowing circuit of *jeevan* and the harmonies between its faculties now operate in resolution. This establishes the understanding through which the seer guides action and its results.

### 1.11.2 Conduct completeness (T3)

Conduct completeness is the destination of motion. Understanding becomes continuous in work, behaviour, and participation in orderliness. Reflection provides truthful understanding, and projection evidences it in living. At conduct completeness, action fulfils relationships and its result is mutual satisfaction.

T2 concerns the settlement of *jeevan*'s internal activity in realisation and resolution. T3 concerns the individual's continuous expression of that resolution in conduct. Its social fulfilment includes contributing to a humane tradition; individual and social completeness remain distinguishable (MVD pp. 15–16, 80). The human goals and social systems through which understanding is transmitted are developed in §1.15.

### 1.12 Progressions and planes

Four terms distinguish the order of manifestation, definite conduct, atomic development, and awakening.

### 1.12.1 Terms for order and development

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Existential progression (*niyati-kram*) | Definite order in which material, pranic, animal, and knowledge orders become manifest under appropriate conditions |
| Way of existence (*niyati-vidhi*) | The four conformance modes explained with the orders (§1.6.1) |
| Development progression (*vikas-kram*) | Physicochemical development and composition, including constitution-oriented atoms, until constitutional completeness becomes possible |
| Awakening progression (*jagriti-kram*) | Qualitative development within constitutionally complete *jeevan* toward activity and conduct completeness |

Existential progression and the way of existence describe the four orders and their conformance (§1.6). Development progression and awakening progression describe the atomic and sentient path. At the animal-order junction, compositional progression supplies the body and development in the atom supplies *jeevan*. In the knowledge order, the human body becomes the medium for the full awakening progression. The relation between this ontological ordering and evolutionary history is examined in §6.2.3.

### 1.12.2 Four planes

Order identifies the kind of manifestation; plane identifies developmental standing. The four planes are physicochemical, delusional, deific, and divine or complete.

| Plane | Developmental standing |
|---|---|
| Physicochemical | Insentient constitution and composition before constitutional completeness |
| Delusional | Sentient *jeevan* without realisation; in humans, body taken as self |
| Deific | Activity completeness: realisation and resolution |
| Divine (complete) | Conduct completeness: living proof and its continuity |

Orders and planes are therefore not interchangeable. The crosswalk below shows how the main classifications meet without turning them into one sequence:

| Manifestation or human type | Developmental source | Plane and completeness | Characteristic expression |
|---|---|---|---|
| Material and pranic orders | Physicochemical composition | Physicochemical; before T1 | Structure- and seed-conformance |
| Animal joint form | Animal body from composition; *jeevan* from T1 | Constitutionally complete *jeevan* before awakening | Hope-bound, species-conformant expression; bodily limitation is not human body-identification |
| Animalistic-human and demonic-human | Human body with deluded *jeevan* | Delusional; T1 only | Body-identification and four-and-a-half activities organised around sensitivity |
| Humane or discerning human | Awakening progression becomes established | Movement from delusional toward deific; no fifth plane is added | Knowing and believing increasingly guide recognition and fulfilment |
| Deific-human | Awakening progression | Deific; T2 activity completeness | Realisation and resolution |
| Divine-human | Continuity of awakening | Divine; T3 conduct completeness | Authentic conduct and living proof in tradition |

The five human types classify modes of consciousness and conduct, not biological species or fixed social classes. The traditional plane name *delusional* covers sentient standing before realisation, but the specific error of taking the body as self applies to humans; an animal's narrower expression is a limitation of its bodily medium.

The completion of this development is also called *padmukti*, release from the planes in the divine or complete condition (SB p. 52). The term concerns freedom from incomplete developmental standing; the realised *jeevan* continues to exist and evidence understanding.

### 1.13 Values, relationships, and evaluation

Human understanding becomes evident in the recognition and fulfilment of relationships. The values involved connect the activities of *jeevan* with what people do for one another and with the material means they use.

### 1.13.1 Relationships and the thirty values

Nothing in nature exists in isolation. Complementarity and mutual recognition are eternally present in coexistence and become evident through relationships among units. At the physical and chemical level, complementarity is expressed as definite interaction and composition. In the biological order it is expressed through nourishment, growth, seed-conformance, and the composition method of cells. In the animal order, sentient selection and taste-recognition are expressed through the limited bodily medium under species-conformance. In the knowledge order, the fully developed human bodily medium makes knowing, believing, recognising, evaluating, and fulfilling available as one coherent possibility.

A relationship (*sambandh*) carries definite expectations toward completeness; a contact (*sampark*) involves voluntarily undertaken expectations. Both remain subject to justice and humane conduct (MVD pp. 61–62, 311–312). Parent and child exemplify a relationship whose values require recognition and fulfilment. As an illustration of contact, neighbours may choose the terms of maintaining a shared wall; those chosen terms do not make their obligation to act justly optional.

Value has order-specific expression. In its universal ontological sense, value is essentiality (*maulikta*) and participation in orderliness (*vyavastha mein bhaagidari*): the definite conduct through which a unit complements others in coexistence (SB p. 50). Usefulness (*upyogita*) is that essentiality as it appears in objects. Human living has four connected dimensions—realisation, thought, behaviour, and work—across which thirty core values are enumerated in five families (PS pp. 151, 154). Their definitions and connections are developed across the primary works.

| Value family | Principal content and field |
|---|---|
| Object values | Usefulness is the definite utility of a material object; art or aesthetic value (*kala-mulya*) adds meaningful convenience and beauty to that usefulness. Here art names a value realised in the object; *kala* also names the skilful activity of expression (§1.14). These are two values within one object-value family. |
| *Jeevan* values | Happiness, peace, contentment, and bliss name harmony within the sentient unit (§1.9). |
| Human values | Fortitude, courage, generosity, kindness, grace, and compassion: the six humane and higher-humane essential natures through which humaneness is evidenced. |
| Established values | Trust, respect, affection, care, guidance, reverence, glory, gratitude, and love become evident when human relationships are recognised and fulfilled. |
| Expressed values (*shishta-mulya*) | The nine corresponding powers in motion: concordance, cordiality, dedication, generosity, naturalness, devoutness, simplicity, humility, and non-otherness. |

These families are related but not interchangeable. Inner harmony, humane disposition, relationship-fulfilment, expressed conduct in relationship, and the usefulness of objects answer different evaluative questions.

Taken together, the four *jeevan* values, six human values, nine established values, nine expressed values, and two object values are the thirty values (*tees mulya*) across realisation, thought, behaviour, and work (Paribhasha, pp. 151, 154). The nine established values in state are paired with the nine expressed values in motion: trust with concordance, respect with cordiality, affection with dedication, care with generosity, guidance with naturalness, reverence with devoutness, glory with simplicity, gratitude with humility, and love with non-otherness (*vishwas–saujanyata*, *samman–sauhardta*, *sneha–nishtha*, *mamta–udarta*, *vatsalya–sahajta*, *shraddha–pujyata*, *gaurav–saralta*, *kritagyata–saumyata*, *prem–ananyata*).

Object values belong to material units and become evident in their usefulness. The other four families concern activities of *jeevan*. Named values are also located at particular faculties and paired with the expressions through which they are carried outward (MVD pp. 328–348; AVD pp. 91–94).

The four *jeevan* values are harmonies between adjacent faculties, experienced respectively at *mun*, *vritti*, *chitta*, and *buddhi* (§1.9). The nine established values are borne three each at *chitta*, *vritti*, and *mun*, with their corresponding expressed values. The humane essential natures are named at *vritti* and *mun*, some at positions of their own and some as the expression paired with another value. The family identifies the evaluative role a value fulfils; the faculty identifies where that activity occurs in *jeevan*. Neither classification replaces the other.

Bearing a value and evidencing it remain distinct. Values are recognised and fulfilled in relationship, and where relationships are not recognised they do not flow (JV, p. 55). The faculty account states what a named value is and where it is borne; the evaluative account of §1.13.3 states how its fulfilment is judged. The full enumeration and its unresolved readings are set out in [*The Sixty-One Activity Pairs of Jeevan*](../The-Epistemology-of-Coexistence/Research-Note-Activity-Pair-Inventory.pdf), and the value families are developed in [*Axiology: Value Theory*](../Axiology-Value-Theory/Axiology-Value-Theory.pdf) §§1.1–1.3.

Recognition and fulfilment are definite below the knowledge order according to structural, seed, and species conformance. Animals do not fail to perform a human evaluation; their narrower expression follows from the bodily medium described in §1.8. Human evaluation can succeed or fail because freedom of action operates while knowing and believing may remain disconnected from recognition and fulfilment. Inherent provision still requires conducive conditions: a seed germinates in a suitable environment, and the human possibility of awakening requires an environment in which understanding can be studied, practised, and evidenced.

### 1.13.2 Evaluative perspectives and justice

Recognition and selection occur before the knowledge order. In animal expression, *jeevan* selects and recognises taste through hope-bound activity mediated by the animal body. The human distinction is explicit evaluation through six perspectives and the capacity to evaluate relationships, values, justice, dharma, and truth through knowing and believing, then to reorganise conduct accordingly. A unit's manifestness and imaging provide a basis of recognition; human evaluation additionally requires understanding what is recognised. Error enters when deluded *jeevan* evaluates from body-identification and treats sensory, bodily, or material criteria as exhaustive.

Human behaviour is expressed through six perspectives: pleasant–unpleasant, healthy–unhealthy, profit–loss, justice–injustice, dharma–adharma, and truth–untruth.

| Perspective | Principal domain | Place in humane evaluation |
|---|---|---|
| Pleasant–unpleasant | Sensation and instinct | Necessary but insufficient as organising standpoint |
| Healthy–unhealthy | Body | Necessary but insufficient as organising standpoint |
| Profit–loss | Material production and exchange | Necessary but insufficient as organising standpoint |
| Justice–injustice | Human relationship and behaviour | Humane regulation of conduct |
| Dharma–adharma | Thought and resolution | Humane regulation of thought |
| Truth–untruth | Existence as coexistence | Ground of realisation and knowledge |

The first three perspectives are not rejected in their proper domains. Food may be pleasant, bodily activity healthy, and production materially beneficial. Delusion occurs when these become the final refuge and overrule justice, resolution, and truth. Humane perspective subordinates sensory, bodily, and material aims to justice, dharma, and truth.

Unit dharma—the inseparable innateness of a unit—must be distinguished from the dharma–adharma perspective. The latter evaluates whether thought and policy support resolution and overall orderliness. Similarly, justice is both a perspective by which conduct is assessed and the name of a completed relational activity.

### 1.13.3 The dual role of justice

As a perspective, justice evaluates behaviour as just or unjust. As completed relational activity, justice is recognising relationships, fulfilling values, evaluating the fulfilment, and achieving mutual satisfaction. Trust, respect, affection, and other relationship values contribute to justice but are not individually identical with it.

Humane conduct brings together value, character, and ethics (*mulya–charitra–naitikta*). Values supply the content to be fulfilled; character concerns definite conduct; ethics concerns the policies by which humaneness is protected and supported (Paribhasha, p. 151). Bearing values in *jeevan* does not by itself ensure their fulfilment: a relationship must be recognised and its responsibilities carried out (JV p. 55).

Consider a person caring for an ill parent. Recognising the relationship, understanding the parent's needs, arranging suitable care, and checking whether it has helped distinguish knowing from fulfilment and evaluation. A kind intention alone does not establish adequate care. Nor does purchasing a useful object complete the relationship: the object supports bodily need, while attention, trust, respect, and care concern how the people relate. The example connects the unit's useful capacity, the caregiver's understanding, and the test of mutual satisfaction.

Law applies across the four orders as definite regulation. In human living, regulation and balance are fulfilled as justice in behaviour, resolved thought as dharma, and realisation in truth as coexistence (MVD p. 174). These connections join happiness as human dharma, definite values in relationships, and understanding fulfilled in conduct. Evaluation is the human way of knowingly recognising and fulfilling those relations (§6.1). Statutory law is an institutional codification; its adequacy is judged by whether it supports justice and mutual satisfaction.

Human families and social assemblies achieve continuity through relationship-fulfilment. Their members remain distinct units who recognise relationships, fulfil values, evaluate the fulfilment, and achieve mutual satisfaction. This is the basis of humane continuity in family, community, and society. It gives social assembly a different basis from the definite composition of a chemical compound (§1.5).

### 1.14 Realisation in coexistence

Through realisation, *jeevan* understands nature as inseparably present in Omnipresence. It knows existence as coexistence, knows itself as the knower, and expresses that understanding as authenticity. This is the goal of development: an activity of *jeevan*, with the distinction between the knowing unit and the pervasive reality preserved.

Knowledge, knower, and known remain distinguishable in realisation. *Jeevan* is the knower. The threefold content of knowledge comprises the holistic view of coexistence, knowledge of *jeevan*, and knowledge of humane conduct. Existence as coexistence is what is seen and known, while the human and *jeevan* goals become meaningful through awakened participation in undivided society and universal orderliness. Their coherence lies in accurate knowing and evidence, not in ontological identity.

Knowledge (*gyan*) is also a name for the actionless pervasive reality in which knowing occurs (MVD p. 35). This usage and knowledge as understood content are related through realisation: *jeevan* knows coexistence without becoming the pervasive reality. Wisdom (*vivek*) recognises the human goals of resolution, prosperity, fearlessness, and coexistence; science (*vigyan*) determines how material means and human activity serve those goals (MVD p. 170). Knowing, judging purpose, and arranging its material fulfilment are thus connected but distinguishable.

Realisation settles the search arising from delusion. Ultimate bliss is named at realised atma, and bliss names the harmony of buddhi and atma (§1.9). Authenticity is the projection of realisation: understanding becomes evident through behaviour, work, and participation. Recognition and fulfilment in orderliness are therefore expressions of realisation, not substitutes for it.

In awakened human living, presentation, communication, and expression carry realised understanding into a shared tradition (AVD pp. 115–116). Presentation (*prakashan*) makes actuality evident through an accomplished and meaningful form; communication (*sampreshana*) indicates completeness, including constitutional, activity, and conduct completeness; and expression (*abhivyakti*) is the mental, verbal, and bodily activity through which one's existence is evidenced for all-round resolution (*abhyudaya*) (PS pp. 22, 119, 206). Understanding is thus presented, made understandable, and lived. These activities involve the coherent participation of *jeevan* through the body described in §1.8. Words direct attention to the activity of units and its inherent meaning, which concerns their properties, essential nature, and dharma; their usefulness is fulfilled in understanding and enabling another to understand (MVD pp. 109–110).

As a skilful expressive activity, art (*kala*) makes the qualities of presentation, communication, and expression evident and meaningful, joining usefulness with beauty (PS p. 56). It concerns how understanding and purpose take an accomplished form in speech, work, and behaviour. In making an object, this is evident as the art value that enhances usefulness (§1.13.1); in communicating understanding, it serves intelligibility and the purpose of resolution. The same humane understanding is evidenced through these connected activities.

A related vocabulary of gross, subtle, causal, and supreme cause identifies different realities: gross forms are compositions and bodies; subtle units are atoms; the causal status is assigned to *atma*; and Omnipresence is supreme cause (MVD pp. 288–289). These terms do not denote three bodies of a single self as in the Advaita analysis (§2.6).

### 1.15 Ontology and societal fulfilment outcomes

Human awakening is evidenced through four goals: resolution, prosperity, fearlessness, and coexistence. Resolution is clarity about existence, *jeevan*, relationships, and conduct, without unresolved contradiction directing action. Prosperity is the family's assurance of producing more physical goods than its assessed needs and using them appropriately. Fearlessness is trust in human mutuality and society. Coexistence is complementary living among humans and with the rest of nature.

The cumulative correspondence between these goals and the *jeevan* values is set out in §1.9. Their fulfilment rests on the human aim of continuous happiness, recognition of relationships, and the responsibility to make understanding effective in conduct.

At the social scale, awakened living develops from family to undivided society and universal orderliness. Five social systems support its continuity. These differ from the four dimensions of individual living—realisation, thought, behaviour, and work—introduced in §1.13.1:

| Social system | Function in humane orderliness |
|---|---|
| Education–sanskar | Makes right understanding and the disposition to live by it available across generations. |
| Justice–preservation | Recognises relationships, fulfils values, evaluates fulfilment, achieves mutual satisfaction, and protects humaneness. |
| Health–restraint | Maintains a bodily condition and disciplined living capable of supporting and evidencing awakening. |
| Production–work | Enables each family to apply labour and skill toward producing more than its assessed physical needs. |
| Exchange–storage | Organises exchange through recognition of labour value and maintains responsible reserves for continuity. |

These are not external institutions added to an otherwise private realisation. They are the public structures through which understanding is transmitted, relationships fulfilled, bodies supported, needs met, and social participation sustained.

Humane tradition means continuity across generations. An individual's conduct completeness contributes to this continuity without depending on the prior awakening of the whole society. Social completeness concerns the wider condition in which resolution, prosperity, fearlessness, and coexistence remain available through education, family, work, and orderliness (MVD pp. 15–16).

### 1.16 Method, evidence, and what Madhyasth Darshan establishes

Madhyasth Darshan presents its method as study, experiment and practice, followed by realisation and evidence in conduct. These activities do not all warrant the same kind of claim:

| Mode of inquiry | What it tests or evidences | What it does not establish by itself |
|---|---|---|
| Logical exposition | Whether definitions and relations form a coherent account | That the premises are empirically true |
| Study and self-examination | Whether coexistence, *jeevan*, and the activities of knowing can be understood and recognised | A public physical identification of *jeevan* |
| Experiment and practice in work and behaviour | Whether the proposed understanding regulates action and produces repeatable relational consequences | A measurement of constitutional completeness |
| Realisation | MD's claimed first-person completion of knowing coexistence, *jeevan*, and humane conduct | Agreement from methods that do not accept realisation as warrant |
| Conduct-evidence | Whether values, mutual satisfaction, resolution, and participation become intersubjectively visible | That every ontological claim has thereby become a scientific measurement |

Instrument-based scientific confirmation remains a further comparison standard where a claim concerns publicly detectable physical structure. The current primary-text account supplies no accepted measurement procedure for constitutional completeness, the *jeevan*–body interface, or post-death reassociation (§6.2).

Living proof (*pramanikta*) is MD's practical evidence of understanding: what is understood must guide action and fulfilment in relationships. The comparison that follows distinguishes this standard from the warrants offered by other philosophies and by public scientific measurement.

## 2. The Advaita Vedanta Answer

Advaita Vedanta holds that existence in the strictest, absolute sense is Brahman alone — one without a second (*ekam eva advitiyam*). The world of names, forms, bodies, and relations is *mithya*: a dependent appearance valid at the empirical level (*vyavahara*), whose claim to independent reality is sublated by knowledge of absolute reality (*paramartha*). The true Self (*Atman*) is neither born nor destroyed, because it is ultimately identical with this non-dual Brahman.

The account here is Shankara-centred classical Advaita. Its principal authorities are the Upanishads, the *Brahma Sutra Bhashya*, and the *Bhagavad Gita Bhashya*. Later systematic vocabulary and pedagogical works such as the *Vivekachudamani* and *Drig-Drishya-Viveka* are used where they clarify the tradition, but they do not by themselves determine claims disputed within post-Shankara Advaita.

### 2.1 Brahman and absolute existence

The Chandogya Upanishad states Advaita's foundational claim directly:

> **"In the beginning this was Existence alone, One only, without a second."**
> - CU 6.2.1

Shankara reads this existence as partless, all-pervasive consciousness, without internal distinction (CU 6.2.1, commentary). The next verse rejects the claim that existence could arise from non-existence (CU 6.2.2). The claim is about the dependence of all name-form on one reality, rather than a temporal event in which non-being becomes being.

At *paramartha*, only this one partless reality exists absolutely. Classical Advaita names that absolute as *nirguna* Brahman — without attributes, actionless, and not an object among objects. *Saguna* Brahman is the same reality associated with Maya and spoken of as Ishvara: operative at *vyavahara*, not a second absolute beside the attribute-free ground (§2.6). *Paramatman* (the Supreme Self) is likewise not a second absolute beside Brahman. When superimposition is removed, the Ishvara–*jiva* distinction is resolved as a standpoint-dependent distinction rather than two independently existing beings being destroyed (VC, v. 244; §2.6).

How that one reality is characterised as Sat-Chit-Ananda and known as *turiya* follows in §§2.2–2.3; the tiered architecture through which the world appears relative to it is stated in §§2.4–2.5.

### 2.2 Brahman as Sat-Chit-Ananda

Advaita reads Brahman as *sat*, *chit*, and *ananda* — being, consciousness, and bliss — three expressions indicating the same non-dual reality. The Taittiriya Upanishad provides a central basis for this understanding:

> **"Brahman is truth, knowledge, and infinite."**
> - TU 2.1.1

Shankara treats each word as a separate predicate of that one Brahman — *satyam brahma*, *jnanam brahma*, *anantam brahma*. Truth (*satyam*) marks what does not change the nature ascertained as its own, and so distinguishes Brahman from mutable things. Knowledge (*jnanam*) is consciousness itself, Brahman's nature rather than an activity that changes its agent. Infinity (*anantam*) is freedom from limitation by time, space, or object (TU 2.1.1, commentary).

The bliss sheath in TU 2.5 remains a covering to be discriminated from the Self. A stronger basis for identifying Brahman with bliss is TU 2.7: Brahman is *rasa*, the essence or fullness through whose attainment one becomes blissful. Advaita therefore uses *ananda* for limitless completeness rather than a passing affect. Experiential pleasure is not the final referent of the teaching.

### 2.3 Brahman as *turiya*

The *Mandukya Upanishad* (MU, vv. 3–7) analyses the Self in four quarters. Waking (*jagrat*) concerns outward experience of gross objects; dream (*svapna*) concerns inward experience of subtle objects; deep sleep (*sushupti*) lacks differentiated objects and desires and is described as undifferentiated consciousness abounding in bliss (MU, vv. 3–5). This supplies a first-person route for distinguishing changing experience from the Self. The text also has a cosmic dimension: verse 6 calls the third quarter the Lord of all and the source into which beings resolve. Its quarters are therefore not merely psychological descriptions, but neither are they MD's developmental orders of nature.

*Turiya* — the fourth — is not another state alongside these three, but the Self known by negating every state-characteristic:

> **"They consider the Fourth to be that which is not conscious of the internal world, nor conscious of the external world, nor conscious of both the worlds, nor a mass of consciousness, nor conscious, nor unconscious; which is unseen, beyond empirical dealings, beyond the grasp (of the organs of action), uninferable, unthinkable, indescribable; whose valid proof consists in the single belief in the Self; in which all phenomena cease; and which is unchanging, auspicious, and non-dual. That is the Self, and That is to be known."**
> - MU, v. 7

Seer-seen discrimination in the *Drig-Drishya-Viveka* and the Gita's field-knower teaching develop the same structural point (§2.7).

### 2.4 Three tiers of reality

Advaita's standard framework separates three levels of reality. Apparent errors (*pratibhasika*) include a rope mistaken for a snake. Shared empirical reality (*vyavahara*) governs bodies, physical laws, ethics, and scripture. Absolute truth (*paramartha*) is Brahman alone. Discrimination (*viveka*) between the real and the unreal is defined as the firm conviction that Brahman alone is real and the universe unreal (VC, v. 20); at the deepest level the individual soul is itself, directly, the Supreme Brahman, and nothing else (VC, v. 216).

*Mithya* names the world's dependent ontological status; the three tiers distinguish the validity of appearances and the knowledge that corrects them. Sublation (*badha*) removes an attribution of reality: correcting the rope-snake removes the belief that a snake is present; knowing Brahman removes the attribution of independent reality to name-form. Knowledge does not turn a previously independent world into an unreal one. Empirical appearances and conduct can continue after the correction (BS 2.1.14; 4.1.15).

| Level | What exists? | Status of world / error |
|---|---|---|
| Apparent (*pratibhasika*) | Rope-snake, dream objects, perceptual errors | Sublated by waking or correction |
| Empirical (*vyavahara*) | Bodies, minds, Ishvara, causes, duties, scriptures, science | Shared, law-governed; operationally valid; sublated only by *brahma-jnana* |
| Absolute (*paramartha*) | Brahman alone | World is *mithya*, dependent appearance |

At *vyavahara*, the world is not unreal in the way a hallucination is; it is unreal relative to Brahman — like a dream relative to waking. The world is not sheer nothing, nor absolutely real; it is *mithya* — dependent appearance.

Later Advaita systematises this status through *anirvacaniyatva*, indefinability as absolutely real or sheer non-being. Shankara already describes name-form as indeterminable as identical with or different from Brahman (BS 1.1.5), and as indeterminable as real or unreal (BS 2.1.14). Later accounts elaborate this into theories of *mithyatva* and error; they should not all be attributed to Shankara as one finished system. The central distinction allows empirical validity without granting the world independent absolute being (§2.10).

### 2.5 Maya, *adhyasa*, and causal doctrine

The multiplicity of the world rests on Brahman through superimposition (*adhyasa*). Shankara's opening definition is that superimposition is the cognition of something in that which it is not: the apparent presentation, in the form of memory, of something previously seen in another locus (BS, *Adhyasa Bhashya*). Subject (*vishayi*, the conscious I) and object (*vishaya*, body, senses, and world) are opposed like light and darkness, yet ordinary dealing proceeds from their mutual superimposition: bodily predicates are placed on the Self, and the Self's consciousness is placed on the body.

The rope mistaken for a snake illustrates this structure. The rope is the substrate (*adhishthana*) of the error; it does not become a snake. Similarly, Brahman is the reality on which name-form depends, without acquiring its apparent divisions. Later Advaita commonly analyses Maya through concealment (*avarana*) and projection (*vikshepa*), and distinguishes cosmic manifestation under Ishvara from individual misidentification under *avidya*. Later schools disagree about the precise relation between Maya and avidya and about the locus of ignorance. Their common commitment is that name-form has no independent reality apart from Brahman, while the embodied *jiva* mistakes the body-mind complex for the Self.

Advaita accepts effect–cause non-difference without adopting Samkhya's real transformation of *prakriti*. The Chandogya's clay teaching states the pattern directly: through knowledge of one lump of clay, all things made of clay are known, since every transformation is a matter of speech and name while clay alone is real (CU 6.1.4). Shankara makes the causal point explicit at BS 2.1.14: an effect has no existence apart from its cause. The *Vivekachudamani* compresses the same teaching: modifications such as a clay jar are nothing but clay; likewise the universe has no being apart from Brahman (VC, v. 251).

At the empirical level, Brahman associated with Maya is taught as Ishvara, the non-different material and efficient cause of name-form (*abhinna-nimitta-upadana-karana*). Yet Brahman does not undergo real modification: the world has no independent being apart from Brahman, while Brahman remains changeless. Later Advaita calls this *vivarta*, apparent rather than real transformation, in contrast with *parinama*. Creation is therefore neither production from nothing nor a real alteration of the absolute. Causal instruction belongs to *vyavahara* and is itself withdrawn at the highest non-dual standpoint.

The later error theory *anirvacaniya-khyati* treats the mistaken snake as neither real nor sheer non-being: it is experienced, yet correction removes the belief in its presence. This illustrates dependent appearance without making the shared empirical world equivalent to a particular perceptual error (§2.4). MD also admits mistaken recognition, but locates delusion (*bhram*) in the real *jeevan*, especially in its identification of the body as self. It does not extend the category of dependent appearance to the reality of the world itself.

### 2.6 Ishvara, jiva, and jagat at *vyavahara*

Because Advaita distinguishes empirical teaching from absolute non-duality, it uses categories structurally necessary to explain the experienced world even though no second reality remains at the absolute standpoint.

Brahman at *paramartha* is *nirguna* — pure, actionless awareness. Ishvara (*saguna* Brahman) is Brahman associated with Maya and taught as the non-different material and efficient cause (*abhinna-nimitta-upadana-karana*) of name-form manifestation, not a creator *ex nihilo*. Ishvara and *jiva* are not two absolutes alongside Brahman. Their distinction is valid within *vyavahara* and is resolved, rather than annihilated as two independently existing things, when non-dual identity is known (VC, v. 244).

Jivatman (*jiva*) is the individual embodied soul — the true Self (*Atman*) conditioned by *upadhis* (limiting adjuncts), appearing as separate until realisation. Reflection (*pratibimba*) and limitation (*avaccheda*) are two ways later Advaita explains how the one Self appears many under adjuncts. The empirical person is also analysed in terms of three bodies — gross (*sthula*), subtle (*sukshma*), and causal (*karana*) — and five sheaths (*pancha kosha*: food, vital, mental, intellect, and bliss). These belong to empirical life without constituting an ultimately separate Self. Their shared vocabulary with Madhyasth Darshan does not make the two ontologies equivalent (§5.7.2).

In the pedagogical account of the internal organ (*antahkarana*), *manas* considers alternatives, *buddhi* determines, *ahamkara* identifies as the embodied agent, and *chitta* remembers (VC, vv. 93–94). These are mental functions illuminated by the Self, not four independently conscious subjects. The reflected-consciousness account explains their apparent consciousness and agency (VC, vv. 103–104; DDV, vv. 1–7). This differs from MD's placement of its five faculties within the sentient *jeevan* unit.

Karma and *samsara* belong to the empirical order: the conditioned jiva continues across births until liberating knowledge, while the subtle body carries the equipment and impressions for experiencing action-results (BG 2.22; VC, vv. 96–100). Ordinary bodily death therefore does not end empirical individuality. Its continuity across births is compatible with the teaching that no separate self is ultimately real.

Jagat (*nama-roopa*) is the physical universe of names and forms — operationally valid at *vyavahara*, ultimately *mithya* at *paramartha*. Bodies, minds, causal order, scripture, and science belong to this shared tier until *brahma-jnana* sublates the apparent separateness of the world from Brahman.

### 2.7 Witness-consciousness and the Self

The *Drig-Drishya-Viveka* (DDV) offers a rigorous seer-seen (*drig-drishya*) discrimination: whatever can be observed — body, sensations, thoughts — is "seen" and therefore not the seer (DDV, vv. 1–5). What remains is *Sakshin*, witness-consciousness — irreducible to matter, and Advaita's primary first-person route for discriminating the Self from body and mind.

The Gita calls the body the field and its knower the knower of the field. Krishna then asks to be known as the knower in all fields and identifies knowledge of both field and knower as knowledge (BG 13.2–13.3 in the cited edition; conventionally 13.1–13.2).

The Self is self-luminous (*svaprakasha*): its presence does not require a second awareness to reveal it, whereas particular thoughts and perceptions can themselves be known (DDV, vv. 1–5). Witness-language expresses this asymmetry during discrimination; it does not posit an ultimately separate witness confronting independently real objects. At liberation the witness is known as Brahman, without a second. Whether irreducible awareness is finally one non-dual Self or many distinct knowers is the dispute in §§5.3.3, 5.6.

### 2.8 Conservation, origination, and temporality

Brahman does not begin: it is partless, actionless, and outside temporal becoming (VC, v. 393). At the empirical level, manifestation, maintenance, and dissolution (*srishti–sthiti–laya*) recur without a first cycle. Shankara explicitly defends beginningless transmigration and creation through the mutual dependence of karma and embodiment, like seed and sprout (BS 2.1.35–36). This cosmology gives the empirical world continuity and causal order without making it an independent absolute.

At *paramartha*, even origination is denied. Gaudapada's doctrine of non-origination (*ajati*) states that neither birth, bondage, seeking nor liberation is ultimately real (MU, Karika II.32, with Shankara's commentary). The distinction is between levels of explanation, not between two successive histories of Brahman. Temporal succession belongs to the empirical order; the Self does not become timeless at the end of a process.

The *Mandukya* analysis of waking, dream, and deep sleep (§2.3) is a first-person route into how temporal states appear and are witnessed; the witness (*turiya*) is not another state among them. Fuller comparison of Advaita's *trikaal* language with Madhyasth Darshan's account of *kaal* as duration of unit-activity is developed in [*Nature of Time*](../Nature-Of-Time/Nature-Of-Time.pdf).

### 2.9 Liberation and the knowledge path

Liberation (*moksha*) is recognition of the ever-established identity of Atman and Brahman, rather than a merger of two independently real beings. The identity teaching *tat tvam asi* — "That thou art" — belongs to CU 6.8.7 and is developed in Shankara's treatment of scriptural identity and the individual Self (BS 1.1.4; 1.4.22). The jiva was never ultimately separate; knowledge removes the contrary understanding.

Liberating knowledge can arise while the body lives (*jivanmukti*). The body's subsequent continuation is explained through already-begun karma (*prarabdha*); when that is exhausted, bodily existence ends, conventionally described as *videhamukti*. Death does not complete an otherwise incomplete liberating knowledge (BS 1.1.4; 4.1.15). Embodied conduct after knowledge remains meaningful at *vyavahara* (§2.10).

Method serves that ontological claim rather than replacing it. Advaita inherits the classical analysis of valid knowledge (*pramana*): for the supreme truth of non-duality, verbal testimony (*shabda*) — the revealed word of the Upanishads, processed through reasoning and meditation — is finally competent; perception and inference operate within the subject-object duality to be transcended. The path is classically threefold: hearing (*shravana*), reasoning (*manana*), and sustained meditation (*nididhyasana*), under a qualified teacher. Teaching itself often proceeds by provisional attribution and later withdrawal (*adhyaropa–apavada*): names and forms are first admitted for instruction, then negated until only the Self remains — the same logic as seer-seen discrimination in the DDV. Full epistemological comparison: [*The Epistemology of Coexistence*](../The-Epistemology-of-Coexistence/The-Epistemology-of-Coexistence.pdf) §2.

The *Vivekachudamani* insists on ethical discipline as prerequisite to knowledge (VC, vv. 17–19). Ethics at *vyavahara* is developed in §2.10.

### 2.10 Ethics, *loka-sangraha*, and what realisation evidences

Advaita's account of the world as *mithya* at *paramartha* does not make ethics dispensable where agency and obligation operate. Before knowledge, discipline, duty, non-attachment, and steadiness prepare the mind for inquiry. The Bhagavad Gita, in Shankara's commentary, also invokes *loka-sangraha* — holding the world together: action is undertaken without possessiveness or attachment to results, and the knower may continue to act for the welfare and guidance of others (BG 3.8, 3.20–3.25). After knowledge, such action does not reinstate ultimate agency or separate individuality; embodied life continues under the already-begun momentum of *prarabdha* until the body falls. *Jivanmukti* (§2.9) therefore joins non-agency at the highest standpoint with responsible conduct at *vyavahara*.

What Advaita recognises at the empirical level must be distinguished from what realisation discloses at *paramartha*. Some disciplines prepare for knowledge; non-attached action and world-welfare also articulate how the knower can live. Empirical responsibility does not require a restoration of ultimate separate agency.

| Domain | Valid at *vyavahara* | Status at *paramartha* |
|---|---|---|
| Ethics and duty | Discipline prepares for knowledge; non-attached action and *loka-sangraha* express knowledge (VC, vv. 17–19; BG 3.8, 3.20–3.25) | No separate agent or obligation remains at *paramartha*; embodied conduct continues at *vyavahara* |
| World and nature | Law-governed, shared, beginningless cosmology | *Mithya* — dependent appearance |
| Individual self | Real as embodied jiva under *upadhi* | Identical with Brahman; separateness dispelled |
| Relationships and society | *Loka-sangraha*, ordained action for world-welfare | Not ultimately separate from Brahman |
| Time and change | Past, present, future, birth and death operationally valid | Sublated when timeless Self is known |

Realisation removes the attribution of independent reality to world and individual; it does not change their ontological status or require the disappearance of empirical conduct. The knower can continue to act for world-welfare without taking separate agency as absolute.

### 2.11 Method, evidence, and what Advaita establishes

Advaita Vedanta answers the study's framing questions as follows:

| Question | Advaita Vedanta answer | Section |
|---|---|---|
| What is existence? | Brahman alone — being, consciousness, bliss, one without a second | §§2.1–2.3 |
| What exists? | Brahman absolutely; *jiva*, Ishvara, and *jagat* real only at *vyavahara* | §§2.4, 2.6 |
| Does it begin? | Brahman does not begin; name-form cycles as *srishti–sthiti–laya* within beginningless *vyavahara* | §2.8 |
| Does the self begin? | *Jivatman* is *vyavahara*-level conditioning; the true Self was never born and never separate | §§2.7, 2.9 |
| Is the world real? | Operationally real at *vyavahara*; *mithya* — dependent appearance — at *paramartha* | §§2.4–2.5 |
| How do we know? | Scripture (*shabda*), reasoning, and contemplative discrimination — *shravana*, *manana*, *nididhyasana* | §2.9 |

Advaita presents a non-dual ontology and a disciplined first-person method for discriminating seer from seen. Whether its scriptural and contemplative grounds warrant denying ultimate reality to plurality remains the comparative question in §6.3.

## 3. Modern Philosophical Approaches

Contemporary Western philosophy contains no single ontology. Physicalism is influential in analytic philosophy of mind and is continuous with much scientific explanation, but it competes with dualism, panpsychism, neutral monism, hylomorphism, idealism, and process metaphysics. The decisive questions are not captured by a simple matter-versus-mind opposition: what counts as physical, whether experience is reducible or fundamental, what individuates a self, how particulars depend on a ground, and whether value belongs to reality or to human practice. SB frames one central dispute by observing that neither one-way emergence has been directly witnessed:

> **"In this course, metaphysical thought has held that matter arises from consciousness, whereas materialist thought has maintained that consciousness emerges from matter. Both ideologies have proposed numerous arguments, practices, and experiments in support of their claims. Yet, neither has matter been seen emerging from consciousness, nor consciousness emerging from matter. What is evident is that consciousness and matter are inseparably present."**
> - SB, p. 48

Madhyasth Darshan's own title — *Samadhanatmak Bhautikvad* (*Resolution Centred Materialism*) — reframes rather than abandons material reality. Against dialectical materialism, which treats conflict as historically generative, SB proposes an orderliness-centred account in which nature remains real, saturated in Omnipresence, and intelligible through resolution rather than perpetual opposition (SB, pp. 8, 44). That proposal must therefore be compared not only with reductive materialism but with several rival accounts of ground, matter, experience, individuality, and relation.

The figure below maps the principal physicalist family. The later subsections then place it beside dualist, panpsychist, hylomorphic, monist, pluralist, process, and neutral-ground alternatives. These positions disagree sufficiently that they cannot be combined into a single “modern” answer in §5.

### 3.1 Physicalist ontology: what exists

> **"I take physicalism to be the view that every real, concrete phenomenon in the universe is…physical."**
> - Strawson 2006, p. 2

Physicalism holds, at minimum, that concrete reality is wholly physical or wholly constituted by the physical. Type-identity and reductive theories identify mental states with physical states; physicalist versions of functionalism identify mental roles with their physical realisations; non-reductive physicalism allows relatively autonomous psychological descriptions while requiring every instance to be physically realised. Emergentist versions assign higher-level properties to organised physical systems. These positions share dependence on the physical while disagreeing about reduction, explanation, and causal autonomy. Functionalism itself specifies causal or computational roles and need not, without a further premise about their realisation, entail physicalism.

Physicalism, methodological naturalism, and scientific realism must be kept separate. Physicalism is a metaphysical thesis about what concrete reality is. Methodological naturalism is a rule of inquiry that seeks testable explanations without invoking non-natural interventions. Scientific realism is a philosophy of science according to which successful mature theories give at least approximate knowledge of a mind-independent world, including unobservable entities. Instrumentalism and constructive empiricism instead restrict commitment to empirical adequacy. Science can be practised under more than one of these philosophical interpretations.

Causal closure supplies a major argument for physicalism: if every physical event has a sufficient physical cause, a non-physical mind seems either causally idle, systematically overdetermining, or in need of a revised causal account (Kim 2005). Closure is not the definition of every physicalism, and non-physical causation cannot be dismissed merely by repeating energy conservation; the conclusion depends on how physical cause, sufficiency, exclusion, and interaction are specified. It nevertheless places a serious burden on Madhyasth Darshan's claim that *jeevan* works through a body: the account must explain what difference *jeevan* makes while accounting for the success of physical descriptions of bodily causation.

### 3.2 The hard problem and the first-person datum

> **"The really hard problem of consciousness is the problem of experience. When we think and perceive, there is a whir of information-processing, but there is also a subjective aspect."**
> - Chalmers 1995, p. 3

Nagel characterises conscious experience through what it is like for the experiencing organism (1974, p. 1). The hard problem asks why physical processing should have that subjective character, beyond the established correlations between brain activity and experience. Physicalists differ over whether this is a temporary explanatory gap, a conceptual limitation, or a mistaken demand. The gap alone does not establish an extra-physical bearer. Advaita's seer-seen discrimination (§2.7) and MD's account of *jeevan* (§6.2.1) must each support their further ontological claims.

Property dualism does not split the difference within physicalism; it denies that all properties are physical. It holds that conscious properties are irreducible even when they depend lawfully on physical systems. Substance dualism goes further by positing a distinct mental bearer. Both preserve the first-person datum but inherit an interaction or dependence problem: they must explain why conscious properties arise where they do and how they participate in physical action. The explanatory gap therefore creates logical space for non-reductive ontology without selecting Madhyasth Darshan's constitutionally complete *jeevan* over dualist, panpsychist, emergentist, or neutral-monist alternatives.

### 3.3 Families of response: panpsychism, emergence, and illusionism

The hard problem generates several families that cross the boundary between physicalism and its rivals. Strawson's “real physicalism” argues that experience is itself physical and that the physical cannot be wholly non-experiential:

> **"Real physicalism, realistic physicalism, entails panpsychism."**
> - Strawson 2006, p. 12

His argument starts from the reality of experience and rejects its emergence from a wholly non-experiential base (Strawson 2006, pp. 2, 12). “Physicalist” and “panpsychist” therefore need not name mutually exclusive camps.

Panpsychism is a family rather than one doctrine. Constitutive micropsychism treats macro-experience as constituted from experiential micro-realities; panprotopsychism places proto-experiential properties at the base; cosmopsychism begins with a cosmic subject and derives local subjects; Russellian monism treats physics as revealing relational structure while leaving the intrinsic nature of its bearers open (Chalmers 2016). Madhyasth Darshan fits none of these exactly. It neither distributes experience across material particles nor identifies Omnipresence with a cosmic subject; sentience belongs only to constitutionally complete *jeevan* (§1.7.1).

Constitutive panpsychism faces the combination problem: facts about many micro-subjects do not transparently entail one unified macro-subject. Cosmopsychism reverses the direction but must then explain decombination or the individuation of local subjects. Integrated information theory supplies a different structural proposal about the unity and boundaries of experience; it does not by itself solve the metaphysical subject-combination problem (§4.6). Madhyasth Darshan avoids micro-subject combination by identifying *jeevan* as one constitutionally complete unit, but must show how that unit and its claimed sentience can be independently identified (§6.2.1).

Emergentism offers a middle path: new properties appear at organisational thresholds — digestion from chemistry, life from biochemistry, mind from neural complexity. Weak emergence treats higher-level patterns as real but dependent on lower-level physics; strong emergence would add causally novel properties irreducible to microphysics. Constitutional completeness also marks a qualitative threshold, but the resemblance is formal rather than explanatory. Madhyasth Darshan places the transition in a particular atom whose constitution becomes closed and free from molecular and weight bondage; biological emergentism places sentience in organised living systems. The critical question is whether these different proposed thresholds can be operationally distinguished (§6.2.1).

Illusionism instead challenges the conception of phenomenal properties that generates the hard problem. It retains access, self-report, discrimination, and functional control while explaining why experience seems to have properties that the theory denies (Frankish 2016, pp. 1, 8). Eliminative materialism makes a different claim: some folk-psychological categories, especially belief and desire as commonsense theory describes them, may fail to refer and may be replaced by neuroscience (Churchland 1986). Dennett's functional and heterophenomenological approach belongs near illusionism but should not be treated as identical with Churchland's eliminativism. These views require MD to clarify the content and reliability of introspection before using it to establish a distinct sentient bearer.

### 3.4 The self: personal identity and persistence

The philosophy of personal identity distinguishes the bearer of experience from the criteria by which a person persists. Physicalists usually hold that persons begin and end with some biological or psychologically organised physical system, but they disagree about which continuity matters. Animalism identifies the human person with the living human organism. Psychological-continuity theories privilege memory, intention, character, and connected consciousness. Constitution views distinguish the person from, while materially constituting the person through, the organism. Narrative and embodied-enactive accounts emphasise temporally extended self-interpretation, agency, and bodily engagement.

Parfit argues that what matters in survival may be psychological connectedness and continuity rather than a further, all-or-nothing fact of identity (Parfit 1984; SEP Personal Identity). Animalists object that psychological criteria can come apart from the persistence of the organism; narrative and constitution accounts object that a person is neither a bare organism nor a detachable stream of memories. None of these theories by itself establishes post-death survival, but their disagreements prevent “the self is a brain model” from standing as the single modern philosophical answer.

Substance and simple-subject views preserve a bearer not reducible to psychological continuity: Aquinas's rational soul is the form of the living body (§3.6.1), while Leibniz's monads are simple individual substances (§3.6.3). Self-model and predictive-processing accounts in cognitive science explain how bodily ownership, perspective, and continuity are represented without positing an immortal unit (§4.5). The comparison with *jeevan* must therefore distinguish at least three questions: what produces the experienced sense of self, what makes a person persist, and whether there is an enduring sentient bearer beyond the body.

### 3.5 Origination, conservation, and world realism

Western philosophy asks whether what exists had a beginning and whether particulars endure through change. Mainstream physicalism holds that local systems — organisms, stars, civilisations — begin and end, while fundamental physics may or may not require a cosmic beginning (§4.2). Conservation principles in physics treat certain quantities as preserved through transformation — a structural parallel later compared with *vastu* through *roop*-change (§1.4, §4.3), though the categories differ.

Scientific realism answers “is the world mind-independent?” affirmatively, while instrumentalism and constructive empiricism restrict commitment to what theories successfully predict or to their observable consequences. Metaphysical idealisms instead take mind, experience, or the conditions of experience as ontologically basic; they cannot be reduced to the claim that objects depend on being presently perceived. Ontological nihilism, mereological nihilism, and moral nihilism answer different questions and should not be combined. Only the first two bear directly on what entities exist; moral nihilism concerns value rather than world-existence.

Philosophy of time adds a further fork. Block-universe eternalism treats past, present, and future as equally real coordinates; presentism privileges the present. Relational theories deny time as an independent container — time is the order of change among things. Cosmological questions about a beginningless substrate are developed in §4.2; the philosophical point here is that Western ontology has no consensus on whether existence as a whole begins, even when particular configurations do. Activity-based accounts of duration are structurally closer to relational and process views than to block eternalism ([*Nature of Time*](../Nature-Of-Time/Nature-Of-Time.pdf) §3).

Ethics cannot be read directly from physicalism. Evolutionary theory may explain capacities for attachment, cooperation, punishment, and norm learning without deciding whether moral claims are true. Metaethical naturalists identify moral facts with natural facts; non-natural realists treat normativity as irreducible; constructivists ground norms in procedures of practical reason; contractualists appeal to justifiability to others; virtue theories organise ethics around human flourishing; existential approaches treat meaning as achieved through commitment. Adaptation is a biological outcome, not by itself an account of what a person ought to value or of life's ultimate purpose.

### 3.6 Alternative Western ontologies

The following ontologies are not historical curiosities appended to a physicalist consensus. Each remains a distinct answer to the relation between ground and particulars, the individuation and persistence of selves, causation, and teleology. Their comparisons with Madhyasth Darshan are structural rather than claims of identity.

### 3.6.1 Hylomorphism and soul-as-form

Aristotelian hylomorphism understands a living substance through matter and form: the soul is the principle by which the body is alive, rather than an additional body inside it. Aquinas develops this account by treating the rational soul as both the body's substantial form and a subsistent principle capable of surviving bodily death. The human person is nevertheless the soul–body composite; a separated soul is not the complete human being (Aquinas, ST I, q. 75, aa. 2, 4, 6; q. 76, a. 1). This further account of subsistence should not be attributed without qualification to every Aristotelian position.

The comparison with Madhyasth Darshan concerns substantial unity. For Aquinas, soul and matter constitute one human substance. MD's joint form joins a constitutionally complete sentient *jeevan* with a pranic body through which it acts; *jeevan* is not the body's substantial form. Both accounts can affirm many enduring souls or selves. The difference lies in what each bearer is and how embodiment belongs to it, not in whether there is one conscious bearer per body (§§1.7–1.9).

### 3.6.2 Spinoza: substance and modes

For Spinoza, God or Nature is one substance with infinitely many attributes; thought and extension are the attributes considered in his account of mind and body. Particular things are modes of this substance, not independent substances or parts that assemble it (*Ethics*, I, Def. 6, Props. 14–15). His parallelism relates the order of ideas to the order of things (II, Prop. 7). MD instead affirms units saturated in *satta*, without treating them as its modifications, and locates active sentience only in *jeevan*. Spinoza also rejects final causes in nature (I, Appendix), whereas MD makes completeness and fulfilled participation central to its account of activity. These are differences of dependence, consciousness, and purpose, despite the shared refusal of an external maker standing outside nature.

### 3.6.3 Leibniz: monads and harmony

Leibniz's monads are simple, partless substances. Each represents multiplicity through perception, which he distinguishes from reflective awareness. They cannot naturally arise or perish through composition or decomposition, although they depend on God for their existence (*Monadology*, §§1–7, 14–19). Plurality and persistence permit a comparison with *jeevan*, but the constitutional difference is decisive: a monad has no parts, while MD describes *jeevan* through a nucleus and orbital faculties. Perception also belongs to monads generally, whereas MD distinguishes insentient material units from sentient *jeevan*.

Monads are windowless: their coordination rests on pre-established harmony rather than efficient interaction between created substances (*Monadology*, §§7, 78–81). MD instead describes *jeevan* operating through a body in real mutuality. Its orderliness belongs to coexistence rather than to a creator's harmonisation of independently unfolding monadic histories.

### 3.6.4 Process philosophy: occasions and becoming

Whitehead's process philosophy shares relational realism and refusal of nature bifurcation. His basic units are "actual entities" — "drops of experience, complex and interdependent" (Whitehead 1929, p. 28). The ultimate for Whitehead is creativity, the principle by which the many prior occasions become one new occasion; each occasion's concrescence — its growing-together from that inherited data — aims at intensity of experience before it perishes (Whitehead 1929). The key contrast remains persistence: Whitehead's occasions are momentary and "perpetually perish" subjectively, gaining "objective immortality" only as data for successors (Whitehead 1929, p. 44), whereas Madhyasth Darshan's *jeevan* is a persisting immortal individual. Process metaphysics also treats time as internal to becoming — a partial affinity with activity-based *kaal* (§3.5; [*Nature of Time*](../Nature-Of-Time/Nature-Of-Time.pdf) §3.3).

> **"Actual entities 'perpetually perish' subjectively, but are immortal objectively."**
> - Whitehead 1929, p. 44

### 3.6.5 Neutral monism: common ground

Russell proposes analysing mind and matter through constituents that, considered in isolation, are neither mental nor material (*The Analysis of Mind*, Lecture I, printed p. 25). Mach likewise treats the psychical and physical as different connections among common elements rather than two kinds of substance (*The Analysis of Sensations*, Ch. XIV, printed p. 310). These approaches challenge the initial mind–matter division; they do not simply add a third substance beneath two unchanged domains.

The resemblance to MD is limited to refusing to classify the ground as an ordinary mind or material object. Neutral constituents enter the analysis of mind and matter, whereas *satta* does not transform into units or constitute them as its parts. Units remain real and active while the ground remains actionless.

### 3.6.6 Berkeley: spirits and ideas

Berkeley's idealism distinguishes active spirits from passive ideas. Sensible objects are collections of ideas dependent on perception; their order and involuntary character distinguish the experienced world from private imagination. Divine agency accounts for that order (*Principles of Human Knowledge*, §§2–3, 25–33, 89). MD also recognises active knowers, but affirms insentient units whose existence does not depend on being perceived. Berkeley retains plural spirits and God, so his idealism is not Advaita's ultimate non-duality either. This is one representative idealism, not a definition of every idealist philosophy.

These alternatives disagree about dependence, embodiment, relation, and persistence. Hylomorphic substances, Spinozist modes, Leibnizian monads, process occasions, neutral constituents, and Berkeleyan spirits cannot be treated as interchangeable names for MD's units.

### 3.7 Method, evidence, and what philosophy establishes

Modern Western philosophy yields several rival answers rather than one typical ontology. The following families group explanatory strategies and can overlap; Strawson's physicalist panpsychism is one example. The final column summarises the named alternatives individually, not as a shared doctrine:

| Question | Physicalist family | Panpsychist / neutral-monist family | Other alternatives |
|---|---|---|---|
| What is fundamental? | Physical entities, structures, events, or processes | Experience, proto-experience, or neutral intrinsic reality underlying physical structure | Form–matter substances, Spinoza's substance, monads, process occasions, or spirits and ideas |
| What is consciousness? | Identical with, realised by, emergent from, or functionally modelled in physical systems | Fundamental or rooted in the intrinsic nature of the physical | Power of an organised living substance, or a feature of actual occasions |
| What is the self? | Organism, psychological continuity, constituted person, narrative or self-model | Unified subject whose derivation remains contested | Embodied substance, persisting soul-form, or temporally ordered process |
| Does the individual survive bodily death? | Generally no; criteria terminate with organism or psychological continuity | Not entailed merely by fundamental experience | Aquinas and Leibniz allow forms of persistence; Whitehead's occasions perish subjectively |
| What is the world's status? | Usually mind-independent under realism; interpretation varies | Real, with intrinsic nature not exhausted by physics | Real as substances, modes, monads, processes, or mind-dependent ideas |
| How is the view justified? | Argument plus continuity with public science | Argument from experience and limits of structural physics | Metaphysical analysis of substance, dependence, relation, and becoming |

The hard problem exposes an explanatory obligation for reductive accounts, but it does not establish which non-reductive alternative is true. Physicalism, dualism, panpsychism, hylomorphism, neutral monism, and process metaphysics distribute the burdens differently: reduction, interaction, combination, individuation, or persistence remains difficult in each. None establishes constitutionally complete *jeevan* or post-death continuity merely from the existence of experience.

Modern science constrains these ontologies through measurement and prediction without, by that success alone, choosing one complete metaphysics. Its findings must therefore be stated separately.

## 4. Modern Scientific Approaches

Modern science seeks publicly testable regularities through observation, instrumentation, mathematical modelling, controlled intervention, replication, and criticism. Its ordinary models do not invoke non-physical intervention, but that methodological practice is not identical with the metaphysical thesis that only the physical exists. Scientific results constrain ontologies; they do not by themselves settle whether scientific realism, instrumentalism, structural realism, physicalism, or a non-reductive metaphysics is finally correct.

Madhyasth Darshan likewise connects evidence with experimentation, behaviour, and experience, while seeking comprehensive resolution through them (SB, p. 44). It treats the human body as provisioned to express understanding (JV, p. 59). Comparing its evidence standard with science requires distinguishing measured mechanisms from claims about what understands and how understanding becomes evident in living.

![Modern science concept map: empirical method constrains cosmology and spacetime, fields and conservation, thermodynamics and living organisation, mind and self-models, competing consciousness theories, and ecology without by itself determining a complete metaphysics or ultimate value](4-modern-science-concept-map.svg)

### 4.1 Scientific scope and ontological restraint

Physics models spacetime, fields, particles, interactions, and thermodynamic regularities. Biology models living organisation, inheritance, evolution, development, and ecological relation. Neuroscience and cognitive science study the neural and computational conditions associated with perception, action, self-representation, and reportable experience. None of these disciplines requires a complete inventory of everything that can exist before inquiry begins.

This restraint cuts in both directions. The success of physical models is evidence that physical structure and causal regularity cannot be ignored; it is not a proof that experience has been reductively explained. Conversely, the hard problem and the first-person datum do not establish an immortal *jeevan*. A responsible comparison asks what each model predicts, what it leaves open, and what additional claim would be required to move from empirical result to ontology.

### 4.2 Cosmology, spacetime, and cosmic history

The ΛCDM model reconstructs an observable universe developing from an early hot, dense state over roughly 13.8 billion years. Measurements of the cosmic microwave background, large-scale structure, expansion, and light-element abundance strongly support this history under the model. They do not establish creation from absolute non-being or prove that the hot early epoch was the first state of all existence (Planck Collaboration 2020).

General relativity treats spacetime geometry as dynamical: matter-energy and geometry constrain one another, while cosmological solutions describe expansion and, under classical extrapolation, singular boundaries. Alternative cosmological proposals explore whether those boundaries indicate a physical beginning or the breakdown of the classical model. Loop quantum cosmology models a bounce from a prior contracting phase (Ashtekar and Singh 2011); conformal cyclic cosmology proposes successive aeons (Penrose 2010); eternal inflation allows continuing inflation beyond an observable bubble (Guth 2007). These programs are mutually different and remain speculative. Their possibility shows only that current cosmology does not settle the metaphysical beginninglessness of existence.

Madhyasth Darshan treats *kaal* as duration of unit-activity, numerically reckoned by humans within existence (§1.4; SB, pp. 65, 251; MVD, pp. 34, 195), while *satta* is timeless because it is non-transforming. Relativity instead incorporates temporal relations within physical geometry. The comparison therefore concerns both ontology and level: measured spacetime is part of the physical model, whereas MD's *kaal* is an account of how duration belongs to activity. Fuller comparison appears in [*Nature of Time*](../Nature-Of-Time/Nature-Of-Time.pdf).

### 4.3 Quantum fields, particles, and conservation

Quantum field theory represents particles as quantised excitations of fields. Particle–antiparticle annihilation and pair creation are not transitions between being and absolute non-being: field states change while conserved quantities appropriate to the interaction are respected. Particle number itself need not be conserved, and fields are not “conserved entities.” Conservation applies to particular quantities through symmetries and dynamical conditions (Weinberg 1995).

This permits a limited structural comparison with Madhyasth Darshan's claim that *vastu* persists through changes of *roop* (§1.4), but the categories remain different. A QFT excitation is not an eternal bounded *ikai*, and a conservation law does not establish the perpetuity of every entity. In general relativity, global energy conservation is additionally subtle because a generic expanding spacetime lacks the global time-translation symmetry required for a simple conserved total energy (Carroll 2010).

Nor is *satta* a physical field. Dynamical fields carry energy-momentum, interact, and vary; *satta* is described as formless, actionless, and non-transforming (§1.2). A quantum vacuum is a physical state with field correlations and fluctuations. In some theories, non-zero field expectation values and spontaneous symmetry breaking are important features; boundary-dependent vacuum effects also have observable consequences. These are not universal properties of every field or vacuum. Their physical and theory-specific character prevents identifying a quantum vacuum with actionless Omnipresence (Weinberg 1995).

### 4.4 Thermodynamics, living organisation, and evolution

Thermodynamic entropy is a statistical quantity associated with the multiplicity of microscopic states compatible with a macroscopic description. For an isolated macroscopic system, entropy does not decrease, apart from statistical fluctuations; “disorder” is only a rough metaphor. A subsystem can become more organised while exporting entropy to its surroundings, as organisms and ecosystems do through exchanges of energy and matter. A closed system can still exchange heat, so closedness alone does not require its entropy to increase (Tong, *Statistical Physics*, §§1.2.1, 4.3.3). MD's *vyavastha* concerns orderliness, participation, and appropriate conduct, which thermodynamic entropy neither measures nor directly evaluates.

Autopoiesis describes living organisation as a self-producing network that continually regenerates the components and boundary through which the network exists (Maturana and Varela 1980, p. 78). It is a theory of living organisation, not by itself a criterion of consciousness. Its organisational closure resembles self-maintenance but not *gathanpurnata*: autopoiesis concerns metabolic networks, whereas Madhyasth Darshan identifies constitutional completeness with a particular sentient atom.

Evolutionary biology explains common descent and adaptation through heritable variation, selection, drift, recombination, developmental constraint, and ecological inheritance. It reconstructs branching lineages rather than a predetermined linear ascent. Origin-of-life research addresses the transition from non-living chemistry to self-maintaining and evolvable systems and should not be conflated with evolution after replication and heredity exist. Madhyasth Darshan's *niyati-kram* instead describes an ordered manifestation of material, pranic, animal, and knowledge orders (§1.6). Relating that ontological ordering to phylogeny, extinction, common descent, and multiple evolutionary lineages remains an open task (§6.2.3).

### 4.5 Mind, self-models, and the end of measurable function

Self-model theories explain minimal selfhood, bodily ownership, perspective, agency, and continuity through representations maintained by an embodied cognitive system (Metzinger 2003; Limanowski and Blankenburg 2013). Predictive-processing and active-inference frameworks model perception and action through hierarchical generative models, precision-weighted prediction errors, and policies that keep an organism within viable bounds (Friston 2010; Clark 2016). Prediction error, surprisal, and variational free energy are related formal quantities, not interchangeable names for one process.

These frameworks show how self-related experience may be organised without positing an immortal unit. They do not constitute the only scientific theory of personal identity, and a model of bodily self-representation does not by itself decide whether there is a further bearer of experience. Seth's “controlled hallucination” formulation emphasises that perceptual experience is an embodied, evidence-constrained construction, not that the world or self is an arbitrary fantasy (Seth 2021).

The cited United States consensus guideline treats brain death/death by neurologic criteria as death of the person, assessed through permanent loss of brain function as a whole and specified clinical criteria (AAN/AAP/CNS/SCCM 2023). That determination does not independently prove that no non-physical subject survives. The narrower conclusion relevant here is that accepted measurement has not established post-death continuity of an individual subject, while the capacities through which a person communicates and acts cease when the relevant organismal and neural functions are permanently lost.

### 4.6 Competing scientific theories of consciousness

Consciousness science has no settled explanatory theory. Global neuronal workspace theory associates conscious access with widespread availability produced by recurrent amplification and global broadcasting (Dehaene and Changeux 2011). Higher-order theories require an appropriate representation of a mental state as one's own (Lau and Rosenthal 2011). Recurrent-processing theory locates a sufficient condition in recurrent sensory processing without always requiring global broadcast (Lamme 2006). These theories primarily target access, report, metacognition, or perceptual awareness and make different neural predictions.

Integrated Information Theory 4.0 begins from proposed phenomenal properties — intrinsicality, information, integration, exclusion, and composition — and seeks their physical counterparts in a system's irreducible cause–effect structure (Albantakis et al. 2023). It is not adequately described as a single numerical Φ threshold that produces a self. Its exclusion principle and maximal complexes propose boundaries for experience, but the theory does not establish immortal individuality or solve the philosophical combination problem simply by quantifying integration.

The 2025 adversarial collaboration between IIT and global neuronal workspace theory tested preregistered predictions using fMRI, MEG, and intracranial recordings. The results challenged important predictions of both programs and did not select a settled winner (COGITATE Consortium 2025). The scientifically important contrast with Madhyasth Darshan is methodological: these programs expose system-level criteria to measurement, while the primary MD texts identify a constitutionally complete sentient atom without supplying a corresponding physical detection procedure (§6.2.1).

### 4.7 Ecology, cooperation, and the limits of biological function

Ecology treats organisms as participants in networks of energy flow, nutrient cycling, predation, competition, symbiosis, and niche construction. Nowak's five mechanisms for the evolution of cooperation are kin selection, direct reciprocity, indirect reciprocity, network reciprocity, and group selection (2006). Such models explain conditions under which cooperation can arise or persist; they do not establish that moral obligation is merely reproductive advantage.

Madhyasth Darshan makes a different claim. It treats complementarity, essentiality-as-value, and definite relationships as structures of coexistence, and it assigns value-recognition and fulfilment distinctively to the knowledge order (§1.13.1). At the human–nature relation it names utility (*upyogita*) and art (*kala*) — making natural abundance usable and enhancing that usefulness through skilled and aesthetic labour (MVD pp. 56–57, 324; JV p. 77). Biological function, ecological dependence, and coexistential value may overlap in cases, but they answer different questions.

Adaptation and survival are therefore not science's answer to life's ultimate purpose. They are explanatory outcomes within evolutionary models. Questions of flourishing, obligation, meaning, and final ends belong to philosophical and practical reasoning informed, but not settled, by biology.

### 4.8 Method, evidence, and what science establishes

| Question | Empirical scientific position | Metaphysical issue left open |
|---|---|---|
| What physical structures are observed? | Dynamical spacetime, quantum fields and excitations, organisms, brains, and ecological systems | Whether physical structure exhausts existence |
| Did existence begin? | ΛCDM describes an early hot dense epoch and subsequent observable history | Whether that epoch is an absolute beginning |
| What happens to the measurable person at death? | Integrated organismal and neural capacities irreversibly cease under accepted death criteria | Whether any non-physical bearer survives |
| What explains self-experience? | Competing models of embodiment, prediction, access, recurrence, integration, and metacognition | Whether those models explain phenomenal subjectivity completely |
| What explains cooperation? | Evolutionary, ecological, developmental, and cultural mechanisms | Whether value and obligation reduce to those mechanisms |
| How are claims tested? | Observation, instrumentation, modelling, intervention, replication, and peer criticism | Whether first-person or conduct evidence warrants additional ontology |

Science has established powerful mechanisms, quantitative constraints, and corrigible histories. It has not established immortal *jeevan*, constitutional completeness at *gathanpurnata*, or post-death continuity. Equally, its methodological success does not by itself prove metaphysical physicalism. The comparison in §5 must preserve both limits.

## 5. Comparison

Madhyasth Darshan, Advaita Vedanta, contemporary metaphysics, and modern science do not all offer the same kind of theory. The first two are comprehensive darshanas joining ontology, epistemology, liberation, and conduct. Contemporary philosophy contains rival metaphysical families. Science supplies empirically constrained models that admit more than one philosophical interpretation. Comparison is valid only when these differences of level are kept explicit.

The comparison therefore begins with common criteria rather than alliances. It asks what each view takes as fundamental, how it individuates beings, how particulars depend on a ground, what persists through change, what consciousness and selfhood are, how claims are justified, and whether value or purpose belongs to reality. Limited pairwise agreements emerge only after those axes are clear.

### 5.1 Criteria for comparison

Ten criteria organise the comparison:

1. Ontological primitives: what is taken as fundamental.
2. One–many structure and individuation: what makes an individual distinct.
3. Dependence between ground and particulars.
4. The world's status and persistence.
5. Causation and transformation.
6. Consciousness, selfhood, and survival.
7. Time and cosmic history.
8. Method and evidence.
9. Value, ethics, and purpose.
10. Exposure to empirical or experiential correction.

These axes reveal limited agreements without implying shared foundations. Madhyasth Darshan and Advaita both oppose reductive accounts of the first person, but panpsychism, dualism, and hylomorphism also do so. Madhyasth Darshan and scientific realism affirm a mind-independent world, but science does not establish MD's stronger claim that the world and all units are perpetual. Advaita and standard scientific accounts both decline to establish an eternal distinct individual self, but Advaita reaches that result through non-dual *shabda* and realisation, while science reports the dependence of measurable personal capacities on living organisation.

![Ten criteria for comparing Madhyasth Darshan, Advaita Vedanta, contemporary metaphysics, and modern science without conflating philosophical and empirical levels](5-comparison-criteria.svg)

Comparative clarity thus depends on naming both the question and the kind of answer being offered: metaphysical commitment, empirical model, contemplative disclosure, or criterion of conduct.

### 5.2 Cross-tradition summary

The first table compares metaphysical positions. “Physicalism” groups positions united by physical dependence. Panpsychist and neutral-monist strategies receive a separate column to make their claims about intrinsic nature visible; the headings can overlap, as Strawson's physicalist panpsychism shows (§3.3).

Their epistemic and axiological commitments complete the metaphysical comparison:

| Question | Madhyasth Darshan | Advaita Vedanta | Physicalist family | Panpsychist / neutral-monist family |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| How is the view known? | Study, experiment and practice, realisation, conduct-evidence | *Shabda*, reasoning, contemplative discrimination | Argument plus continuity with public science | Argument from experience and limits of structural explanation |
| Where does value enter? | Values and relationships are structurally real; humane conduct evidences awakening | Dharma and non-attached action at *vyavahara*; liberation through knowledge | Metaethical positions range from realism to constructivism; physicalism alone does not decide | Not fixed by the ontology of consciousness |

Empirical science supplies the constraints summarised in §4.8: publicly testable models of physical history, living organisation, and cognition. It does not occupy another column as a single complete metaphysics. Its findings matter across these positions, especially where they make claims about bodily function, causal interaction, and survival.

### 5.3 Key contrasts

### 5.3.1 Ground and existence

Existence itself is answered differently at the root. Madhyasth Darshan holds eternally present coexistence (*saha-astitva*): formless Omnipresence (*satta*) and countless real units (*ikai*) held in saturation, neither made from the other (§1.2). Advaita holds Brahman alone — being, consciousness, and limitless fullness without a second — as absolutely real; the manifold world has no independent being at the highest truth (§2.4). Physicalism takes concrete physical reality as exhaustive, though its members disagree about reduction and emergence (§3.1). Panpsychism and Russellian or neutral monism question whether physics reveals the intrinsic nature of that reality (§3.3, §3.6.5). Hylomorphism, Spinoza, Leibniz, and Whitehead supply further schemes of form–matter substance, modes of one substance, plural monads, and relational occasions (§3.6). None reproduces MD's two-term coexistence of actionless *satta* and permanently distinct units.

### 5.3.2 World realism

Whether the world retains final ontological weight divides Madhyasth Darshan from Advaita. Madhyasth Darshan holds *jagat satat*: the world remains real alongside the changeless ground (MVD, p. 13; §1.4). Advaita makes the empirical world robust for knowledge, duty, and liberation while denying it independent absolute being (§§2.4–2.5). Scientific realism agrees with MD only on mind-independence and investigability; it does not establish that the world or every unit is perpetual. Instrumentalism and constructive empiricism withhold even the realist inference from predictive success. The valid alignment is therefore MD with scientific realism against final world-subordination, not MD with science as such against Advaita.

### 5.3.3 Self, consciousness, and survival

Madhyasth Darshan holds many immortal, constitutionally complete *jeevan* units that work through bodies (§§1.7–1.8). Advaita holds that the true Self is Brahman and that separate *jivatman* belongs to conditioning at *vyavahara* (§2.6). Reductive and functional physicalisms identify selfhood with an organism, psychological organisation, or representational process; animalism, constitution, narrative, and enactive accounts supply different criteria of persistence (§3.4). Panpsychism makes experience fundamental but does not thereby secure an immortal personal subject, while hylomorphism and Leibniz preserve stronger individual bearers (§3.6). Scientific self-models explain bodily ownership, perspective, and regulation, and accepted death criteria mark the irreversible end of measurable personal function; neither result alone settles the metaphysical bearer question (§4.5).

### 5.3.4 Causation and beginninglessness

The positions diverge over whether effects have being apart from causes, whether a ground transforms, what individuates a persisting entity, and whether causal discourse is fundamental. Advaita teaches effect–cause non-difference and apparent transformation at *vyavahara* while withdrawing causal plurality at *paramartha*. Madhyasth Darshan separates non-transforming *mahakaran* from transforming units. Physical theories use dynamics, symmetries, constraints, probabilities, and boundary conditions rather than one universal doctrine of efficient causation. Emergentist, process, and hylomorphic accounts add distinct dependence relations. §5.4 compares these without treating conservation laws as metaphysical causes.

### 5.3.5 Method and what counts as evidence

The traditions use different but overlapping criteria for knowing. Madhyasth Darshan joins study through observation, examination, and survey (MVD pp. 88, 115), experiment and practice in behaviour and work (MVD p. 88; SB p. 44), and *anubhav* whose understanding becomes evident in humane conduct and tradition (MVD pp. 12, 88). Advaita treats Upanishadic *shabda* as uniquely competent for non-dual Brahman, with reasoning and contemplative discrimination removing contrary understanding (§2.9). Philosophy tests coherence, explanatory power, entailment, and fit with experience and science. Science tests operational predictions through observation, modelling, intervention, replication, and public criticism (§4.1).

| Inquiry | Principal method | Claimed reach | Exposed limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Madhyasth Darshan | Study → experiment and practice → realisation → conduct-evidence | Coexistence, *jeevan*, and humane order | Needs clearer intersubjective criteria for constitutionally complete *jeevan* |
| Advaita | *Shabda*, reasoning, contemplative discrimination | Non-dual identity and removal of superimposition | Its authority and sublation criterion are not shared by rival traditions |
| Contemporary philosophy | Conceptual argument, phenomenology, explanatory comparison, continuity with knowledge | Coherence and comparative plausibility of ontologies | Persistent disagreement and limited decisive tests |
| Modern science | Observation, instrumentation, modelling, intervention, replication | Public mechanisms, regularities, histories, and constraints | Does not alone decide a complete metaphysics or ultimate value |

No single tribunal settles every claim, but the views are not beyond comparison. Each can be tested for internal consistency, fidelity to its sources, phenomenological adequacy, fit with established public knowledge, clarity about what would count against it, and whether its practical evidence follows from its ontology. Fuller epistemology appears in [*The Epistemology of Coexistence*](../The-Epistemology-of-Coexistence/The-Epistemology-of-Coexistence.pdf) §1.6 and §5.3.

### 5.3.6 Ethics and life's purpose

Madhyasth Darshan makes value and relationship structural to coexistence and treats awakened humane conduct (*manaviya aacharan*) as evidence of understanding (§§1.13.1, 1.15). Advaita seeks *moksha* through knowledge of identity with Brahman; discipline prepares for knowledge, while non-attached action and *loka-sangraha* express knowledge without restoring ultimate agency (§2.10). Physicalism alone decides no ethics. Naturalist realism, non-natural realism, constructivism, contractualism, virtue ethics, and existential accounts remain open within broadly naturalistic philosophy (§3.5). Evolution and ecology explain capacities, functions, and consequences; adaptation and survival are not normative conclusions or ultimate purposes (§4.7). The comparison must therefore keep biological function, moral justification, liberation, and coexistential fulfilment distinct.

### 5.4 Causal doctrine: effect–cause non-difference, *Vivartavada*, and transformation

Causal doctrine exposes how differently the views understand dependence, transformation, persistence, and explanation (§5.3.4).

Advaita: the Chandogya clay teaching and Shankara's commentary on BS 2.1.14 establish effect–cause non-difference: the effect has no being apart from the cause. At *vyavahara*, Brahman associated with Maya is taught as both intelligent and material cause, while name-form appears without real alteration of Brahman. Later Advaita calls this *vivarta* in contrast with *parinama*. At *paramartha*, cause, effect, Ishvara, and world are not independent second realities, so causal teaching is ultimately withdrawn rather than promoted into a final mechanism.

Madhyasth Darshan: *Satta* is *mahakaran*, the non-transforming sustaining condition rather than an efficient maker (§1.4). It is neither efficient cause (*nimitta*) — it performs no action and issues no commands — nor material cause (*upadana*) that becomes the units. Units of nature undergo real transformation through *shram–gati–parinam*; compositions form and decompose, while *vastu* persists through changes of *roop*. Development, constitutional completeness, awakening, order-specific cycles, and *sanskar*-mediated human continuity cannot be reduced to one linear chain. The resemblance to *parinamavada* is limited to real transformation at the level of units; MD rejects the thesis that one material cause transforms into the entire plurality.

Classical Samkhya affirms two kinds of irreducible principle: many conscious witnesses (*purusha*) and one active, non-conscious *prakriti*. Its twenty-four-principle material account includes root *prakriti* and twenty-three evolutes; purusha is distinct from that sequence (*Samkhya Karika* 3, 18–22). Intellect (*buddhi*) and mind (*manas*) belong to non-conscious prakriti (Karikas 22, 27), whereas MD places its five faculties within sentient *jeevan*. The resemblance in plural conscious bearers therefore has a limit: Samkhya's purusha is an inactive witness, while MD's jeevan is active. Samkhya's liberation is discriminative disengagement (*kaivalya*, Karikas 62–68); MD's awakening fulfils participation in coexistence. These different final aims do not by themselves establish how responsibly adherents of either view live.

Physical explanation and its metaphysical interpretation must be distinguished. Physics uses dynamical equations, symmetries, conserved quantities, probabilistic transitions, spacetime structure, constraints, and boundary conditions. These are not all instances of classical efficient causation, and conservation laws are not themselves causes. Biological explanation adds mechanism, development, function, selection, and historical dependence. A physicalist interpretation denies the need for a non-physical sustaining ground, while emergentist and process views disagree about whether higher-level causation or internal becoming is irreducible.

| Aspect | Advaita | Madhyasth Darshan | Physical explanation | Process / emergent alternatives |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ground–effect relation | Effect has no being apart from Brahman at *vyavahara* | Units coexist in *satta* but are not made from it | Models systems through physical structure and history; no transcendent ground appears in the model | Becoming or higher-level organisation may be irreducible |
| Does the ground transform? | Brahman does not; *vivarta* names apparent transformation | *Satta* does not; units really transform | Fields and spacetime can be dynamical; no universal static substrate is established | Whitehead's creativity is expressed through perishing occasions; strong emergence posits novel powers |
| What persists? | Brahman; name-form lacks independent being | Real units and *vastu* through *roop*-change | Theory-specific conserved quantities and causal/structural continuities | Organised substance, processual inheritance, or emergent pattern |
| Does causation remain ultimate? | No; causal plurality is withdrawn at *paramartha* | Real unit-activity remains within perpetual coexistence | Usually yes as physical dependence or dynamics, though its interpretation varies | Relation, form, process, or emergence may be more basic than efficient cause |
| Beginninglessness | Brahman beginningless; empirical cycles taught within *vyavahara* | Coexistence and units do not arise from *abhava* | Observable cosmic history is finite under ΛCDM; absolute beginning unsettled | Varies by system |

The comparison shows why conservation cannot be used as a direct scientific confirmation of either *vivarta* or MD's perpetual units. It supports transformation without creation from everyday nothing in specific models; the ontological interpretation remains additional.

### 5.5 Entity comparison

The overview in §5.2 does not make similarly positioned concepts equivalent. This section identifies the principal mismatches between proposed counterparts, then distinguishes an entity's constitution from its relations and scientific description. Abrahamic and Dvaita ontologies remain outside scope.

#### 5.5.1 Ground, self, and matter

| MD concept | Proposed counterpart | Why the concepts are not interchangeable |
|---|---|---|
| Ground: *satta* | Brahman, Spinoza's substance, or neutral constituents | Brahman alone is ultimately real; Spinoza's particulars are modes; neutral constituents enter the constitution of mind and matter. MD's units are neither appearances nor modifications nor parts of *satta*. |
| Sentient bearer: *jeevan* | Atman, rational soul, monad, or actual occasion | Atman is non-dual; Aquinas's soul is the body's substantial form; a monad is partless; Whitehead's occasion perishes subjectively. MD's sentient atom has a closed constitution and remains individually distinct. |
| Material embodiment: body | Advaita's gross body or a physicalist organism | Advaita gives the body empirical reality; physicalist accounts make physical organisation sufficient for concrete embodiment. MD's body is a real pranic composition operating jointly with *jeevan*. |

#### 5.5.2 Relationships, causation, and purpose

MD affirms both definite bearers and real relations. Boundary, constitution, capacity, order, and *dharma* belong to the unit; manifested *guna*, usefulness, and fulfilled participation become evident in mutuality. A bearer is therefore neither an empty relational position nor something whose contribution can be understood wholly in isolation (§§1.3, 1.13.1).

Relational dependence does not have the same meaning in every ontology. Whitehead makes inherited relations constitutive of a new occasion; Aquinas's formal cause explains the unity of a living substance; Leibniz coordinates monads without efficient interaction. None is simply the saturation relation of units in actionless *satta* (§§3.6, 5.4). Their accounts of fulfilment also require their own premises. Hylomorphic flourishing, Whiteheadian intensity, Advaita's liberation, and MD's humane conduct cannot be inferred merely from the shared word “relation.”

#### 5.5.3 Empirical description and its ontological limits

Scientific entities are identified within models and procedures: a particle by its physical properties and interactions, an organism by its living organisation, and a neural correlate through measurable relations to experience or behaviour. Equating any of these with an ontological category requires shared identifying criteria. Similar words such as “atom,” “energy,” or “order” do not supply those criteria. Conversely, a metaphysical claim about a unit's bodily effects remains answerable to relevant observations even when its full interpretation exceeds the scientific model (§§4.3–4.6, 6.2).

### 5.6 The Sat-Chit-Ananda contrast, distributed

Advaita's Sat-Chit-Ananda and Madhyasth Darshan's coexistence both speak of being, consciousness, and fulfilment. The contrast concerns what those words name and whether world and individual plurality retain ultimate reality. It develops the ground and sentient-bearer comparison in §5.5.1.

For Advaita, *sat*, *chit*, *ananda* are three names for one ultimate reality without a second; the world is finally *mithya*. For Madhyasth Darshan, the same vocabulary is distributed across coexistence, not compressed into one subject:

| Concept | Advaita Vedanta (Unified Non-Dualism) | Madhyasth Darshan (Distributed Coexistence) |
|---|---|---|
| Sat (Being) | Brahman alone is absolutely real; the world's attributed independent reality is sublated. | Beingness (*astitva*) is coexistence of formless Omnipresence and countless real units. |
| Chit (Consciousness) | Pure awareness, identical to the inner Self; the world of names/forms is inert. | Omnipresence is pervasive knowledge/condition-of-intelligibility (§1.2), but active sentience (*chaitanya*) belongs to the *jeevan* unit. |
| Ananda (Bliss) | Intrinsic nature of Brahman; sensory joy is a distorted reflection. | Harmony realised within awakened *jeevan* — bliss as buddhi–*atma* harmony and humane conduct — not Brahman's intrinsic *ananda* alone (§1.9). |

The *chit* row sharpens the difference between seers. Advaita's witness, once discriminated, is Brahman itself, without a second knower. MD's knower is one of many constitutionally complete *jeevan* units, each capable of knowledge unfolding when awakened (§1.8). Both resist reduction of the first person to an observed brain-state. Dualism, panpsychism, hylomorphism, and other non-reductive views can share that resistance while giving different accounts of its bearer. The specific dispute here is whether irreducible awareness is one non-dual Self or many permanently distinct knowers.

Modern philosophy has no single parallel formula. Panpsychism makes experience fundamental, Russellian monism distinguishes physical structure from intrinsic nature, and process philosophy joins actuality with experience and becoming; none distributes being, sentience, and fulfilment in the precise Madhyasth Darshan pattern or identifies them as Advaita's one Brahman.

### 5.7 Advaita structural fork: analogues and unmappable categories

Advaita Vedanta and Madhyasth Darshan arise from different core commitments — non-dualism and co-existentialism — yet share portions of an Indian philosophical vocabulary. Four terms permit limited functional comparison; they are not close ontological equivalents. Several further categories required by Advaita have no Madhyasth counterpart. Advaita gives no independent absolute being to world or individual plurality, whereas Madhyasth Darshan affirms real distinct *jeevan* and perpetual coexistence.

The four analogue pairs, before the detail in §§5.7.1–5.7.4:

| Advaita category | Madhyasth Darshan analogue | Shared ground | Where they diverge |
|---|---|---|---|
| Brahman | *Satta* (Omnipresence) | Formless, all-pervasive, non-transforming, timeless, actionless | Brahman is the *sole* absolute reality (world *mithya*); *Satta* coexists with real nature (§5.7.1) |
| *Atman* / *Paramatman* | *Jeevan* (immortal sentient unit; *atma* as nucleus) | Both deny that the conscious principle is reducible to the gross body | One universal non-dual Self vs. many permanently distinct, active *jeevan* units (§5.7.2) |
| Conditioned *jiva* | Deluded or *bhramit jeevan* | Embodied selfhood involves ignorance and body-identification | Advaita denies final individuality; MD treats individuality as real and corrects its understanding (§5.7.3) |
| *Jagat* / name-form | Nature / units (*prakriti* / *ikai*) | The physical universe of change, bodies, name, and form | *Jagat* is ultimately *mithya* and sublated vs. *jagat* is *satat* — perpetual and eternally real (§5.7.4) |

Categories Advaita needs with no Madhyasth counterpart — Maya, *adhyasa*, *mithya*, Ishvara as Brahman associated with Maya, and liberation as non-dual knowledge — are treated separately in §5.7.5; forcing them into the table above would misstate an asymmetry as a parallel.

#### 5.7.1 Brahman and *Satta*

The shared ground is formless, all-pervasive, non-transforming, timeless, and actionless. The divergence is decisive: Advaita's Brahman is the *sole* absolute reality (*paramartha*), making the world dependent appearance (*mithya*); Madhyasth Darshan's *Satta* coexists with nature (*prakriti*), both real. Brahman is awareness itself (*chit*); *Satta* is actionless energy (*kriya-shunya urja*) — the condition of intelligibility — while active sentience (*chaitanya*) belongs exclusively to the *jeevan* unit (§1.2).

#### 5.7.2 *Atman*, *Paramatman*, and *Jeevan*

The limited commonality is negative: neither tradition identifies the conscious principle with the gross body. Advaita holds that the true Self (*Atman*) is non-dual Brahman and never an individual substance. Madhyasth Darshan holds that *jeevan* units are permanently distinct, multiple, active, and individually immortal. Within *jeevan*, *atma* names the nuclear faculty of realisation — not a counterpart of Advaita's universal Self. The analogy must therefore not begin from “the same immortal core”; the ontological bearer is precisely what differs.

The traditions share *kosha* vocabulary without sharing its ontological classification. MD distributes the five koshas across material, pranic, animal, and human living, including the fuller capacity of awakened human understanding (MVD, pp. 49–50; §1.6). This classification is distinct from both its five faculties of *jeevan* and its four developmental planes. Advaita instead discriminates the Self from all five sheaths of the empirical person (VC, vv. 149–153, 209–211). Neither the named koshas nor their ordering supplies a one-to-one map between the two accounts.

#### 5.7.3 *Jiva* and deluded *Jeevan*

Both name the individual embodied self bound by ignorance and body-identification. In Advaita, the *jivatman–Paramatman* distinction operates at *vyavahara* through limiting adjuncts. Knowledge reveals that it never divided the Self into independently real beings (CU 6.8.7; BS 1.4.22; VC, v. 244). Ordinary bodily death leaves empirical rebirth in place; liberation ends ignorance without producing a new identity (§§2.6, 2.9). For MD, awakening resolves delusion while preserving the active, distinct individuality of *jeevan*.

#### 5.7.4 *Jagat*, name-form, and units

Both point to the physical universe of change, bodies, name, and form. In Advaita, the Jagat is ultimately *mithya* and sublated at the highest realisation. In Madhyasth Darshan, the world of units is *satat* (perpetual) and eternally real.

The same fork appears when Advaita's three tiers of reality (*satta-traividhya*) are set beside Madhyasth Darshan's three truths (*teen satya*). The mark of the Advaita tiers is sublation (*badha*): higher knowledge withdraws the lower as not finally real. The mark of the three truths is complementarity: realisation does not cancel the unit or its relations; it makes their harmony in coexistence evident.

| Question | Advaita: three tiers | Madhyasth Darshan: three truths |
|---|---|---|
| What is counted? | Apparent, empirical, and absolute reality (*pratibhasika*, *vyavaharika*, *paramarthika*) | Inherent, positional, and existential truth (*vastugat*, *vastu-sthiti*, *sthiti satya*) |
| How do the levels relate? | Knowledge corrects the reality attributed to an appearance | The three aspects complete one another |
| Status of the unit and world | Dependent appearance, never independently absolute | Real unit, real mutuality, real saturation |
| What realisation does | Removes the attribution of independent reality to the world | Reveals coexistence without cancelling units |

#### 5.7.5 Categories Advaita needs that Madhyasth Darshan rejects

Several central Advaita categories have no Madhyasth equivalent because co-existentialism explicitly rejects them.

Later Advaita commonly describes Maya through concealment (*avarana*) and projection (*vikshepa*), while Shankara's indispensable commitment is that name-form has no independent being and the *jiva* superimposes body-mind predicates on the Self (§2.5). Madhyasth Darshan has no cosmic power or substance of illusion. Ignorance (*agnyan* / *bhrama*) is a correctable absence or distortion of understanding, resolved through study, realisation, education, and conduct rather than through sublation of the world's reality.

In Advaita, world and individuality are understood through superimposition (*adhyasa*) and effect–cause non-difference (BS 2.1.14 with Śaṅkara's *bhāṣya*; §2.5). Madhyasth Darshan has no ontological superimposition. Units are saturated (*samprikt*) in *satta*: a real, non-reductive relation in which units are active while *satta* remains actionless (§1.2; SB, pp. 57, 62, 69). “Mutual” must not imply reciprocal action by *satta*. Nor should Advaita *adhyasa* be confused with Madhyasth Darshan's homonym in the animal order — species-traditional inertial impression from gestation (§1.6; SB, pp. 62–63).

Advaita's *mithya* describes the world's dependent status: neither independently absolute nor sheer non-being (CU 6.2.1–6.2.2 with Śaṅkara's *bhāṣya*; VC, v. 251; §§2.4–2.5). Madhyasth Darshan rejects this intermediate status. Existence and real units are not annihilated, while compositions and forms arise, transform, and decompose. “Perpetual” therefore applies to coexistence and the underlying reality of units, not to every configuration or body.

In Advaita, Ishvara is Brahman associated with Maya and taught at *vyavahara* as the non-different material and efficient cause (*abhinna-nimitta-upadana-karana*) of name-form, not a creator *ex nihilo* (§§2.5–2.6). Madhyasth Darshan rejects a cosmic governor or efficient creator who plans the world or dispenses its order. That is not rejection of a pervasive ground: MVD's “Reality” list includes “God is omnipresent, deities are many” (MVD p. 13), where *God* names actionless Omnipresence and no deity is elevated into the ultimate maker. Orderliness (*vyavastha*) is self-regulation (*swatah-saspurt*) in the coexisting orders (§1.6.1); *satta* remains *kriya-shunya*.

Advaita seeks *moksha*: recognition of the ever-established identity of Atman and Brahman, in which separate individuality is known never to have been absolute (CU 6.8.7; BS 1.1.4, 1.4.22). MD's awakened coexistence preserves distinct knowers and real relationships. Shared vocabulary therefore locates a disagreement about what knowledge reveals; it does not remove that disagreement.

## 6. Critical Review

The comparison raises three kinds of question: whether each account is internally coherent, whether its sources support the synthesis presented here, and what reasons favour it over alternatives. Textual support answers the second question; it does not by itself answer the other two.

### 6.1 Internal coherence of the Madhyasth Darshan position

MD connects coexistence, real units, beginninglessness, sentience, and fulfilment in one account. Its world-realism preserves the ontological weight of nature and relationships, while its definition of human *dharma* as happiness and its account of value connect understanding to humane conduct (§§1.3, 1.13.1, 1.15). These are more substantial premises than the bare statement that things coexist.

The distinction between an enduring bearer and a changing configuration also makes bodily death intelligible within the system without requiring annihilation of existence. Whether this establishes the persistence and personal continuity of a particular *jeevan* remains a further question (§§1.4, 1.7.1, 6.2.2).

These are strengths within the system's premises. They do not by themselves establish those premises against Advaita or competing modern ontologies (§§6.2–6.4).

### 6.2 Open problems for Madhyasth Darshan

The principal open questions concern identifying *jeevan*, explaining persistence and embodiment, relating evidence standards to scientific history, and justifying the connection between existence and obligation.

#### 6.2.1 Constitutional completeness and public identification

Madhyasth Darshan gives a definite account of the sentience threshold. An evolving-constitution atom remains under molecular and weight bondage; through contraction and expansion it becomes free from those bondages, its particle constitution closes, and it becomes the hope-bound sentient atom called *jeevan* (MVD, p. 91; SB, pp. 55, 59, 114). The primary texts locate sentience in constitutionally complete *jeevan*, not in biological complexity alone. Omnipresence remains actionless; the knower is *jeevan*. The darshan locates a qualitative transition within development in the atom and distinguishes it from the compositional progression that produces molecules, cells, and bodies (§§1.5, 1.7–1.8).

This is a specific ontological proposal. Its open evidential problem is that the texts do not provide a public procedure for identifying the proposed closed particle constitution, release from weight bondage, or hope-bondage in contemporary physical terms. A resemblance to thresholds in emergentism or integrated-information theories does not establish identity: those theories locate their thresholds in organised systems (§§3.3, 4.6). The question is what observation could distinguish constitutional completeness from the capacities already attributed to a living physical system.

The physics vocabulary also needs an explicit interpretation. The source places electromagnetic, gravitational, weak, strong, and mediative forces alongside the sentient faculties (SB, pp. 84–85), while distinguishing imaging from motion and pressure (SB, pp. 251–252). A literal physical correspondence requires measurable quantities, units, and discriminating predictions. An analogy requires a stated common relation and a limit to the correspondence. Calling every difficult passage metaphorical would remove a claim the source may intend; treating familiar names as established physical identities would assert more than has been shown.

#### 6.2.2 Persistence and the *jeevan*–body joint form

Quantity conservation alone does not establish personal survival: a quantity such as charge may be conserved while an organised system loses its identity. MD's case adds constitutional completeness: the closed constitution no longer gains or loses particles and is free from molecular and weight bondage (MVD, p. 91; SB, pp. 55, 59, 114). Its statement that *jeevan* continues after bodily death and can work through another body presupposes this account (JV, PDF pp. 20, 55; printed pp. 19, 54). Persistence follows within MD from this further premise, rather than from a generic conservation law (§§1.7.1, 1.9).

The broader argument from saturation has a related limit. The source connects non-annihilation with the fact that every fragment remains saturated when a unit is divided (SB, p. 57). Continued saturation of fragments supports their presence within coexistence; it does not, by itself, prove that no entity could cease to exist or that a composite retains its identity after division. The doctrine of perpetual existence and its proposed justification therefore need to be distinguished.

Even if an enduring constitution is granted, individuation remains a separate question: what makes this *jeevan* this individual? The role of *sanskar*, memory, and personal history must be clarified, including which continuities persist across bodily death and which would count as evidence of the same individual. Equal constitutional capacities do not supply a complete criterion of personal identity.

The remaining question is whether this persisting unit and its reassociation can be independently evidenced. Madhyasth Darshan describes the human as a joint form: the body is a material instrument, *jeevan* is the sentient seer–doer–enjoyer, and the two operate together through the body's nervous and sensory organisation (MVD, pp. 115, 288–289; SB, pp. 91–92). The account distinguishes two sources of limitation. An animal body, or an impaired human nervous system, constrains what can be expressed through the bodily medium; it does not imply an incomplete *jeevan*. A normally developed human body supplies the provision for complete understanding, but body-identification and the resulting organisation around sensitivity leave only four and a half activities effectively evidenced until knowing and believing guide recognition and fulfilment (JV, pp. 73–74, 92–94).

Since *jeevan* is described as an atom rather than a Cartesian non-physical substance, the issue should not be prejudged as an injection of energy from outside nature. Yet the texts do not translate transmission between *jeevan* and the nervous system into measurements that distinguish it from a brain-only account. Causal closure, organismal and psychological criteria of persistence, and the loss of measurable personal function at death all bear on this question (§§3.1, 3.4, 4.5). The missing evidence concerns how bodily impairment constrains expression, what difference *jeevan* makes to bodily activity, and what could establish post-death continuity.

#### 6.2.3 Evidence standards and evolutionary scope

MD expects understanding to become publicly evident through resolution, justice, mutual fulfilment, and participation in orderliness (MVD, pp. 88, 115; SB, p. 44; §§1.14, 5.3.5). This is a substantial practical evidence standard. Its relation to ontological evidence still needs explanation: how does evidence of humane conduct discriminate the proposed constitution of *jeevan* from rival accounts that also permit such conduct? Logical argument, first-person verification, conduct, and instrument-based measurement can inform one another without functioning as interchangeable tests.

The apparent conflict between *niyati-kram* and evolutionary contingency also needs narrowing. MD's progression names an ordered relation among material, bio, animal, and knowledge orders, while evolutionary biology explains branching changes within living populations through variation, inheritance, selection, drift, development, and contingency (§§1.6, 4.4). These claims are not direct contradictories merely because one uses definite order language and the other uses contingency: they operate at different descriptive levels. The sharper questions are whether MD requires each transition to occur necessarily, how its compositional claims map onto biological descent, and what evidence would distinguish a necessary existential progression from a retrospective classification of the forms that happened to arise. Until that mapping is supplied, *niyati-kram* remains a distinctive commitment of the darshan rather than a result established by evolutionary science.

#### 6.2.4 Value, fulfilment, and normative grounding

MD's case for humane conduct combines several premises: human *dharma* is happiness; value expresses essentiality and fulfilled participation; and understanding identifies definite expectations in relationships (§§1.3, 1.13.1, 1.15). Coexistence by itself would establish only that things exist together, not what a person ought to do. The normative argument depends on those fuller claims about human fulfilment and the conditions under which it is realised.

The critical questions are therefore whether those premises are independently defensible, how they distinguish beneficial participation from harmful but causally effective activity, and how disagreements over actual fulfilment are resolved. Evidence in conduct is relevant to this task, but it must assess the relationship and its result rather than assume that every naturally occurring relation is already humane. The detailed argument belongs to [*Axiology: Value Theory*](../Axiology-Value-Theory/Axiology-Value-Theory.pdf).

### 6.3 Advaita — strengths and limits

Advaita combines ontological economy with a developed account of error and liberation. Its argument against existence arising from non-existence (CU 6.2.2), seer-seen discrimination, and analysis of waking, dream, and deep sleep address different aspects of the relation between experience and reality. Empirical ethics, *loka-sangraha*, and beginningless cosmology give the world practical and explanatory standing (§§2.1–2.10).

MD's principal objection concerns the ultimate status of that world. It requires distinct knowers and real relationships to remain at the highest understanding, whereas Advaita denies ultimate separateness. That is a substantive disagreement in premises; failure to reproduce MD's categories is not itself an internal contradiction in Advaita. Nagraj frames the origination question directly:

> **"How can the jeeva and jagat, which originated from the Truth, Knowledge, and Infinite Brahma, be an illusion?"**
> - MVD, *The Alternative*

This question concerns how a world dependent on Brahman can lack independent reality. It is related to, but not identical with, later controversies about the locus of ignorance. The quoted objection alone does not establish an engagement with a particular later school's solution.

Advaita's reply distinguishes empirical causal instruction from ultimate non-duality: name-form never had independent being apart from Brahman (BS 2.1.14; §§2.4–2.6). It does not claim that Brahman manufactured a second independently real world and later made it unreal. The remaining dispute is whether the arguments for sublation warrant giving plurality this dependent status, and whether empirical reality adequately grounds the continuing significance of relationships. MD answers the second question by retaining ultimate plurality; Advaita rejects the demand that responsibility at *vyavahara* requires such plurality. Comparing those answers requires examining their reasons for the distinction between empirical and ultimate reality, not merely counting their fundamental entities.

### 6.4 Modern approaches — strengths and limits

**Strengths.**

- Scientific approaches are strongest where Madhyasth Darshan is least operational: public measurement, empirical correction, cognitive modelling, and precise mechanism. Self-model theory and predictive processing explain embodied selfhood and regulation without requiring an eternal *jeevan* (§4.5), while consciousness programs expose distinct predictions to comparative testing (§4.6).
- Contemporary philosophy adds conceptual pressure from several sides rather than one physicalist front. Causal exclusion, animalism, panpsychist combination, hylomorphic embodiment, and processual persistence each test a different MD commitment. Scientific realism aligns with MD on mind-independent nature, but only on that question (§5.3.2).

**Open limits.**

- The hard problem remains: correlation of brain and experience is not disputed, but why physical processing is accompanied by subjectivity remains open (§3.2), and rival consciousness programs lack consensus (§4.6). That is not proof of *jeevan*; it marks an explanatory burden for reductive accounts and a branching point among several non-reductive alternatives.
- Non-reductive alternatives face their own identifying and explanatory tasks: subject-combination for micropsychism, individuation for cosmopsychism, and the relation between an enduring bearer and embodied activity for soul theories. These are distinct problems; their existence does not make all alternatives equally supported (§§3.3–3.6).

Modern scientific and philosophical approaches therefore supply indispensable mechanisms and public tests without yet delivering a single settled ontology of consciousness or selfhood. Predictive models explain aspects of bodily regulation and self-experience but do not by themselves settle the ontological bearer; an explanatory gap is not an existence proof for *jeevan*, and a successful mechanism is not automatically a complete metaphysics.

## 7. Conclusion

Madhyasth Darshan's distinctive claim is coexistence: formless Omnipresence (*satta*) and countless real units (*ikai*), beginningless and neither made from the other. The plural world remains real in its own right.

> **"Brahma is truth, the world is perpetual."**
> - MVD, p. 13

MD connects this world-realism to a distinct sentient bearer and an account of fulfilment. Units retain definite constitutions and capacities; their properties, usefulness, and participation become evident in mutuality. Human knowing can understand that order or mis-evaluate it. Awakening is fulfilled when understanding regulates conduct and supports mutually fulfilled relationships.

The comparison locates both agreements and decisive differences. Scientific realism also affirms mind-independent nature, but does not establish perpetual units or actionless *satta*. Advaita and several non-reductive philosophies resist reducing experience to a physical description, but disagree about the number, constitution, and persistence of its bearers. In Advaita, empirical rebirth is compatible with ultimate non-duality; in MD, awakening preserves distinct *jeevan*. These differences remain after shared vocabulary is clarified.

Further work must address public identification of constitutional completeness and the interpretation of physical vocabulary (§6.2.1); individuation, persistence, and joint operation with a body (§6.2.2); the relation between conduct evidence, scientific measurement, and evolutionary history (§6.2.3); and the premises connecting coexistence to obligation (§6.2.4). The primary texts support a connected ontology. Establishing its claims against alternatives requires further argument and evidence at these points.

| *Sanvedansheelta* / *sangyansheelta* | Sensitivity through the body and senses, and cognisance of relationship, value, and orderliness through *jeevan*. Awakened living balances them by regulating sensitivity through understanding (§1.8). |

| Way of existence (*niyati-vidhi*) | Definiteness in the conduct of each of the four orders; paired with *niyati-kram* (order emergence ↔ order conduct; §1.6.1). |
| Development progression (*vikas-kram*) | Atomic-level movement through the physicochemical complex until an atom reaches constitutional completeness and sentient *jeevan* appears (§1.12). |
| Awakening progression (*jagriti-kram*) | Sentient-level movement within constitutionally complete *jeevan* toward fuller activity and conduct (§1.12). |
| Padmukti | Release from incomplete developmental standing in the divine or complete condition; *jeevan* continues to exist (§1.12.2). |
| Prarabdha | Results still to be undergone within the relation between knowing, desiring, doing, and enjoying (§1.10). |

| Object-value family | Usefulness and art or aesthetic value; two object values within the thirty-value canon (§1.13.1). |
| Utility value (*upyogita*) | What an object can fulfil through its definite properties. Changing price does not change that function; material condition and conditions of use can change actual fulfilment (JV p. 123; §1.13.1). |
| Art (*kala*) / art value (*kala-mulya*) | *Kala* makes qualities evident and expression meaningful, joining usefulness and beauty. Art value names its useful and aesthetic accomplishment in an object; the activity also serves the presentation and communication of understanding (§§1.13.1, 1.14; PS p. 56). |
| Human values | The six values of fortitude, courage, generosity, kindness, grace, and compassion through which humaneness is evidenced (§§1.13.1, 1.6). |
| Established values | The nine relationship values—trust, respect, affection, care, guidance, reverence, glory, gratitude, and love—borne at *chitta*, *vritti*, and *mun* and flowing when relationships are recognised (MAD reader p. 30; JV pp. 108, 138; §1.13.1). |

| Value, character, ethics | The content of humane fulfilment, definite humane conduct, and policies supporting humaneness; together they constitute the account of humane conduct (§1.13.3). |
| Evaluative domain | Conduct, thought, or realisation — the three humane perspectives discipline distinct domains (§1.13.2). |
| Sentient mode | How effort–motion–result maps once constitutional completeness is reached — distinct from insentient unit-activity (§§1.10–1.11). |

| Methodological naturalism | A rule of inquiry seeking publicly testable explanations without invoking non-natural interventions; it is not itself the metaphysical thesis that only the physical exists. |

Scientific realism, structural realism, instrumentalism, and constructive empiricism offer competing interpretations of what successful science warrants. The metaphysical families above also contain internally diverse positions (§§3.1–3.7).

## Editorial Notes

### Scope, text editions, and pagination conventions

Their page references use one-based positions in the linked PDFs; JV's printed page is one below that position. *Paribhasha Samhita* (PS) uses printed pages, eight below PDF position; *Manav Abhyas Darshan* (MAD) uses displayed native-reader pages. *Manav Karm Darshan* (KD) is used through the Hindi text and an identified working-English paraphrase where the Hindi has been checked. Its section numbers and printed pages are specified where used.

Full knowability is treated in [*The Epistemology of Coexistence*](../The-Epistemology-of-Coexistence/The-Epistemology-of-Coexistence.pdf) §7.1; time, *trikaalabadh*, and spacetime in [*Nature of Time*](../Nature-Of-Time/Nature-Of-Time.pdf); and institutional governance, *dharma-niti*, *rajya-niti*, and statutory order in the planned [*Governance Justice and Undivided Society*](../Governance-Justice-And-Undivided-Society/Governance-Justice-And-Undivided-Society.pdf). [*How to Form Self-Sustaining Organizations*](../How-To-Form-Self-Sustaining-Organizations/How-To-Form-Self-Sustaining-Organizations.pdf) applies the assembly template socially.

### English translations and names for *satta* (*vyapak*)

The translations alternate “Omnipotence” and “Omnipresence” for the same all-pervasive reality, *satta*; *vyapak* means all-pervasive. This study uses “Omnipresence” in analytical prose to avoid the theistic implications of “Omnipotence”; quotations retain their source wording. The translated “Space” renders *shunya*, formless emptiness, rather than physical *akasha*.

Uniform Energy, Knowledge, Consciousness, Eternity, God, and Absolute Energy also name this reality (SB p. 48). Their meaning follows the relations explained at MVD p. 35: bliss through realisation, truth through uniform realisability, supreme reality through conservation and regulation, pervasiveness through uniform presence, consciousness in relation to sentient nature, and absolute energy through saturated units' activity. Completeness names the ground's absence of transformation. Absolute and relative energy are distinguished at MVD p. 40.

This study uses Light (*prakash*) for *satta*. PS p. 80 describes realisation in the pervasive as eternal light (*shashvat prakash*) and gives it the name *jyoti*. PS p. 137 includes *jyoti* in the names of *Brahm*, alongside pervasive reality, uniform satta, knowledge, and consciousness. The source word *jyoti* is retained here to explain those passages; *prakash* is the term used in the exposition and glossary. The account of *satta* as light in §1.1 synthesises these connected uses, while preserving the contextual meanings of *prakash* discussed below.

These names do not attribute a knowing mind, personal will, measurable physical energy, or physical extension to the ground. In particular, “(consciousness)” at SB p. 48 names *satta*, while active sentience belongs to *jeevan*. *Gyan* likewise names both the actionless ground (MVD p. 35) and knowledge as understood content. The study retains these contextual uses: *jeevan* knows coexistence, itself, and humane conduct; *gyan-avastha* names the human order capable of knowing. The related roles of *gyan*, *vivek*, and *vigyan* follow MVD p. 170 (§1.14). This distinction governs the comparison with Advaita's *chit* (§§5.6, 5.7.1).

### Saturation, manifestness, and imaging formulations

The saturation formulations overlap. SB p. 57 associates being soaked with forcefulness, submerged with activeness, and surrounded with regulation; PS p. 114 associates them with energy-endowment, mutual recognition, and regulation. §1.2 preserves these connections without assigning each description one exclusive consequence. Permeation in units and transparency in mutuality (MVD p. 32) connect with recognising images (PS p. 114); force-endowment in saturation grounds manifestness (SB p. 248). AVD pp. 115–116 explicitly connects the permeation of insentient and sentient nature by state-complete satta with units' force-endowment, activeness, and manifestness.

*Prakash* is defined through imaging at MVD p. 45 and through a heated body at PS p. 119. These contexts do not exhaust its meanings: the account of the pervasive reality also includes the eternal light of realisation (*shashvat prakash*; PS pp. 80, 137). PS p. 119 defines *prakashman* through being imaged on all sides and manifest with the four aspects, and *prakashmayata* through imaging, properties, essential nature, and dharma in orderliness. “Manifestness” explains *prakashmanata* while preserving this light and imaging vocabulary; *prakash*, *prakashman*, and *prakashan* are not interchangeable names for intelligibility.

The constituted unit (*bimb*), its image (*pratibimb*), and imaging (*pratibimban*) are distinguished at PS pp. 121 and 135. SB pp. 248–252 separates imaging from properties' effects; its distinction of imaging and *prakashan* from motion or pressure (p. 252) supplies no optical or information-transfer mechanism. An image provides a basis of recognition, not complete human understanding of all four aspects. “Imaging” translates *pratibimban*; “reflection” translates the inward knowing activity *pratyavartan* (§1.8). Advaita's *pratibimba* has a separate explanatory role (§2.6).

The cloth-in-water illustration in §1.2 explains the three everyday relations; it is this study's illustration, not an attributed source example. Water is bounded material, whereas Omnipresence is formless and saturation is inseparable from every unit. Likewise, “natural” and “excited” describe order-definite activity and its displacement, rather than psychological moods.

### Form, properties, essential nature, and *dharma*

“Properties” (*guna*) and “essential nature” (*svabhav*) can suggest predicates understood in isolation. Here they retain their relational definitions: relative powers and their effects, and the essentiality or usefulness of those effects (MVD p. 47). The bearer and its capacities remain real; manifested effects depend on the actual mutuality. *Dharma* names inseparable innateness and fulfilment. These translations preserve the distinctions among constitution, imaging, effects, usefulness, and understanding.

The reviewed passages connect constitution with activity and conduct without quantitatively deriving every *guna*, *svabhav*, or *dharma* from particle structure alone. The effort–motion–result correspondences overlap (SB pp. 60–62); they do not reassign the four aspects one-to-one. “Evaluation” also has different scopes: animal recognition of essential nature as friendliness or opposition (SB pp. 249–250), and human judgment of values, dharma, and resolution (§§1.3, 1.13.1–1.13.2).

### Value families, faculty pairings, and thirty-value enumeration

This study uses “contact” for *sampark*, occasionally rendered “association” in Gupta's MVD translation, and “relationship” for *sambandh* (pp. 61–62). Contact's particular expectations are voluntary; relationship's expectations are predetermined toward completeness. Both carry duties of justice (MVD pp. 61, 311–312), so voluntary terms do not make justice optional.

The five families named at MVD p. 305 and JV p. 43 receive an explicit thirty-value enumeration at PS pp. 151 and 154: four *jeevan*, six human, nine established, nine expressed, and two object values. MAD reader p. 30 supplies the principal established–expressed pairs; illustrative wording at JV p. 108 does not replace that enumeration. Gupta's “civic values” translates *shishta-mulya*; “expressed values” identifies its nine relationship expressions here and in [*Axiology: Value Theory*](../Axiology-Value-Theory/Axiology-Value-Theory.pdf) §1.3. Other uses of *mulya* need not add members to this enumeration.

Family identifies evaluative role; faculty identifies a value's location in *jeevan* (MVD pp. 328–348; AVD pp. 91–94). The AVD English appendix reverses force/power headings relative to their Hindi orientation. This study follows *bal* as state and *shakti* as motion (KD §3.8, printed p. 84), checking definitions and pairings rather than English headings alone. The inventory cited in §1.13.1 records detailed readings. Object values concern material units; each *jeevan* harmony is borne at the outer faculty of its pair (§1.9).

### Expressive activities and completeness transitions

The attribution of presentation, communication, and expression to every entity follows SB p. 179 and MVD p. 109 (§1.3). Their connection with sentient manifestness follows the account of *jeevan* manifesting and communicating through bodily composition (SB p. 52), its active and manifest forces and powers (p. 63), and humans communicating and expressing capacity, ability, and receptivity during awakening progression (p. 66). This progression retains both general usage and developed human meaning. The fuller definitions at PS pp. 22, 119, and 206 concern awakened human activity: meaningful presentation of actuality, indication of completeness, and expression for all-round resolution (§1.14). These terms overlap: *abhivyakti* includes *prakashan*, *jeevan*'s activities, and bodily activity. They neither attribute human intention to insentient units nor define three successive stages of imaging.

The reviewed definitions do not assign the three terms exclusively to vritti–chitta, chitta–buddhi, and buddhi–atma. MVD p. 328 brings expression, communication, and exposition together under realisation-based ultimate bliss. *Kala* makes qualities evident and expression meaningful, joining usefulness and beauty (PS p. 56); *kala-mulya* is its object-value application.

T1, T2, and T3 are this study's labels for constitutional, activity, and conduct completeness. They connect result–immortality, effort–restfulness, and motion–destination (SB p. 58) with atomic development, realisation, and humane conduct. Individual completeness is distinguished from social continuity (MVD pp. 15–16); conduct completeness does not require prior universal awakening. All ten *jeevan* activities remain inherent; “four and a half” describes their restricted effective organisation in body-centred human living (JV pp. 73, 93–94). The three sensory perspectives and three humane perspectives explain the scope of restricted deliberation (MVD p. 67); their count is not a quantitative measurement of cognitive capacity. Bodily capacity separately limits what a joint form can express, especially in the animal order (§1.8).

### Advaita Vedanta sources, terminology, and textual conventions

The principal basis is the Upanishads with Shankara's authenticated commentaries, the *Brahma Sutra Bhashya*, and the *Bhagavad Gita Bhashya*. The *Vivekachudamani*, traditionally attributed to Shankara but of uncertain authorship, and the *Drig-Drishya-Viveka*, attributed variously to Shankara or Bharati Tirtha, serve pedagogical explanation rather than settling historical disputes. Gaudapada's non-origination doctrine is cited from the *Mandukya Karika* with Shankara's commentary.

Shankara's *Adhyasa Bhashya* grounds the superimposition account; *anirvacaniya-khyati* names a later systematic error theory (§2.5). MD's *bhram* concerns a real *jeevan* misidentifying a real body as itself; its animal-order *adhyasa* means gestational impression (§5.7.5). Likewise, MD's three truths concern the unit's aspects, its position in mutuality, and its saturation in Omnipresence (SB pp. 250–251; MVD pp. 88, 97; YAT). They are complementary, rather than Advaita's tiers of sublation or a *yatharthata / vastavikta / satyata* ladder.

*Mithya* names dependent ontological status; tiers of validity distinguish what sublation corrects. Shankara already calls name-form indeterminable as identical with or different from Brahman (BS 1.1.5), and as real or unreal (BS 2.1.14). Later formulations of *mithyatva*, error, concealment/projection, and apparent transformation are distinguished from those bases. No verified first occurrence of *sad-asad-anirvacaniya* is claimed, nor is the complete later theory assigned to Shankara. Nagraj's origination objection remains distinct from the later debate over ignorance's locus (§6.3).

The *ananda* comparison (§§2.2, 5.6) rests on TU 2.1's *satyam–jnanam–anantam* and TU 2.7's identification of Brahman as *rasa*, fullness through whose attainment one becomes blissful. TU 2.5's bliss sheath remains an object of discrimination. Thus *ananda* indicates limitless completeness, rather than a passing feeling or the sheath alone.

The cited Gambhirananda Gita edition counts Arjuna's opening question as 13.1; its field-and-knower verses 13.2–13.3 correspond to 13.1–13.2 in editions omitting that question from the count. References distinguish these conventions and, where applicable, printed pages from PDF positions.

- **§1 (introduction).** Existence as coexistence of Omnipresence (*satta*) and units of nature, insentient (*jada*) and sentient (*chaitanya*): SB pp. 48–49; MVD pp. 11, 34. Continuous seeking of happiness; four stages of happiness: MVD p. 163. Societal telos (fearlessness, prosperity, trust, undivided society): MVD p. 160 ff.; JV p. 61; §§1.15–1.16 references below.
- **§1.1.** Two-pole structure: "What is evident is that consciousness and matter are inseparably present. Upon examining their fundamental nature, we learn that all of existence is essentially nature (matter) saturated in Omnipotence (consciousness). Here, 'seeing' is intended in the sense of understanding. Since nature saturated in Omnipotence is inseparably present, existence itself is eternally manifest in the form of coexistence." (SB, p. 48). Omnipresence as actionless energy (*kriya-shunya urja*), ground of energising, regulation, conservation: SB pp. 48–49. English names for *satta* (Omnipotence, Uniform Energy, Space / *shunya*, Knowledge / *gyan*, Consciousness, Absolute Energy): SB p. 48. Units bounded from six directions; form, property, essential nature, and *dharma* as inseparable aspects: SB p. 48; MVD pp. 11, 34. Source explanations of the names for *satta*: MVD p. 35. *Jyoti* among the names for Brahm alongside uniform satta, and its definition through experience in the pervasive as eternal light: PS pp. 80, 137. Atom as root of physical, chemical, and *jeevan* activity; nucleus and orbits as root quantity: KD 3.1; MVD pp. 42, 78, 149. Separation "never happens": SB p. 70; JV p. 18. No absent place: SB pp. 48, 62, 69. Inter-unit distance held in *satta* as Space: SB pp. 57, 59, 79. State-complete (*sthitipurn*) / state-dynamic (*sthitishil*): SB pp. 50, 68–69. Transparence, permeability, and pervasiveness as what evidences *satta* as one indivisible reality: "The Omnipotence eternally present as omnipresence is an indivisible entity, because its reality is apparent as transparence, permeability and omnipresence." (SB, p. 46); restated as omnipresent, eternal, ever-existent, transparent, and permeating: SB p. 160. Permeating in insentient and sentient units and transparent in mutuality: MVD p. 32; the connection with images and recognition: PS p. 114.
- **§1.2.** Saturated (*samprikt*) — soaked, submerged, surrounded: SB pp. 48–49. Permeability as the ground of saturation, and the source's connection to non-annihilation: "Being soaked itself is forcefulness. It becomes clear that units are soaked because Omnipotence is permeating. No matter how many times we divide a unit, each fragment remains submerged, surrounded, and soaked. It also becomes clear that a unit cannot be annihilated." (SB, p. 57). The three aspects taken together, with permeativeness inferred from soakedness: SB p. 243. Surroundedness as what establishes regulation: "This very surroundedness itself clarifies the regulation of every unit, because every unit … remains regulated within a definite distance from one another, which itself is formless existence." (SB, p. 79). Endowment chain (soaked → energy → force / magnetic force → recognition → orderliness): SB pp. 57, 61–62; KD 3.1. Recognition endowment: "Nature is recognisable in the state-complete Omnipotence with its countless unitness. Consequently, a provision for one unit to recognise another got manifest in existence." (SB, p. 50); complementarity given in the bond: SB pp. 49–50, 53; bounded plurality provisions recognition: SB pp. 50, 57, 62; capacity (*kshamata*), ability (*yogyata*), receptivity (*patrata*): SB pp. 57, 62, 79; MVD p. 62; "transparent in mutuality": MVD p. 32. Activeness endowment (forcefulness as force-side name of the same bond): "Every unit in its atomic state is active as orderliness, because it has inherent energy due to being saturated in Omnipotence." (SB, p. 69); "Unit + Energy fullness = Activeness." (SB, p. 69) — the Hindi term is *ikaitva*, unit-ness, which the translation renders as bare "Unit"; "Thus, without basic impulsion, the energy remains unmanifest, and without the energy, there is no basic impulsion." (SB, p. 62); "Without absolute energy, there is no basic impulsion in matter, and without matter, absolute energy is not revealed. Nature is eternally present in absolute energy." (MVD, p. 40); activeness as testimony of permeativeness: MVD p. 32; JV p. 150; forcefulness and basic impulsion endowed: SB p. 57; activeness always with contact in mutuality: SB p. 62; effort–motion–result (*shram–gati–parinam*): "Since nature is eternally active within existence, every activity has inseparable effort, motion and result." (SB, p. 53); also SB pp. 58, 69; MVD p. 40. Regulation endowment: MVD p. 26; SB p. 57. Saturation itself as knowledge: "Saturated (samprikt): – Insentient and sentient nature is submerged, soaked and surrounded in Omnipotence. This itself is coexistence, coexistence itself is eternal, and this itself is knowledge. Law, regulation, balance, justice, dharma and ultimate truth are evident in coexistence only." (MVD, pp. 32–33). Completeness drive grounded in the endowments; conducive environments and naturalness: SB pp. 50–51. Overlapping saturation descriptions: SB p. 57 links soakedness with forcefulness, submersion with activeness, and surroundedness with regulation; PS p. 114 links soakedness with energy-endowment, submersion with mutual recognition, and surroundedness with regulation. Permeation by state-complete satta as the ground of force-endowment, activeness, and manifestness: AVD pp. 115–116; force-endowment in saturation as the root of manifestness: SB p. 248. Image as the basis of mutual recognition: PS p. 121; imaging and influence in insentient mutuality: SB pp. 248–249.

A definite-proportion compound exhibits new conduct rather than merely retaining its constituents' conducts: MVD p. 42. Imaging and light (*prakash*): MVD p. 45; the unit (*bimb*), its image (*pratibimb*), and imaging (*pratibimban*): PS pp. 121, 135. Being manifest with all four aspects: PS p. 119. The radiance-and-image discussion treats imaging as evidence of mutual recognition and distinguishes it from the influences of properties; it extends to animal recognition of essential nature and human exchange of values and evaluation of dharma: SB pp. 248–252. Imaging as indispensable to mutual manifestation and its distinction from motion or pressure: SB pp. 251–252. Essentiality manifest, communicated, and expressed according to state, motion, plane, and order: SB p. 179; all units' expressions as activity saturated in Omnipresence: MVD p. 109. Sentient *jeevan* manifesting and communicating through a body: SB p. 52; its forces and powers as active and manifest: SB p. 63; communication and expression of human capacity, ability, and receptivity during awakening as manifestness: SB p. 66. Atom as root quantity with the four aspects: KD 3.9–3.10. Companion facts: "Each unit is a whole along with its environment." (SB, p. 13); "Each unit is orderliness with its ness and participates in overall orderliness." Domain regulation: "The regulation of human behaviour takes place through justice, discipline in thoughts takes place through dharma, and realisation takes place only through truth." (MVD, p. 137). (MVD, p. 15); also MVD p. 162; KD *vikalp* / Ch. 1 parallel (working English). Hindi perspective terms: *priya–apriya*, *hita–ahita*, *labh–alabh*, *nyaya–anyaya*, *dharma–adharma*, *satya–asatya*.
- **§1.13.3.** Knowledge–law–regulation–balance–justice–dharma–truth: MVD p. 174. Statutory law as institutional codification: MVD p. 311. "Recognising relationships, fulfilling values, evaluating, and achieving mutual satisfaction is justice." (MVD, p. 311). Value, character, and ethics as the account of humane conduct: PS p. 151. Value-flow depends on recognising relationships: JV p. 55. The caregiver illustration is this study's application. Human happiness and relationship-values provide the normative premises: MVD pp. 50–51, 61–62, 67. Family and social assemblies as continuity through relationships: the composition comparison draws on JV pp. 48, 67, 82 and MVD pp. 92–93; social continuity is developed in §1.15.
- **§1.14.** "The goal of development is for nature, saturated in Omnipotence, to be realised in Omnipotence. Realisation itself is the form of awakening. It is only through awakening that there is the orderliness of recognition and fulfilment. Recognition and fulfilment themselves constitute authenticity; this is the expression of realisation." (SB, p. 81). Verity chain — "truth itself is abundance (coexistence), realisation of abundance itself is bliss, bliss itself is jeevan": MVD pp. 14–15. Bliss evidenced in living: JV p. 61. The threefold content of knowledge — holistic view of coexistence, knowledge of *jeevan*, and knowledge of humane conduct — is stated in SB p. 116 and MVD p. 126. KD distinguishes the roles: *jeevan* is knower and seer, existence-as-coexistence is the known and seen, and the threefold content is knowledge (KD 3.17–3.18). The roles become harmonised in awakening without erasing their distinctions. *Gyan* as a name for actionless pervasive reality: MVD p. 35; *vivek* and *vigyan* as recognition of goals and determination of direction for their accomplishment: MVD p. 170. Presentation, communication, and expression in awakened human living: PS pp. 22, 119, 206; their joint realisation-based use: MVD p. 328; realised understanding expressed, communicated, and made manifest in human tradition: AVD pp. 115–116. Form activity, word activity, and communication as understanding and enabling understanding: MVD pp. 109–110. Art as making qualities evident and expression meaningful, joining usefulness and beauty: PS p. 56. Gross compositions, subtle atoms, causal *atma*, and supreme cause *satta*: MVD pp. 288–289.
- **§1.15.** Four human goals — resolution (*samadhan*), prosperity (*samridhi*), fearlessness (*abhay*), coexistence (*saha-astitva*). Alignment with jeevan values (points to §1.9 table): "Resolution = Happiness. We realise happiness wherever we are resolved… Thereafter, we realise peace while evidencing resolution and prosperity in the family. We realise contentment by living orderly in society. We realise bliss in the course of evidencing our understanding." (JV, p. 61). Undivided society (*akhand samaj*), universal orderliness (*sarvabhaum vyavastha*); five dimensions and their functions — education-character (*shiksha-sanskar*), justice-preservation (*nyaya-suraksha*), health-restraint (*swasthya-sanyam*), production-work (*utpadan-karya*), exchange-storage (*vinimay-kosh*): MVD pp. 259–260; humane tradition (*manav parampara*). Individual and social completeness distinguished: MVD pp. 15–16.
- **§1.16.** The evidence distinctions assemble the preceding account of study, realisation, practice, and humane conduct; see §§1.11.2, 1.10, 1.14 references. They distinguish MD's warrant from independent public identification of its proposed sentient unit.
