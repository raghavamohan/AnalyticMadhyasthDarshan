          Why Humans Are Not Just Material

          Author: AnalyticMadhyasthDarshan.org — a group of people studying philosophy. Source repository: raghavamohan/AnalyticMadhyasthDarshan.

          Edited on: September 8, 2026, 7:36 AM IST

          The question: Can a wholly physical account explain conscious experience, individual selfhood, and our ability to understand reasons and values? What grounds Madhyasth Darshan’s proposal that their bearer is ?

          A human being can be well fed and still feel that life lacks meaning. We experience, question, judge, and seek understanding. The question is whether these are fully explained by the organisation of body, brain, and social life, or whether sentience is a further reality that such an account must recognise. This study develops reasons for the latter view while examining the strongest replies to it.

          To say that humans are just material, in the sense examined here, is to say that their experience and thought belong entirely to the organised physical and chemical life of the person. It does not mean merely food, possessions, or concern for bodily comfort. A physicalist can recognise love, reasoning, and moral commitment as real features of an embodied person. The disagreement concerns what makes those features possible and whether a physical account is sufficient to explain them.

          Three representative answers guide the inquiry: Madhyasth Darshan, which affirms body and a real sentient jeevan together; a physicalist interpretation of modern science; and Advaita Vedanta, which identifies the deepest Self with non-dual consciousness. The argument for sentience beyond a wholly physical account is kept distinct from the further claims that its bearer is an individual unit, that it has a particular atomic constitution, and that it persists after death.

          1. The Madhyasth Darshan Answer

          holds that a human is the joint form of body and : an insentient body together with a real sentient self. The body provides the means of sensing and acting; jeevan experiences, evaluates, understands, and directs its participation in the world. Human fulfilment therefore concerns both bodily provision and the understanding through which a person lives in relationships.

          “Existence is not just physicochemical matter, but all physical, chemical and jeevan entities are inseparably present in Omnipresence.” — MVD, The Alternative, point 7, p. 5

          This answer joins an account of lived experience to an account of nature. The experiential distinctions come first below; the claim that a constitutionally complete sentient unit bears them follows in §1.3.

          1.1 A fresh starting point

          Shri A. Nagraj argues that two inherited approaches left the human question unresolved. Matter-centred inquiry explained bodily processes without adequately understanding the person, while mystery-centred spiritual traditions placed decisive knowledge beyond ordinary articulation. This is his criticism of those approaches, rather than a neutral history of science or Indian philosophy. His alternative is to make existence, the sentient self, and humane conduct understandable through study and evident in living (MVD, The Alternative, points 1 and 5, pp. 3–4).

          1.2 The argument from experience

          The starting point is familiar experience: what satisfies us, what we seek, how we experience ourselves, and why justice matters. Madhyasth Darshan reads these together as expressions of jeevan. They establish questions an adequate account of the human must answer; the physicalist interpretation of the same experiences is developed in §2.2.

          1.2.1 Bodily satisfaction and personal fulfilment

          A meal answers hunger, but it does not settle a broken relationship or the knowledge that one has acted unjustly. Conversely, a person may endure discomfort while remaining content because they kept a promise or helped someone. Bodily well-being and personal fulfilment support each other, yet neither simply substitutes for the other. Madhyasth Darshan connects this distinction to the different requirements of body and jeevan:

          “Activities such as eating and sleeping serve the body’s needs, while values and evaluation serve jeevan’s purpose. Mere sustenance of the body does not equate to jeevan’s fulfilment.” — SB, Ch. 7, PDF p. 92

          Its six perspectives () explain the direction of evaluation. Priya concerns sensory pleasure, hita bodily health and nourishment, and labh gain, particularly obtaining more value than one gives. These concerns guide much ordinary activity. When they govern life on their own, acquisition can continue without resolving how to live. Awakening brings them under the guidance of justice (), , and truth () (MVD, pp. 58, 66; JV, pp. 138–139).

          Truth concerns reality as coexistence, including the person’s own place within it. Dharma concerns what is inherent in beings and their participation in order; happiness is the human dharma. Justice joins the recognition of relationships to the fulfilment of their values, evaluation of that fulfilment, and mutual satisfaction. These perspectives direct activity towards fulfilment in a shared world (MVD, pp. 100, 115, 336).

          Pleasure, health, and provision retain their place under this guidance. Prosperity replaces accumulation for its own sake, and production proceeds through the fulfilment of relationships. Exploitation can yield a private gain while harming both the person and the wider world on which that gain depends. The change therefore concerns what counts as fulfilment, as well as the means of attaining it (JV, pp. 138–139; The Ontology of Coexistence §1.12).

          1.2.2 The aspiration for continuous fulfilment

          Food, rest, and sensory pleasure have occasions and limits. The human aspiration, as Madhyasth Darshan understands it, is for happiness that continues: a settled way of living rather than an endless succession of pleasant sensations. This aspiration is often pursued through comfort and accumulation, yet acquiring more does not necessarily resolve the uncertainty about how to live. Nagraj connects that search to jeevan and interprets human production beyond immediate bodily needs as another expression of its capacity (JV, pp. 24–25).

          Art, mathematics, philosophy, and institutions illustrate how human activity extends beyond immediate subsistence. Other animals also produce, learn, and transmit practices; the comparison concerns how these capacities are organised and extended (§2.3.4). Within Madhyasth Darshan, the positive answer to the search for fulfilment is four jeevan values: happiness, peace, contentment, and bliss. These name harmonies among its faculties, progressively joining hope, thought, desire, resolve, and realisation. Fulfilment is therefore a quality of the sentient self’s activity and relationships, rather than a quantity of goods consumed (JV, p. 137; The Ontology of Coexistence §1.8).

          1.2.3 The experienced self and the changing body

          We can attend to bodily pain, ageing, or fatigue while also reflecting on what these changes mean for us. We can distinguish the body we evaluate from ourselves as the one evaluating it. Madhyasth Darshan develops this distinction into its account of jeevan: the sentient self acts through a changing body while retaining its own constitution. Identifying oneself wholly with the body is therefore described as delusion (SB, PDF pp. 91–92).

          The same account underlies the statement that jeevan does not sleep. Sleep belongs to the bodily medium, while the sentient unit continues its activity (SB, PDF p. 92). How this claim relates to experience and recall during sleep is examined in §2.2.3.

          1.2.4 Justice as an inherent human orientation

          Madhyasth Darshan holds that the human being seeks justice, wishes to act correctly, and is oriented towards truth from birth. Appropriate education and relationships develop this orientation into the capacity to give justice and recognise truth (SB, Ch. 1, PDF p. 15). On this account, teaching does more than install social preferences: it helps a person recognise and fulfil what relationships require.

          The claim gives a direction to human development: justice can be understood and fulfilled, rather than reduced to whichever preference happens to prevail. How early social attention develops into a recognised obligation is considered in §2.2.4.

          These four observations support a distinction between bodily maintenance and the wider life of a person. The further step to jeevan depends on a stronger premise: bodily organisation cannot itself be the bearer of experience, aspiration, understanding, and evaluation. That premise needs its own defence. The distinction between functions does not alone establish distinct entities; §§2 and 5 examine the competing explanations.

          1.3 The account of what exists

          Madhyasth Darshan places the sentient self within a real and perpetual nature. Its account connects the orders of nature, development in the atom, the body–jeevan joint form, and the human possibility of awakening.

          1.3.1 The four orders of nature

          Nature is described through four orders: material, biological, animal, and knowledge. Each is distinguished by characteristic activity, essential nature, and dharma. Material constituents compose biological forms, and biological cells compose animal and human bodies. In the animal order, a sentient unit works through a body with the hope to live. In the human or knowledge order, the same kind of unit can understand, evaluate, and responsibly participate in the other orders. Happiness is the human dharma; fortitude, courage, generosity, kindness, grace, and compassion characterise humane conduct (SB, PDF pp. 55, 179–180; MVD, p. 115).

          Two developments must remain distinct. Compositional progression provides bodies and their environments. Development in an insentient atom reaches the sentient status called jeevan. Increasing bodily complexity is therefore not itself the production of sentience in this account (The Ontology of Coexistence §§1.5–1.6).

          Material, biological, and animal nature already exhibit definite forms of orderliness. Human awakening makes possible an understood participation in that order through relationships, production, and the nurture and right use of nature (MVD, p. 27; SB, PDF pp. 236–237).

          1.3.2 The transition to sentient jeevan

          Constitutional completeness () means that an evolving atom has incorporated the particles required for its constitution and no longer gains or loses them. The texts describe an irreversible transition: the atom becomes free from molecular-bondage and weight-bondage and enters hope-bondage. Its constitution remains stable while its sentient activities can develop from delusion towards awakening (MVD, p. 91; SB, PDF pp. 55, 92).

          “Jeevan is neither physical nor non-physical. It is a constitutionally complete atom and a sentient entity.” — JV, p. 36

          This transition belongs to the darshan’s account of development within nature. The question of how constitutional completeness could be independently established is considered in §5.1.

          The continuing identity of jeevan after bodily death follows within Madhyasth Darshan from this closed constitution. Bodies form and decompose; the sentient unit persists and can work through another body. Conservation of quantity alone would not establish that survival: the additional claim is that this particular constitution cannot disintegrate (JV, pp. 19, 54; The Ontology of Coexistence §§1.14, 6.2.2).

          1.3.3 Body and self are not the same

          The joint form gives the body and jeevan different but connected roles. The body supplies sensory organs, the brain, movement, and the bodily conditions of expression. Jeevan is the sentient participant: it experiences, selects, evaluates, and understands through this medium. Sensation belongs to their joint activity: a body constructed without jeevan would not evidence sensitivity, let alone understanding (JV, pp. 36–37).

          Sensitivity concerns experience and response through the body and senses; comprehension concerns understanding relationships, values, and order. Both involve jeevan. Awakening brings them into balance by guiding sensory activity through understanding (SB, PDF p. 64). Bodily impairment constrains expression through the medium; delusion concerns how the already-present sentient self identifies and directs itself (The Ontology of Coexistence §§1.7, 6.2.2).

          1.3.4 Knowledge in the human order

          The human body provides the developed nervous system through which jeevan can understand existence and make that understanding evident. The unfolding of knowledge () occurs through the awakened human joint form. The knower is jeevan; the body supplies its means of expression (MVD, pp. 93, 115–116).

          Realisation (anubhav) is the knower’s realised understanding of coexistence. It informs understanding and resolve, which guide thought, choice, and the recognition and fulfilment of relationships. The person can examine what they seek in the light of what they understand. Knowledge becomes evident through this connection with conduct, work, and communication (MVD, pp. 97–100).

          Realisation is the goal of development in this account. Constitutional completeness establishes the sentient unit; awakening develops the understanding and conduct of a jeevan already present. The human body, education, and relationships provide the means through which this awakening can become evident in living (MVD, pp. 27, 115; SB, PDF p. 81; JV, pp. 138–139).

          The brain (medhas) is described as receiving and processing signals associated with jeevan’s hopes, thoughts, desires, resolves, and authenticity, with bodily processes responding to them (MVD, pp. 83–84). This gives the brain a substantial role in the source account. The unresolved question is what measurable relation connects this description to neural activity, including the effects of injury and stimulation (§§2.3.3, 5.1; The Epistemology of Coexistence §7.3).

          1.3.5 The wisdom layer

          Madhyasth Darshan also uses kosha vocabulary for the capacities expressed across nature’s orders. In awakened humans, vigyanmaya names the opening of knowledge, wisdom, and science, through which the causes of unhappiness and their resolution become understandable (MVD, pp. 49–50). These are capacities expressed through the developing joint form. The five enduring faculties within jeevan — , , , , and atma — belong to a different part of the account. Advaita’s five sheaths also serve a different purpose (§3.2.4; The Ontology of Coexistence §1.7).

          1.4 The proposed test

          Understanding is fulfilled in living. Madhyasth Darshan asks a person to study existence, jeevan, and humane conduct; examine the proposal in their own experience; and make the understanding evident in relationships, work, and participation. Its evidence chain is Realisation – Behaviour – Experiment. Realisation must become resolution, work, conduct, and an awakened human tradition (MVD, p. 12).

          Authenticity () includes the ability to convey that understanding to another. Nagraj connects confidence in his understanding with its examination in living, fulfilment of responsibilities, and transmission to others (JV, pp. 25–27). This makes conduct and teaching integral to the proposal’s test. Whether these outcomes also establish its particular account of the sentient atom is a separate question addressed in §5.4.

          1.5 What makes this answer distinctive

          Madhyasth Darshan affirms a real world in which many enduring sentient selves participate through bodies. Its account joins individuality to relationship, and understanding to responsible conduct. Human fulfilment comes through awakening in this world. Its distinctive claim is that the sentient participant is a constitutionally complete unit, irreducible to the organisation of its bodily medium.

          2. Scientific Evidence and the Physicalist Answer

          Modern science studies human beings as evolved organisms whose mental lives depend on brains, bodies, relationships, and culture. Physicalism interprets this dependence as evidence that the organised person is sufficient to bear experience and thought. This need not mean that a useful account of love or justice can be replaced by a description of particles. The stronger claim is that these capacities require no sentient entity beyond the embodied person. Scientific findings constrain this interpretation without making it the only possible one.

          2.1 What science establishes — and what it does not

          Changes to the brain can alter particular experiences and motivations. The evidence includes interventions, such as stimulation, as well as observations of injury and development. A theory of the human must explain these detailed relationships, including why some capacities change while others remain. Establishing dependence, however, differs from establishing that experience is identical with neural activity.

          What a person can express also differs from what they can experience. Bodien et al. (2024) studied 353 adults with severe brain injuries. Among 241 who showed no observable command-following, 60 followed instructions through patterns detected by brain imaging or electrical recordings. Their cognition was discovered through brain activity despite its lack of outward expression. The sample does not establish how common this is in every clinical population, but it rules out treating absent movement as sufficient evidence of absent experience. Conversely, it supplies no evidence of cognition without a functioning brain.

          2.2 The physicalist reading of experience

          Physicalists can accept the experiences described in §1.2 while disputing what follows from them. The issue is whether differences within experience require different kinds of entity, or whether one embodied person can have bodily needs, lasting commitments, and awareness of both (Dennett 1991; Churchland 1986).

          2.2.1 Different fulfilments remain embodied

          A physicalist account need not identify flourishing with bodily maintenance. Relationships, social learning, and reflection develop forms of fulfilment that cannot be replaced by food or comfort. Their difference from hunger does not place them beyond bodily organisation.

          Neural injury makes this question concrete. Lockwood et al. (2024) compared 25 people with damage to the ventromedial prefrontal cortex, a region involved in valuation, with groups who had other brain injuries or no injury. The affected group were less willing to exert effort for another person’s benefit, while effort for their own benefit was relatively preserved. This was a selective difference in motivation, not simply an inability to move. The task measures choices and effort; it does not directly establish that inner concern for others has disappeared. Nor does it measure all morality or disprove a acting through an impaired medium. It does require that account to explain why a particular injury affects the willingness to help (§1.3.3).

          2.2.2 Unlimited wanting has naturalistic explanations

          Recurring desire need not imply an unlimited entity. Organisms must keep meeting changing needs, while learning and culture create goals that extend far beyond immediate survival. A person can also seek a settled life while continuing to need food and action within it. Continuous aspiration and endless acquisition should therefore be distinguished.

          Active inference offers one candidate explanation of ongoing pursuit. It models choices through both preferred outcomes and opportunities to learn about the world (Friston et al. 2015). Regulation can also anticipate future needs, so activity continues even when no immediate deficit is felt. This helps explain why achieving one goal need not end activity. It does not establish that the aspiration for continuous happiness is merely biological regulation, or supply a complete theory of fulfilment. It gives the physicalist a possible account to develop alongside ’s interpretation (§1.2.2).

          2.2.3 The felt self can be modelled as embodied

          The sense of owning a body, being located somewhere, remembering one’s past, and remaining the same person are related but distinguishable. Lyu et al. (2023) found that stimulation of particular sites in the anterior precuneus, toward the back of the brain, changed bodily and spatial self-experience in nine patients. The felt relationship between self and body is therefore itself sensitive to neural intervention. This concerns particular aspects of selfhood, not the complete explanation of a subject.

          Dreaming also occurs in an active brain. The DREAM database brings together 2,643 awakenings from 505 participants and relates reports of experience to preceding electrical brain activity (Wong et al. 2025). Dreams occur in both rapid-eye-movement and non-REM sleep. The research distinguishes reports of experience, experience whose contents cannot be recalled, and no experience; even the last may reflect failed recall. Dreaming during bodily immobility does not establish independence from neural activity. The claim that jeevan does not sleep must consequently distinguish continuous experience from a continuing capacity or bearer (§1.2.3).

          2.2.4 Early social attention and moral development

          Human social responsiveness begins early, but its interpretation needs care. Geraci et al. (2025) found that newborns around five days old looked longer at helpful or approaching interactions than at hindering or avoiding ones, with controls for non-social movement. This supports early sensitivity to social interactions. It does not show that newborns understand justice or already possess a moral doctrine.

          The evidence also varies across tasks. A coordinated study across 37 laboratories did not reproduce the expected preference for helpers in its tested sample of older infants (Lucca et al. 2025). Different ages, displays, and responses mean that this is not a direct refutation of the newborn finding. Together, the studies require distinctions between attention, preference, fairness expectations, and explicit obligation. Naturalistic accounts can allow early capacities that develop through experience. Their early appearance alone does not identify a separate bearer (§1.2.4; Ethics-And-Morals-In-Human-Beings).

          2.3 The physicalist account of what exists

          Physicalism reads these findings as evidence for capacities belonging to an organised living being. Its replies to §1.3 concern how those capacities arise, why they vary, and what explanatory work a further entity would perform.

          2.3.1 Evolution and the four orders

          Evolution describes branching relationships and changes in organisation rather than four separate ontological orders. This does not prevent meaningful distinctions between living and non-living systems or between different cognitive abilities. It asks how such distinctions arise and what evidence supports their boundaries. The existence of animals with complex capacities is already compatible with Madhyasth Darshan’s attribution of jeevan to animals. The disputed step is its separate atomic development into sentience, for which the study offers no independently identified physical transition (§1.3.2).

          2.3.2 Mind emerges from organisation

          Physicalists take the organised brain and body, rather than their constituent matter considered separately, to realise mental capacities. Matter remains after the irreversible destruction of brain organisation, while no continuing personal capacities are publicly established. The physicalist concludes that the person does not survive. This is an interpretation of the dependence evidence, not an observation of a subject’s disappearance. Reversible loss of circulation or behavioural response must also be distinguished from irreversible brain destruction when assessing claims about death (§5.1).

          2.3.3 Parsimony and the interaction challenge

          Calling the brain a medium accommodates dependence without explaining its particular form. A damaged instrument could obstruct expression, but the account must explain selective changes in motivation and self-experience, not only impaired movement. Such findings do not logically refute the medium proposal. They favour neural explanations insofar as those explanations predict the pattern while the added entity supplies no further prediction. Physicalists therefore ask what jeevan explains that the embodied account cannot.

          There is also a causal question. If every physical event has a sufficient physical cause, how does a distinct jeevan contribute to an action? Kim’s exclusion argument presses the alternatives of identifying the mental cause with the physical process, accepting two sufficient causes, or denying the additional cause any physical effect (Kim 2005). Madhyasth Darshan could challenge that starting assumption, but would then need to explain the relation it proposes. Conservation of energy alone does not establish physical causal closure, and a non-reductive account need not introduce extra energy. The unresolved issue is what the joint-form relation changes and how that change could be assessed (§1.3.4; The Epistemology of Coexistence §7.3).

          2.3.4 Cognition, culture, and the brain

          Language and cumulative culture allow people to inherit achievements they could not recreate alone (Tomasello 2014). Some ingredients have wider roots: bumblebees learned a two-step puzzle from trained demonstrators that control bees did not independently solve under the tested conditions (Bridges et al. 2024). This demonstrates a form of social learning, not human reflective knowledge in an insect. It encourages precise comparisons rather than an absolute opposition between animal instinct and human understanding. A physicalist interprets human distinctiveness through developed capacities and cultural history; Madhyasth Darshan must show why those resources leave the knowledge order’s activity unexplained (§1.3.4).

          2.3.5 Layered function, not koshas

          Neuroscience distinguishes interacting sensory, emotional, and cognitive functions without assigning them to koshas or a separate wisdom-bearing unit (Kandel et al. 2021). Similarities with the developmental envelopes described in §1.3.5 may help comparison, but do not establish a shared anatomy. Nor does identifying neural conditions of understanding decide whether its content is true or its use wise.

          2.4 Publicly controlled evidence

          First-person reports are part of scientific inquiry: dream research could not identify its target without them. Public checking adds comparisons, independent analysis, and procedures that can expose mistaken interpretations. Specifying an expected result before collecting data helps prevent every outcome from being counted as support.

          The 2025 COGITATE collaboration used this approach to test competing predictions of integrated information theory and global neuronal workspace theory in 256 participants. Some predictions gained support and others were challenged, including predictions about sustained communication between regions at the back of the brain and responses when a stimulus disappeared. These results leave substantial questions open without showing that neural explanation as a whole has failed (Cogitate Consortium 2025). The same discipline can strengthen §1.4’s proposed verification: an account must allow outcomes that would count against its interpretation, even when its practices remain valuable.

          2.5 What science leaves open

          Explaining when experience occurs and what changes it leaves the disputed question of why those processes are experienced at all. Philosophers disagree over whether this hard problem requires a different account of what exists or a better understanding of how we know and describe experience (Chalmers 1995; Dennett 1991). The arguments are examined in §4.

          The alternatives also extend beyond the three positions compared here. Non-reductive physicalism allows psychological explanations their own importance while affirming physical constitution. Embodied and enactive approaches study cognition through a living being’s engagement with its surroundings; they need not reject physicalism. Russellian monism asks whether experience reveals aspects of reality left unspecified by physics’ account of structure and relations. Panpsychist versions place basic experiential properties more widely in nature (Strawson 2006; Goff 2019). None thereby establishes Madhyasth Darshan’s individual immortal unit. These alternatives show why an objection to one form of reduction does not settle the choice of ontology. Every proposal still owes an account of experience consistent with the detailed evidence about its bodily conditions.

          3. The Advaita Vedanta Answer

          Advaita Vedanta holds that consciousness is the deepest reality of the human being. The true Self () is identical with , the one reality on which the world depends. Body, thought, desire, and the sense of being a separate person all belong to experience, but none is the final Self. The human is therefore not merely material; at the deepest level, the apparent division between a conscious person and an independent material world gives way to non-duality.

          3.1 A different starting point

          Advaita begins with awareness as the condition of every experience. We can question what we perceive, remember, or believe, but those questions themselves occur in awareness. The inquiry turns from the changing contents of experience towards that through which they are known. This is witness-consciousness: the awareness in which bodily sensations, thoughts, and the feeling of being an individual appear (DDV, vv. 1–5).

          The witness is not another thinker hidden behind the ordinary mind. Thinking, choosing, and wanting are themselves known. Advaita therefore takes the inquiry beyond the active inner self that affirms as . The distinction matters: the two traditions resist a body-only account, but they do not mean the same thing by the Self.

          3.2 The argument from experience

          Advaita joins the Upanishadic neti neti (“not this, not this”) with the distinction between the seer and the seen. Whatever appears as an object of awareness can be examined and distinguished from awareness itself. The inquiry proceeds through body, sensation, thought, and ego, rather than stopping at the familiar feeling of “I” (BU 2.3.6; DDV, vv. 1–5).

          3.2.1 Two kinds of fulfilment are witnessed, not the Self

          The relief of hunger and the contentment of acting well differ in experience. Advaita accepts the distinction, while observing that both are known: bodily relief arises and passes, and so does the satisfaction following a just action. Neither changing state exhausts the awareness through which it is experienced. The inquiry therefore continues beyond both bodily satisfaction and the felt fulfilment of a person (DDV, vv. 1–5).

          3.2.2 Unlimited wanting is witnessed; the witness does not want

          Desire can return after a bodily need has been met. Advaita places this wanting, together with planning and the search for lasting happiness, within the changing life of the mind. Awareness knows the presence of desire and its absence. It is not itself a desire that must be satisfied. The distinction directs the seeker towards freedom from mistaken identification with the movements of mind (DDV, vv. 1–5).

          3.2.3 Awareness across waking, dreaming, and deep sleep

          The Mandukya Upanishad examines waking, dreaming, and deep sleep. Waking experience concerns the outward world; dreaming presents an inwardly experienced world; deep sleep is described through prajna, in which differentiated objects are not cognised and ignorance remains. Advaita reads these changing conditions in relation to a Self that none of them exhausts (MU, vv. 3–7).

          The waking judgement that one slept peacefully forms part of this account of continuity. It needs to be distinguished from a detailed memory of what occurred during sleep. The further interpretation of such reports is considered alongside sleep research in §2.2.3 and the distinction between experienced unity and personal continuity in §4.3. Turiya names the non-dual Self that is not exhausted by waking, dreaming, or deep sleep. It is not a fourth blank interval added after the other three.

          3.2.4 Moral preparation and the five sheaths

          Truthfulness, self-control, and responsible conduct prepare the seeker for knowledge. They have force within everyday life, called . Advaita does not dismiss them because they are not the final reality. Its inquiry asks the seeker to distinguish the Self from what changes, while living with the discipline needed for that inquiry (TU 1.11; VC, vv. 16–30).

          The Taittiriya Upanishad moves inward through food-body, vitality, mind, intellect, and bliss. Advaita develops these as the five sheaths: annamaya, pranamaya, manomaya, vijnanamaya, and anandamaya. Each is examined in the search for the Self (TU 2.1–2.5; VC, vv. 154–212). These sheaths differ from both Madhyasth Darshan’s developmental envelopes and its faculties within jeevan (§1.3.5).

          3.3 The account of what exists

          Advaita connects this inquiry into experience with its account of reality. Witness-consciousness is understood as Atman, and Atman as Brahman. The connection rests on the wider reasoning of the tradition and the Upanishads, not on the seer–seen distinction taken alone.

          3.3.1 Brahman alone is real; the world is appearance

          Advaita holds that Brahman alone is ultimately real, while the world is : dependent for its reality on Brahman, with its apparent independence overcome through knowledge. The world is neither wholly independent nor sheer non-being. Bodies, relationships, inquiry, causation, and conduct remain meaningful within ordinary experience. What is questioned is their apparent independence from Brahman (VC, v. 20; BSB, Adhyasa Bhashya).

          This distinction between everyday and ultimate reality prevents mithya from being reduced to “nothing exists” or “nothing matters.” Advaita’s claim concerns what the world ultimately is, even while people act, learn, and bear responsibility within it.

          3.3.2 You are not the body — or the mind

          Body, senses, thought, and the feeling of being “me” can each become objects of awareness. Advaita reasons that none can therefore be the final seer. It identifies that seer as the Self that was never born and does not die (DDV, vv. 1–5; KU 1.2.18; BG 2.20). The conclusion reaches beyond ordinary personal identity: the changing mind and its history are also distinguished from Atman. Whether the seer–seen distinction warrants these conclusions is examined in §5.3.

          3.3.3 The self is not individual; the question dissolves

          The apparent separate person (jiva) arises through ignorance (avidya): self and not-self are confused, and the properties of one are attributed to the other. Advaita calls this superimposition (adhyasa). Liberation (moksha) is the removal of this mistake through knowledge. The Self does not become Brahman; it is recognised as having always been Brahman, one without a second (BSB, Adhyasa Bhashya; CU 6.2.1, 6.8.7).

          From this standpoint, the question “Is the human just material?” contains an assumption that must itself be examined: that a separate human stands within an independently existing material world. Advaita holds that the distinction is valid in ordinary life but does not describe ultimate reality. Both the person and the world depend on Brahman.

          3.4 Scripture, reason, and meditation

          The classical path joins hearing the teaching (shravana), reasoning over it (manana), and sustained contemplation (nididhyasana), under a qualified teacher (BU 2.4.5). Reason is used to examine doubts and apparent contradictions; contemplation addresses habitual identification with body and mind. Scripture is treated as a means of knowing the Self where ordinary perception cannot make it an object.

          This is a substantive claim about how knowledge is possible. Advaita asks the seeker to understand and realise the teaching, while granting the Upanishads an authority that a physicalist or a student of Madhyasth Darshan need not accept. The comparative question is how the method distinguishes genuine understanding from conviction shaped by its starting assumptions (§5.3).

          3.5 What makes this answer distinctive

          Advaita locates the final Self in unchanging awareness, beyond the active individual mind. Madhyasth Darshan gives lasting reality to individual jeevan units and to the world in which they live. Advaita gives persons and relationships their place within everyday reality, while denying that they remain ultimately separate. Their shared resistance to a body-only account therefore leads to different understandings of individuality, knowledge, and liberation.

          4. Comparing the Three Views

          The three positions differ over what experience reveals, what a human being is, and how those claims can be assessed. Scientific findings constrain every account of embodied life. Physicalism draws a further conclusion about what those findings mean for the nature of mind.

          4.1 The central differences

          For , both the world and individual sentient selves are fully real. This allows relationships, understanding, and responsible conduct to retain their place in the account of existence. Advaita gives them their place within everyday reality but holds that their apparent separateness is overcome in knowledge of . Physicalism treats the embodied person as sufficient for experience and action, while asking how capacities develop through biological and social life.

          Madhyasth Darshan’s position between these two answers is not the origin of its name. Madhyasth refers to the mediative or regulative role through which Omnipresence and atomic nuclei sustain order (The Ontology of Coexistence §1.11). Its affirmation of a perpetual world and many distinct units must be understood through that account, rather than as a compromise between materialism and Advaita.

          The traditions also differ over the reach of their methods. First-person examination can reveal distinctions within experience. Public experiments can identify conditions on which reported experience and action depend. Reasoning can examine whether a proposed explanation follows from its premises. Conduct can show whether an understanding is lived and conveyed. The question is how these kinds of evidence support one another without allowing success in one domain to settle every claim in another.

          Further comparisons, including Buddhist accounts that deny a permanent self, help separate rejection of material reduction from affirmation of an immortal individual. The wider range is considered in The Ontology of Coexistence §5. The following questions bring the central disagreement into focus.

          4.2 What a physical description tells us about experience

          The strongest reason for questioning a wholly material account concerns the presence of experience itself. A description of pain can explain the detection of injury, the transmission of signals, withdrawal, learning, and a person’s report. The further question is why any of these processes should hurt. Explaining their contribution to survival does not yet explain their felt character. Madhyasth Darshan locates that character in a sentient knower; Advaita starts from awareness; the physicalist identifies it with an activity of the organism.

          The knowledge argument makes the disagreement vivid. Imagine someone who knows every physical fact about colour vision but has never experienced colour. On seeing red, would this person learn something about what red looks like? If so, and if what is learned is a fact absent from complete physical knowledge, physical facts would not exhaust the facts about experience. The argument’s force lies in the difference between describing experience and knowing it through undergoing it (Chalmers 2003).

          The physicalist disputes the inference. Someone can acquire a new way of recognising or thinking about a state without discovering a second kind of reality. First-person acquaintance and a scientific description may concern the same state, just as different descriptions can identify the same person. The analogy illustrates the reply without establishing the proposed identity between experience and brain activity. Papineau develops this reply through the distinction between concepts acquired through experience and concepts used in physical explanation (Papineau 2020). The question is whether the new understanding reveals something additional in reality or a new way of knowing what was already described.

          This study gives weight to the first possibility. An explanation should account for the experienced character of pain as well as the work pain does. That is a reason to question the sufficiency of a description confined to structure and function. To establish that physicalism is false, the argument must show why the proposed identity cannot account for this difference; the present explanatory difficulty alone does not establish that failure.

          4.3 Why experience belongs together

          Experience also has a unity that demands explanation. When someone listens to a friend, the voice, the expression on the friend’s face, and the listener’s concern can occur together within one experience. Explaining how information is coordinated for action addresses part of this phenomenon. It remains possible to ask why the coordinated processes are experienced together by a subject.

          Bayne and Chalmers distinguish this experienced unity from the ability to combine information in thought or report. Their argument challenges theories that explain separate conscious states without explaining their belonging to a common experience (Bayne and Chalmers 2003). Madhyasth Darshan’s single jeevan offers a definite bearer of that unity. This is an attraction of the proposal: it starts with a sentient individual rather than attempting to assemble one subject out of many already separate subjects.

          A physicalist can nevertheless hold that a unified experience is realised by coordinated activity distributed through an organism. Unity in experience does not by itself require a spatial point at which everything converges. Nor does it establish an indivisible atom or an enduring identity after death. The competing accounts must explain the kinds of unity that remain, and the kinds that change, when neural connections are altered (§2.3.3). Madhyasth Darshan’s proposal still needs to explain how one jeevan receives and experiences the particular contents made available through that changing organisation.

          Studies of people whose major connection between the brain’s hemispheres has been severed complicate a simple answer. Some information becomes difficult to compare across the two sides, yet findings from a two-patient study and a later one-patient study have been interpreted as preserving aspects of conscious unity (Pinto et al. 2017, 2023). These small samples and particular tasks do not settle how many subjects there are. They show why neither “two disconnected hemispheres mean two people” nor “one person proves an indivisible atom” follows directly from the evidence. The relation between neural coordination and experienced unity remains a question for both accounts.

          4.4 Reasons, values, and the life of a knower

          A complete account of the human must also explain understanding. A belief concerns something: a friend’s promise, yesterday’s event, or a mathematical relation. We assess it for truth and ask whether the reasons offered justify it. Similarly, a judgment about justice can be criticised even when it is popular or useful. Madhyasth Darshan’s account of the knowledge order gives these activities a central place in human life. Its attraction is the connection it draws between knowing, evaluating, and fulfilling relationships.

          The importance of that connection becomes clear when private gain conflicts with the conditions of shared fulfilment. Consider a business that increases its returns while depleting the water on which its workers, neighbours, and future activity depend. Its immediate gain conceals a failure in its relationships. Becoming more efficient at pursuing an aim does not settle whether the aim, or the measure of success, is reasonable. A wider understanding can change what the person considers worth pursuing.

          Madhyasth Darshan gives this change a definite place in its account of awakening. Pleasure, health, and gain come under the guidance of justice, , and truth. Realisation of coexistence informs the knower’s understanding, resolve, and conduct (§§1.2.1, 1.3.4). Jeevan is the participant who can examine its aims, recognise what relationships require, and guide its bodily and material capacities accordingly. Fulfilment concerns the quality of participation in a shared world, beyond the quantity acquired by one participant (JV, pp. 138–139; MVD, pp. 97–100).

          This argument concerns humane and lasting fulfilment. A society’s persistence alone does not establish its justice: an unjust arrangement may endure, and someone with extensive knowledge of a system may still exploit it. The role of is therefore essential. Understanding wider relationships must become their recognition and fulfilment, with mutual satisfaction as part of its assessment. A larger total output does not by itself establish that those relationships have been fulfilled (MVD, p. 336).

          Research on shared resources supports the practical importance of attending to such relationships. Ostrom reports experiments in which people withdrew too much from a common resource under isolated, anonymous conditions. Communication and rules developed by participants improved joint returns. Her wider account shows how cooperating institutions can sustain shared resources without one authority possessing complete knowledge of the whole (Ostrom 2010). This supports the need to consider wider consequences, while challenging the claim that local activity must inevitably cause collapse.

          Local information also differs from a purely private aim. In harvester ants, brief contacts with returning foragers help regulate the rate at which others leave the nest. Field experiments and a model show how this feedback can coordinate colony foraging without each ant knowing where food is distributed (Prabhakar et al. 2012). Such coordination does not establish a reflective understanding of coexistence. It does show why successful collective activity cannot by itself prove that an individual must comprehend the whole. Madhyasth Darshan already attributes jeevan to animals; this example concerns the need for an overall perspective, not the presence or absence of sentience.

          The physicalist can likewise accept the importance of wider understanding. An embodied person can learn about distant effects, communicate, and reconsider goals. Understanding relations beyond one’s immediate surroundings does not alone establish that the knower lies beyond physical processes. A process can have a physical explanation while also expressing a reason. Naturalistic theories of meaning seek to explain how representations concern things by examining their use in perception, action, learning, and communication. Papineau, for example, connects representational content with the biological functions of the processes that use it (Papineau 2016). Such accounts have to explain both successful understanding and mistakes; a mere sequence of physical causes does not tell us which belief is justified.

          Evolutionary usefulness likewise gives no automatic guarantee of truth. Accurate beliefs often help organisms act successfully, while other beliefs may persist because they secure approval or protect group identity. Sterelny’s recent reconsideration of moral realism stresses this difficulty: his earlier defence had overestimated the extent to which the evolution of cooperation supported reliable moral judgment (Sterelny 2025). This leaves room for a serious challenge to explaining moral authority through social usefulness alone. It does not show that physically embodied minds cannot recognise valid reasons, or that only jeevan could do so. Madhyasth Darshan must explain how its account supports reliable understanding and how errors can be recognised and corrected.

          The need for understanding therefore gives substance to the account of the knower. Within Madhyasth Darshan it explains the importance of awakening in a jeevan already present. The further claim that the need for a wider perspective caused jeevan to become sentient does not follow from that need alone. Its account of development towards realisation (§1.3.4) still requires grounds for the transition itself. The affirmative case joins the experience of understanding to the capacity to examine one’s aims and recognise reasons for acting; these remain questions for a complete account of the human.

          4.5 From these considerations to jeevan

          These arguments strengthen the case for taking experience and understanding as real features that an account of nature must explain. They support questioning a picture of human life exhausted by the workings of an insentient mechanism. Their connection with Madhyasth Darshan is therefore substantive: jeevan is proposed as the sentient individual whose experiencing, understanding, and evaluation become evident through the body. Understanding one’s place in coexistence connects that individual’s experience with the capacity to question aims and fulfil relationships.

          Several further judgments are needed to accept that proposal. Human fulfilment exceeding bodily maintenance does not establish that experience exceeds every possible physical explanation. Even if experience is irreducible, it might belong to an organism, to a fundamental aspect of nature, or to the universal awareness proposed by Advaita. Identifying a distinct individual bearer would still leave its constitution and persistence to be established. The difference between bodily and personal fulfilment, the reality of experience, the unity of a subject, constitutional completeness, and survival are connected questions, but an answer to one cannot serve as the evidence for all the others.

          Madhyasth Darshan’s value here lies in offering a connected account to examine. Its unity of consciousness, individuality, understanding, and responsibility gives reasons to pursue it. Comparison must establish whether those connections explain the evidence more fully than the alternatives and whether its distinctive claims can withstand independent criticism.

          5. Critical Review

          The three accounts differ in explanatory ambition and in the evidence supporting their claims.

          5.1 Madhyasth Darshan — individual sentience in a shared world

          Strengths.

          • connects a real world, individual experience, understanding, and responsibility. Its account of fulfilment gives relationships and humane conduct a place within its explanation of what a human is.
          • A single sentient provides a proposed bearer of experienced unity and agency. It avoids combining many already distinct subjects, while retaining enduring individuality in contrast with Advaita’s final account.
          • Its demand that understanding become evident in work, conduct, and transmission creates an opportunity for public comparison when outcomes and conditions of success are clearly stated.

          Weaknesses.

          • The transition to sentience needs independent support. Constitutional completeness is a definite source claim, but its publicly identifiable conditions remain unspecified. The account must also explain why closing an insentient atom’s constitution should bring experience into existence. Describing that experience as previously latent would leave the connection to be explained (The Ontology of Coexistence §6.2.1).
          • The bodily medium needs a more exact account. The joint form allows bodily impairment to constrain expression. It must also explain why particular neural changes affect particular aspects of motivation, memory, and self-experience. This includes apparent changes in what a person values and is motivated to do, as well as difficulties in carrying out an otherwise intact intention. The account does not imply that every jeevan is already awakened or fully compassionate; the question is how neural changes relate to the person’s present understanding and motivation. Calling the brain an instrument leaves this detailed relation to be explained (§§1.3.4, 2.2.1, 2.3.3; The Ontology of Coexistence §6.2.2).
          • Persistence requires its own evidence. Neither experienced unity nor conservation establishes an immortal person. Temporary circulatory arrest and irreversible brain destruction are different conditions. In a prospective surgical study, some patients recalled experiences and some guessed presented words, but none explicitly recalled the experimental targets. Chance could not be excluded for the guesses. Experience timing was uncertain, and recordings could not establish that the reported experiences occurred without brain activity. This was no demonstration of post-death survival (Ross et al. 2025).
          • Practical verification needs protection against circularity. Agreement with a teacher cannot itself establish realisation. Assessment must allow disagreement, failed outcomes, and ethical conduct under rival philosophies. Even independently demonstrated practical benefit would support the tested conduct claims, while the proposed atom would still require separate evidence (The Ontology of Coexistence §6.2.3).

          Madhyasth Darshan offers a coherent direction for investigating individual sentience, but its particular bearer, bodily relation, and persistence remain claims to establish.

          5.2 Physicalism — strong bodily explanations, disputed completeness

          Strengths.

          • Physicalism draws support from the detailed dependence of human capacities on an evolved brain, body, and social world. The interventions and comparisons in §2 give this support greater force than correlation alone.
          • It can recognise meaning, personal fulfilment, and moral reasoning without adding another entity, while retaining psychological and social explanations of physically realised capacities.
          • The scientific explanations it draws on can be independently tested and corrected. These methods provide strong constraints on every account of the human, including Madhyasth Darshan.

          Weaknesses.

          • Bodily explanation has not settled the nature of experience. Explaining a person’s responses and reports leaves the dispute in §4.2: whether the experience is fully accounted for or whether something essential remains outside that description. Replies based on different ways of knowing the same state must be assessed as arguments.
          • Introspection needs explanation rather than blanket acceptance or dismissal. A physicalist may explain why experience seems irreducible through the brain’s simplified representations of its own activity. It must show how those representations arise and why the proposed correction is warranted (Graziano 2024).
          • Success cannot make the method itself a proof of physicalism. Science does not have to assume that it detects only matter. A claimed additional cause can be investigated through its effects. The existence of complete physical explanations for everything remains a stronger claim than the success of the explanations presently available.
          • A history of values is not their full justification. Explaining how a judgment arose does not by itself show that it is true or binding. Naturalistic ethics has serious resources for this task, but its conclusions require argument (§4.4).

          Physicalism remains a powerful interpretation of the evidence, while its claim to exhaust experience and understanding faces substantive philosophical objections.

          5.3 Advaita Vedanta — awareness and the limits of individuality

          Strengths.

          • Advaita gives awareness a foundational place and examines the identification of the self with changing bodily and mental states. Its disciplined inquiry treats the first-person character of experience as central.
          • Its non-dual account seeks to understand apparent plurality through one ultimate reality, while preserving inquiry and ethical obligations within ordinary life.

          Weaknesses.

          • The observer argument needs qualification. The fact that a body or thought can be observed does not establish that an embodied organism cannot observe itself. Advaita must answer that alternative as well as distinguish awareness from particular experienced contents.
          • Universal identity requires further warrant. Self-inquiry alone does not establish that all subjects are one , or that the world is . Those claims depend on wider reasoning and the authority accorded to Upanishadic testimony.
          • Dependent appearance remains difficult to explain. Advaita must account for ignorance and plurality without granting them the independent reality it denies. Its schools differ over how this is done.
          • The final significance of relationship is disputed. Madhyasth Darshan’s objection concerns whether individual persons and relationships retain ultimate significance when their separateness is overcome in knowledge; it does not deny Advaita’s teaching of humane conduct.

          Advaita offers a substantial challenge to bodily identification, while its universal conclusion requires commitments beyond the immediate presence of awareness.

          5.4 Where the argument leads

          This study favours the view that humans are not exhausted by insentient physicochemical organisation. Its reason is the apparent difference between what a process does and what it is like to undergo it: even a complete description of the first seems to leave the second unexplained. Unity, understanding, and evaluation give further substance to the question of the sentient knower. The physicalist reply that these are different ways of knowing the same reality remains a serious challenge to this judgment.

          Madhyasth Darshan is a promising account within that broader judgment because it joins individual sentience with a real world and responsibility in relationships. Awakening gives this connection a direction: the knower understands its place in coexistence and guides its aims and conduct through that understanding. This gives reasons to investigate jeevan; it does not establish constitutional completeness or immortality. Confidence in those further claims should depend on evidence that can distinguish them from competing explanations. A reproducible relation between the proposed unit and neural activity would matter to the bodily account. Independent evidence of personal continuity would matter to survival. Carefully compared outcomes in conduct would matter to practical claims.

          A useful test must specify the expected result and what would count against the proposal before its outcome is known. For conduct, this means comparing clearly described practices and outcomes with credible alternatives, with assessment open to people who do not accept the philosophy. For the body–jeevan relation, an unexplained neural result would be a starting point for inquiry; identifying jeevan would require evidence for its particular properties. Success in one test cannot substitute for the others.

          Further success in explaining experience and understanding entirely through bodily organisation would strengthen physicalism. Evidence for an independently persisting sentient unit would strengthen Madhyasth Darshan. The present inclination therefore carries a continuing obligation: to develop the affirmative account, answer its strongest objections, and revise it wherever better reasons or evidence require.

          Editorial Notes

          Source terms and their physical interpretation

          Constitutional completeness, molecular-bondage, and weight-bondage translate the source terms , anu-bandhan, and bhar-bandhan. They belong to ’s account of an insentient atom becoming sentient. Their use here does not identify a recognised particle, equate freedom from weight-bondage with zero rest mass, or establish a measured physical mechanism (MVD, p. 91). How these terms might connect to physical measurement remains open. The phrase “neither physical nor non-physical” also belongs to the darshan’s own classification of nature (JV, p. 36). If a sentient unit were independently identified, a further question would arise: whether it extends a physical account or supports a different understanding of nature.

          Here “atom” retains the source’s term paramanu for a unit with its own particle constitution. The account includes a nucleus and orbiting constituent particles; constitutional stability does not mean an absence of constituents (MVD, p. 78). Its relationship to the atoms identified in contemporary physics remains to be established. Calling it a constituentless primitive would change the claim being examined.

          Understanding the wider order

          The wider perspective discussed in §4.4 means understanding oneself and one’s actions within coexistence. It does not infer a physically non-local knower from the breadth of what is understood. Nor does anubhav mean possessing information about every event in existence. The connection from realisation to conduct follows the source’s account of understanding, resolve, thought, and the fulfilment of relationships (MVD, pp. 97–100). The business and shared-water example illustrates this connection; it is not a source example or a proof that all local activity causes collapse.

          Intelligibility and the transition to sentience

          Pervasive and the sentience of have related but distinct roles in the source account. Existence is intelligible; the constitutionally complete unit is the knower. Describing their connection as “latency actualised” would add an interpretation: it would require showing why intelligibility entails a latent capacity for felt experience. The study therefore states the source’s irreversible transition positively without using that interpretation as its explanation (MVD, pp. 35, 91; SB, PDF pp. 48, 92).
