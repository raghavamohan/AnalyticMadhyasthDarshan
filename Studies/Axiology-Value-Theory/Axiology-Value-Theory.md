**The question:** How does value belong to a unit as activity in coexistence, and how is it understood, established, and fulfilled in human living? What forms does value take, what endures or changes with time, and in what senses can values be compared? How does the Co-existentialism of Shri A. Nagraj compare with Advaita Vedanta, modern Western value theory, and the natural and social sciences?

This study examines value, evaluation, and the ordering of values in **Madhyasth Darshan** (Co-existentialism), as presented by **Shri A. Nagraj**. Its central problem is how value belongs to active units in coexistence and takes different forms in human living. It asks what remains definite through changing circumstances, how those forms are related, and what warrants comparison and correct evaluation.

[The Ontology of Coexistence](../The-Ontology-of-Coexistence/The-Ontology-of-Coexistence.pdf) supplies the account of Omnipresence, units, form, properties, essential nature, *dharma*, the four orders, and *jeevan*. [The Epistemology of Coexistence](../The-Epistemology-of-Coexistence/The-Epistemology-of-Coexistence.pdf) examines knowing, believing, recognising, fulfilling, and evaluating. Axiology connects that ground and those activities with the forms of *mulya*: the harmonies of *jeevan*, humane conduct, relationship values and their expressions, and usefulness, aesthetic qualification, and symbolic valuation in things. Recognition and learning concern how their meanings become understood and reliably fulfilled.

Ethics, aesthetics, prosperity, family relationships, and education develop particular applications of this account. Axiology must nevertheless explain their common basis. It cannot leave labour, material provision, or educational practice outside its argument when fulfilment and evaluation depend on them. The comparison with Advaita, Western philosophy, and science tests both this explanatory reach and the warrant for its claims.

## Standpoint and scope

These studies are written from the standpoint of a **scientist and technologist** — someone trained to graduate-level **physics and mathematics**, at home with contemporary cosmology, quantum theory, conservation laws, and the logic of formal models.

From that background, value can appear to be something minds add to an otherwise valueless world: a preference, a reward signal, or a social convention. Scientific accounts of mechanism and measurement do not by themselves settle whether this appearance is correct. The status of well-being, the passage from factual description to obligation, and the possibility of goods independent of actual preference remain questions for investigation.

This paper reads the primary Madhyasth Darshan texts, states the account they develop, and compares it with **Advaita Vedanta**, **modern Western philosophy**, and **the natural and social sciences**. Physics and mathematics are **one leg** of the comparison. The purpose is rigorous comparative understanding through definitions, arguments, textual accuracy, and fit with public knowledge, rather than persuasion or devotional endorsement.

The philosophy is presented in clear, checkable prose. An optional appendix records the structural relations of value through qualitative predicates, distinguishing primary definitions from the assumptions of a formal reconstruction. A fuller mathematical treatment may follow as definitions become stable across the studies; understanding the exposition requires no mathematical formalism.

## 1. The Madhyasth Darshan Answer

Madhyasth Darshan holds that value belongs to existence as coexistence. A unit has value through its essentiality and participation, whether anyone approves of it or recognises it correctly. Human beings encounter this value through *jeevan*, whose activities include recognition, evaluation, and the fulfilment of values in relationships and work. Human living involves several forms of *mulya*: their content, the circumstances of fulfilment, and the purposes they serve determine what can endure, change, or be compared. Awakening brings these activities into accordance with reality and makes their fulfilment continuous in living.

### 1.1 The active unit and value as essentiality

Value (*mulya*) names the essentiality (*maulikta*) of a unit in coexistence. Essentiality concerns the unit's characteristic participation and the innateness it bears in its order of existence. It belongs to the unit through its essential nature (*swabhav*) and *dharma*, and remains the basis of evaluation whether it is correctly recognised or mistaken (MVD, pp. 47, 124–125; SB, pp. 50, 87).

> **“Entire beingness implies the essentiality of units in every plane and order. Essentiality refers to value.”**
> — SB, p. 50

A unit is a definite whole of activity. Form (*roop*) concerns its bounded configuration; properties (*guna*) concern its relative powers and their effects in mutuality; essential nature concerns the characteristic conduct and usefulness of those powers in its order; *dharma* concerns its inseparable innateness. These aspects belong together in the existing unit. A quantitative description establishes dimensions, amounts, or rates; understanding essentiality identifies what the activity amounts to in participation, such as integration, nourishment, protection, or resolution. This qualitative significance is the subject of value (MVD, p. 47; SB, pp. 60–62, 87; AVD, p. 184).

The unit's activeness is grounded in its saturation in Omnipresence, *satta*: the all-pervading ground in which units exist and remain active. [The Ontology of Coexistence](../The-Ontology-of-Coexistence/The-Ontology-of-Coexistence.pdf), §1.2, develops saturation and effort–motion–result; here they ground value in the unit's continuing activity. Strength (*bal*) in state (*sthiti*) and power (*shakti*) in motion (*gati*) are inseparable aspects of its activity. Basic impulsion (*mool cheshta*) is expressed through effort, motion, and result (*shram–gati–parinam*): effort is aligned with *dharma* and essential nature, motion with essential nature and properties, and result with form. Essential nature thus participates in both the strength borne and the power expressed. These relations describe the same continuing activity in mutuality (MVD, pp. 35, 40; SB, pp. 60–62).

The four orders differ in their characteristic participation. The material order manifests integration and disintegration; the pranic order adds growth and vitalising or devitalising conduct. Animal living joins body and *jeevan*, with the hope to live; the knowledge order brings the human possibility of understanding and the purpose of happiness. Formation and dissolution both participate in natural orderliness. The significance of an effect must therefore be understood through the order and mutuality involved. Reciprocal participation in insentient units and reflective human evaluation have their respective places in this account (SB, pp. 179–182, 249).

Human essential nature is expressed as fortitude, courage, generosity, kindness, grace, and compassion, as §1.3 will specify; human *dharma* is happiness. Human beings fulfil their value through orderliness in themselves and participation in wider orderliness. Knowing concerns understanding reality, while believing concerns accepting a meaning as a basis of activity; correct recognition depends on their accord ([The Epistemology of Coexistence](../The-Epistemology-of-Coexistence/The-Epistemology-of-Coexistence.pdf), §1.5). Human conduct can be inhumane, humane, or higher-humane as understanding develops, while happiness remains human *dharma* through both mistaken and resolved attempts to attain it (MVD, pp. 15, 97, 115, 125; SB, pp. 179–182, 249).

Essentiality is already present in the active unit. Human understanding can become established, and labour can establish a useful configuration of material. The former concerns the knower's acceptance and conduct; the latter concerns what the configured material can fulfil. Both take place within an existence whose units already have essentiality (MVD, pp. 56, 83, 124–125; SB, p. 87).

### 1.2 The structure of value in jeevan

*Jeevan* is the conscious unit whose faculties are *atma*, *buddhi*, *chitta*, *vritti*, and *mun*. The harmonies of these faculties are one form of value among those distinguished in §1.3. Values inherent in *jeevan* are activities: they are understood, accepted, felt, evaluated, and expressed through its living. Usefulness, also called object value, belongs to things; human experience and evaluation of value belong to *jeevan*. Evaluation itself is sentiment activity, *bhav-kriya*, whose correctness depends on awakening (MVD, p. 203; JV, pp. 137–139).

Actionless, changeless knowledge is named *chetana* in relation to sentient nature, *chaitanya*; [The Epistemology of Coexistence](../The-Epistemology-of-Coexistence/The-Epistemology-of-Coexistence.pdf), §1.1, develops this distinction. Realisation and the understanding of value belong to the activity of *jeevan* saturated in that ground (MVD, pp. 35, 74–75, 97–100; SB, pp. 63–64).

The relation of state and motion gives value an active structure within each faculty. State is the strength borne in the activity; motion is its expression as power. In awakened *jeevan*, the understood basis and its meaningful expression have the following forms (KD, §3.8, p. 84).

| Faculty | Activity in state / strength | Activity in motion / power |
|---|---|---|
| *Atma* | Realisation (*anubhav*) | Evidence (*praman*) |
| *Buddhi* | Understanding (*bodh*) of actuality, reality, and truth | Resolve (*sankalp*) to express it |
| *Chitta* | Contemplation (*chintan*), with direct apprehension (*sakshatkar*) of justice, *dharma*, and truth | Visualisation (*chitran*) |
| *Vritti* | Deliberation or comparison (*tulan*) in justice, *dharma*, and truth | Analysis (*vishleshan*) |
| *Mun* | Tasting (*asvadan*) the value accepted | Selection (*chayan*) for its meaningful fulfilment |

The faculties also bear definite relations to one another. Happiness (*sukh*), peace (*shanti*), contentment (*santosh*), and bliss (*anand*) name harmonies between their activities. Concurrence (*samatva*) or absence of opposition (*nirvirodh*) has a specified content: happiness is resolution with behaviour free of wrongdoing; peace involves thought free of injustice; contentment involves desire free of attachment; bliss involves understanding free of ignorance. The value is the harmony lived and felt in those activities (MVD, pp. 327–328, 331, 333, 346; JV, p. 138; MAD, reader p. 47).

| Jeevan value | Harmony between faculties | Cumulative fulfilment in living |
|---|---|---|
| Happiness (*sukh*) | *Mun* and *vritti* | Resolution |
| Peace (*shanti*) | *Vritti* and *chitta* | Resolution and prosperity |
| Contentment (*santosh*) | *Chitta* and *buddhi* | Resolution, prosperity, and fearlessness |
| Bliss (*anand*) | *Buddhi* and *atma* | Resolution, prosperity, fearlessness, and coexistence |

The cumulative fulfilment connects these harmonies with human living: each wider fulfilment retains the preceding content (KD, §3.12, p. 112). These fulfilments extend from the person through family and society to universal orderliness, as developed in [The Ontology of Coexistence](../The-Ontology-of-Coexistence/The-Ontology-of-Coexistence.pdf), §1.15. Inner satisfaction becomes evident through self-governed conduct across these relationships (JV, pp. 53, 62, 140; AVD, p. 228). The harmonies' structural definiteness lies in the specified relations of activity and their understood meaning. A person's actual understanding and conduct determine whether the harmony is fulfilled; self-examination and evidence in living bear on whether its presence has been correctly recognised (§1.4; §§A.1–A.3).

Contentment includes freedom from the sentiment of lack together with readiness to evidence production beyond need through science aligned with wisdom. Its paired expression is abundance, *shree*. Bliss is borne at *buddhi*; ultimate bliss, *paramanand*, belongs to realisation in *atma*, with authenticity as its expression and evidence. The concurrence of *atma* and Omnipresence is eternally present, while realisation brings that reality into awakened human living (MVD, pp. 327–328, 331; JV, p. 138).

Evaluation at *vritti* can take pleasant–unpleasant, beneficial–harmful, and gain–loss as its perspectives. These orient attention towards sensory attraction, bodily advantage, and acquisition. The humane perspectives are justice–injustice, *dharma–adharma*, and truth–untruth. Justice concerns fulfilled values in relationships; human *dharma* concerns resolution and happiness; truth concerns coexistence as it is. Under humane understanding, pleasure and bodily health have their place within justice, while prosperity replaces acquisitive profit as the economic purpose (MVD, pp. 58, 66, 336–337; JV, pp. 139–140).

Correct evaluation involves the coordinated activity of the whole *jeevan*. Contemplation and direct apprehension at *chitta* concern the reality whose meaning is visualised; understanding at *buddhi* supplies certainty and resolve; realisation at *atma* grounds its evidence in living. Deliberation, analysis, tasting, and selection express that understood meaning. A person can visualise a desirable result and calculate how to obtain it while still misunderstanding the relationship involved. Establishment of understanding concerns the development through which these activities accord with reality and become continuous in fulfilment (MVD, pp. 83, 99–100, 156, 203; KD, p. 84).

Constitutional completeness is *jeevan*'s settled constitution, activity completeness its freedom from delusion, and conduct completeness the continuity of that understanding in living; [The Ontology of Coexistence](../The-Ontology-of-Coexistence/The-Ontology-of-Coexistence.pdf), §§1.6–1.6.3, develops these transitions, distinguishing inherent capacity from its fulfilment in understanding and conduct (MVD, p. 83; KD, p. 37).

### 1.3 Forms of value in human living

The principal forms of value bring different aspects of human living into view: the harmonies of *jeevan*, humane essential nature, established relationship values and their expressions, and usefulness and aesthetic value in things. Symbolic value concerns money and assigned valuation in exchange (MVD, pp. 183, 306; JV, pp. 43, 55, 137–139). Their bearers, fulfilment, and relations can be distinguished as follows.

| Form of value | Bearer and meaning | Time | Kind of comparison |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Jeevan* values | Happiness, peace, contentment, and bliss as faculty harmonies | Enduring content; fulfilment concerns present activity | Cumulative fulfilment (§1.2) |
| Human values | Human essential nature in conduct | Enduring content; conduct develops | Humane and higher-humane expression |
| Established values | Values borne in *sthiti/bal* and fulfilled in relationships | Relational content endures across time and place | Trust as common; love as complete |
| Expressed or civic values | Corresponding outward values in *gati/shakti* | Acts and means meet changing circumstances | Each expression is dedicated to its established counterpart |
| Usefulness value | What a thing can fulfil in nourishment, protection, and participation | Constancy and temporality claims distinguished in §1.5 | Greater or less for a specified need; assessed apart from price |
| Aesthetic value | Convenience and aesthetic qualification added to usefulness | Temporal with usefulness (§1.5) | Added convenience assessed relative to usefulness |
| Symbolic value | Money and assigned monetary valuation | Amount or price can change | Monetary amounts; usefulness assessed separately |

The dedication sequence also names need (*avashyakta mulya*). In this map, need is treated through the assessment that connects useful means with the values their deployment fulfils, rather than as a further row (§1.6; MAD, reader p. 34).

These forms participate together in living. Generosity, for example, articulates humane essential nature and also gives expression to care in a relationship. The faculty location of a relationship value identifies a named activity within the coordinated *jeevan*. Its understanding and fulfilment involve the meaning of the relationship and the whole conscious unit (MVD, pp. 125, 203, 331–340; AVD, pp. 91–94). The temporal claims about usefulness are stated separately in §1.5 and examined in §7.5; enduring relational content and situated fulfilment are developed in §1.4 (MAD, reader p. 51).

Human values articulate the essential nature appropriate to resolved living. Fortitude (*dhirata*) is firmness towards justice; courage (*veerta*) deploys intellectual and physical powers to make justice available; generosity (*udarta*) offers body, mind, and wealth according to need for awakening, health, and prosperity. These characterise humane expression. Kindness (*daya*), grace (*kripa*), and compassion (*karuna*) characterise higher-humane expression: kindness supplies missing content where receptivity is present, grace develops missing receptivity where content is available, and compassion works to make both available where both are absent. Love includes their combined expression (MVD, pp. 52, 60–61, 125, 331, 340).

Established values (*sthapit mulya*) are borne in strength or state; expressed values, also called civic or *shishta* values, are their corresponding expressions in motion or power. Their one-to-one correspondence gives understood value a definite form of expression. The nine principal established relationship values and their expressions occupy the following named positions (MAD, reader p. 30; JV, p. 108; AVD, pp. 91–94; MVD, pp. 331–340).

| Established value in bal / sthiti | Corresponding expressed value in gati | Faculty |
|---|---|---|
| Care (*mamta*) | Generosity (*udarta*) | *Mun* |
| Guidance (*vatsalya*) | Naturalness (*sahajta*) | *Chitta* |
| Trust (*vishwas*) | Concordance (*saujanyata*) | *Vritti* |
| Affection (*sneha*) | Dedication (*nishtha*) | *Mun* |
| Gratitude (*kritagyata*) | Humility (*saumyata*) | *Vritti* |
| Glory (*gaurav*) | Simplicity (*saralta*) | *Vritti* |
| Love (*prem*) | Non-otherness (*ananyata*) | *Chitta* |
| Reverence (*shraddha*) | Devoutness (*pujyata*) | *Chitta* |
| Respect (*samman*) | Cordiality (*sauhardta*) | *Mun* |

Care sustains nurture and protection; guidance sustains development towards comprehensive understanding. Affection gives continuity to just meeting; gratitude acknowledges help received towards development; glory accepts an example for emulation; reverence moves towards excellence and completeness; respect accepts excellence in personality and talent. Their expressions make these acceptances evident in conduct (MVD, pp. 331–340).

Trust joins fulfilled mutual value with understanding of orderliness and communication of resolution. Its expression is cooperation, participation, and complementarity. Love includes realisation of completeness and the combined expression of kindness, grace, and compassion; non-otherness expresses it through complementary activity with human beings and the rest of nature. Trust is the common relationship value (*samya mulya*), and love the complete value (*purna mulya*) (MVD, pp. 331, 336; MAD, reader pp. 30, 43).

### 1.4 Recognition, establishment, fulfilment and justice

A relationship (*sambandh*) is a mutuality whose expectations are predetermined towards completeness. A contact (*sampark*) is a mutuality whose expectations are voluntarily determined. Both involve value and are answerable to justice. Agreed expectations carry responsibilities concerning what is undertaken and how it is fulfilled (MVD, pp. 61–62; KD, p. 20).

Established relationship values endure across proximity and distance, past, present, and future. Their definite content supplies the basis for recognising what is due. The intellectual and material means of fulfilment are assessed in the circumstances: nurturing a child or helping a learner calls for suitable resources, ability, and receptivity. Care can remain the recognised value while the food, protection, or help required changes with the person's situation (MAD, reader p. 51; MVD, pp. 61–62, 331–340).

The actual relationship and the person's recognition of it can differ. Knowing and believing concern correctness of recognition; fulfilment gives recognised value expression in mutuality. When the meaning of a relationship is understood and accepted, its value becomes available as the basis of selection and participation. In this sense understanding becomes established in the person, and the corresponding expression becomes available for continuing fulfilment. The value's essential content remains the reference through these changes in understanding and conduct (MVD, pp. 83, 97, 203; JV, pp. 138–139; KD, p. 84).

The named relationships include parent–child, sibling, spousal, master–associate, teacher–student, friendship, and orderliness relationships. [Family Relationships and Values](../Family-Relationships-And-Values/Family-Relationships-And-Values.pdf), §§1.1.3–1.2.1, develops these names through the responsibilities and purposes they identify. Their fulfilment involves comprehensive resolution, nurturing, protection, usefulness or complementarity, right-use, and purposeful-use. Humane duty consists in acts that fulfil values; family, society, and orderliness also confer rights within participation (MVD, pp. 61–62, 190; JV, pp. 108–109).

Justice comprises recognition of relationships, fulfilment of their values, evaluation, and mutual satisfaction. Evaluation examines whether participation has fulfilled the recognised responsibility and whether the outcome accords with human essentiality. The relationship and its values supply the standard; mutual satisfaction is the fulfilment of this relational process (MVD, pp. 311, 336).

Evaluation also guides deliberation before action. The person recognises the responsibility, assesses available means, and selects an appropriate contribution. Accomplished participation then supplies the outcome to evaluate. Self-examination asks whether justice is present on both sides; the resulting conduct and its effects also become evident to those with whom the person lives and works (JV, pp. 98, 138–139; KD, p. 84).

Nourishing a child illustrates these distinctions. The food's usefulness concerns what it can provide for nourishment. Care concerns the recognised relationship and responsibility for nurture, while generosity expresses the offering of available means. The caregiver's understanding, the adequacy of the food and provision, and the child's actual nourishment enter the assessment in their respective ways. The example brings object usefulness, human understanding, and relational fulfilment into one participation (§§1.3, 1.5).

Understanding the responsibility can coexist with inadequate resources or skill. An effective contribution can also occur while a learner is still developing the understanding that would make it independently sustained. Claims to established care therefore call for examination of both understood meaning and responsibilities actually fulfilled. This distinction gives education a practical task: develop correct recognition, capacity and means, and continuity of fulfilment (§1.8; MVD, pp. 61, 94, 203).

The same *jeevan* recognises, evaluates, and tastes value. Happiness, peace, contentment, and bliss are present in the experience of established values; inward harmony and outward fulfilment belong together in meaningful participation (JV, pp. 138–139; MAD, reader p. 51). What has been expressed can be examined again in thought and settled in understanding. Further expression can then embody a clarified recognition, a corrected means, or an understanding sustained through its evidence (MVD, pp. 83, 218; KD, pp. 112–113).

### 1.5 The value of things

Usefulness value (*upyogita mulya*) concerns what a thing can do for nourishment, bodily protection, and participation. Food, housing, and adornment figure among common aspirations, with transport and communication among special aspirations. Understanding human purposes and family needs guides the assessment of what should be produced, acquired, and used (MVD, pp. 56–57; JV, p. 123).

Usefulness is described as definite through examples of useful things. A kilogram of wheat is said to have the same usefulness as a hundred years earlier, with interference nevertheless capable of diminishing it. The usefulness of sesame, medicine, rice, wheat, and vegetables is likewise described as constant. Aesthetic addition is meaningful where convenience is added to usefulness (JV, pp. 123, 137–138).

Usefulness and beauty are also described as temporal (*samayik*), because needs, use, exchange, and deployment are temporal. This account identifies enduring established, human, and *jeevan* values as the basis of stable social living (MAD, reader p. 51). The relation between this temporal description and the constancy claims remains a textual question (§7.5).

Human labour can establish usefulness and aesthetic value on natural abundance. Application (*prayog*) is the endeavour, continued until success, to establish these values through labour in response to need; production (*utpadan*) standardises the successful process. Here establishment concerns a useful configuration and its capacity for fulfilment. Production–work includes the skills, resources, agriculture, and industry through which families can produce beyond assessed need (MVD, pp. 56, 260, 268; JV, pp. 137–138).

Money is symbolic value (*prateek mulya*). An increase in its quantity or in the price assigned to a good does not itself increase the good's usefulness. Manufactured scarcity can raise symbolic valuation while obstructing access to things people need. Prosperity consequently requires understanding need, the ability and readiness to produce adequate provision, and its availability for right-use (MVD, pp. 183, 331; SB, pp. 211–212).

Use deploys means in production and service. Right-use (*sadupyog*) directs body, mind, and wealth towards participation in family orderliness. Purposeful-use (*prayojansheelta*) serves awakening, undivided society, and universal orderliness. These ends give purposeful-use a social scope from family to world society ([How Undivided Society Is Established](../How-Undivided-Society-Is-Established/How-Undivided-Society-Is-Established.pdf), §1.1; MVD, p. 57).

In nourishing a child, the food's preparation gives useful material an appropriate form, while its provision fulfils a responsibility of care. Price records a monetary valuation; nourishment and fulfilled care require their own assessment (§1.4).

Exchange requires evaluation of labour and of the goods through which its results become available. The proposed order protects labour and its fruits from exploitation and seeks exchange of labour-valued goods free from profit and loss. Labour deployment belongs to production; labour-value assessment belongs to exchange; evaluation of established and expressed values belongs to justice. These are distinct responsibilities within the same social order (MVD, pp. 260, 268–269; SB, p. 237).

Rightful wealth can be received through remuneration, gift, and award. Material provision thus includes legitimate receiving alongside production, with implications for the care of people whose needs exceed their present productive capacity (AVD, p. 170; MVD, pp. 61, 340). Production and use also involve the other orders: natural abundance is to be used in accordance with usefulness and regeneration, and human participation is to nurture the conditions of coexistence (SB, pp. 141, 200–201). The ecological decisions and procedures needed to fulfil that responsibility remain questions for investigation (§7.9).

### 1.6 How values are ordered

The forms of value are related through dedication (*samarpan*). Usefulness serves need; need serves expressed or *shishta* value; expressed value serves established value; established value serves human value; and human value serves *jeevan* value. This order gives production and material provision their purpose in fulfilled relationships, humane conduct, and the harmony of *jeevan*. Need links useful means with the values their deployment fulfils (MAD, reader p. 34).

Higher value (*guru mulyan*) concerns a long-lasting result or non-transformability. Advancement moves towards higher value or resolution, and mutual ascent supports such development. The contrast with lower value and decline concerns the endurance and direction of fulfilment. This supplies a criterion of development through which greater or lesser value can be considered (MVD, pp. 43, 110, 126).

Inclusion has its own scope. The account of the four orders describes the more developed as including what is borne in the less developed: the higher order retains the properties, essential nature, and *dharma* of the lower orders within its more comprehensive living. The relation is asymmetric; the less comprehensive cannot encompass the more comprehensive within its existing measure. Knowing the more developed remains possible and belongs to the opportunity for development (MVD, pp. 47, 219–222; KD, p. 37). Cumulative fulfilment in human living likewise retains resolution as prosperity, fearlessness, and coexistence become fulfilled (§1.2; KD, p. 112).

Commonness and completeness concern the scope of relationship value. Trust is common in relationships, while love is complete in its encompassing participation and expression of kindness, grace, and compassion. These determinations concern what is fulfilled in mutuality (MAD, reader pp. 30, 43).

Dedication therefore specifies the purpose served, endurance concerns lasting fulfilment, inclusion concerns the content retained in more comprehensive development, and commonness or completeness concerns the scope of relationship value. Each relation makes a particular kind of comparison intelligible. Their implications for contested obligations and allocation require examination in the actual relationships involved (§§7.1, 7.7).

Acquisitive profit gives greater and lesser worth a different purpose: obtaining greater worth in return for lesser worth or labour. The other party may surrender the greater through helplessness, willingness, or ignorance. This perspective is rejected in favour of prosperity through production and exchange governed by justice. Pleasure and bodily health remain within humane living, while useful provision serves resolved relationships and the fulfilment of *jeevan* (MVD, pp. 58, 67; JV, pp. 139–140).

### 1.7 Error and awakening

Delusion involves over-evaluation, under-evaluation, and mis-evaluation. Something may be assigned a significance greater or smaller than its essentiality warrants, or recognised as something other than what it is (MVD, pp. 152, 306). Mistaking a rope for a snake illustrates mis-evaluation; mistaking iron for gold or gold for iron illustrates over- and under-evaluation (SB, PDF p. 146). At the human level these errors affect self-assessment, relationships, acquisition, and the use of resources.

Correct evaluation develops with awakening. *Nirakarshan*, freedom from attraction or attachment in the relevant definition, is freedom from the three evaluative errors. It permits conduct governed by essentiality. Non-doership concerns freedom from wrongful action within a life that remains active. Self-evaluation is the first step in the study of awakening, and complete correctness belongs to the coordinated activity of awakened *jeevan* (MVD, pp. 87, 152, 203).

Fear and temptation direct relationships through threatened harm or promised acquisition. They cannot serve as relationship values or be evaluated as fulfilled values. Recognition of relationships, their responsibilities, and mutual satisfaction provides the humane basis of participation (JV, pp. 109–110).

Human beings deploy body, mind, and wealth towards what they regard as most valuable. Under delusion the four basic instincts—eating, sleep, defence, and mating—are treated as the highest purpose; discerning evaluation can place the three *eshanas*—progeny, wealth, and reputation—highest; delusionless evaluation places freedom from delusion highest. Evaluation in these states is correspondingly named attachment, awakening, and reality. The change concerns the evaluator's understanding and the purpose directing desire and conduct (MVD, pp. 58, 60, 319).

The body serves nourishment, protection, and participation; *jeevan* fulfils its purpose through understanding and happiness. Awakening develops the recognition of these purposes and their proper relation. The evaluator can then recognise usefulness, relational responsibility, and inward fulfilment in their respective places (JV, pp. 93–94, 123).

### 1.8 Continuity of value in persons and tradition

Continuity concerns understood value becoming a sustained basis of fulfilment. A person can act generously on one occasion and later respond with miserliness. The earlier contribution may have fulfilled a need, while generosity has yet to become continuous. Establishment involves the person's own understanding, through which meaningful conduct can be sustained across circumstances (MVD, pp. 61, 94; KD, pp. 37, 84).

*Sanskar* comprises acceptances towards completeness and the understanding and tendencies through which meaningful activity is expressed. It includes bodily, verbal, and mental participation through doing, enabling another's doing, and consenting. *Susanskar* supports awakening and resolved living; *kusanskar* supports decline. What a person understands and accepts thus bears on subsequent conduct and on the examples made available to others (MVD, pp. 90, 94).

Learning from another's conduct can occasion rethinking. *Aapyayan*, internal reception, concerns welcoming, tasting, and taking value into oneself, evidenced through evaluation. *Parimarjan*, qualitative refinement, concerns change in hope, thought, desire, and resolve. What is expressed and examined in living can become accepted in understanding and realisation and supply the basis for further expression. These activities connect received example, the learner's own understanding, and continuing participation (MAD, reader p. 45; MVD, pp. 94, 99–100; KD, pp. 112–113).

Understood value is evidenced in lawful work, just relationships, resolution, and realised coexistence (MVD, pp. 12, 80, 174); [The Ontology of Coexistence](../The-Ontology-of-Coexistence/The-Ontology-of-Coexistence.pdf), §1.11, develops law and conformance. Education, justice, production, exchange, and health are institutional fields for examining fulfilment, developed in its §1.15 (MVD, p. 260; AVD, p. 265). Values supply purposes whose continuing determinations in wealth, behaviour, and use of means belong to character and ethics (JV, p. 55; [Ethics and Morals in Human Beings](../Ethics-And-Morals-In-Human-Beings/Ethics-And-Morals-In-Human-Beings.pdf), §§3.1, 3.5–3.6).

Education develops knowing, believing, recognising, and fulfilling through study, practice, and self-evaluation (KD, pp. 18–19; MVD, p. 87); [The Epistemology of Coexistence](../The-Epistemology-of-Coexistence/The-Epistemology-of-Coexistence.pdf), §§1.5–1.6, examines these activities and their transmission.

Understood meaning, actual fulfilment, and evidence of fulfilment remain distinguishable. Appendix A states their structural relations; §7.8 examines how learning becomes establishment and continuity.

## 2. The Advaita Vedanta Answer

Advaita Vedanta understands liberation through knowledge of the identity of *Atman* and *Brahman*. Ignorance supports identification with limited bodies, minds, and possessions; knowledge removes that misidentification. This account gives liberation a distinctive place among human purposes while retaining a domain of duties, disciplined action, and ethical preparation in empirical life (BSB, Introduction and 1.1.4).

### 2.1 Human purposes and the status of liberation

The four *purusharthas* are *dharma*, *artha*, *kama*, and *moksha*: right living, material means, enjoyment, and liberation. Material acquisition and enjoyment have their proper place under *dharma*. Liberation differs from an acquired possession or produced experience because knowledge discloses the Self whose reality does not depend on production. Discrimination between enduring reality and transient attainments therefore guides the aspirant's priorities (BSB, 1.1.4; VC, vv. 17–20).

### 2.2 The Self and dearness

The Maitreyi teaching examines why husband, wife, children, wealth, and other things are dear. Their dearness is for the sake of the Self, and the inquiry proceeds through hearing, reflection, and contemplation of the Self. In Advaita this teaching directs attention beyond identification with the individual ego towards the reality through which dearness is intelligible (BU, 2.4.5).

Bliss belongs to the Upanishadic account of Brahman and the freedom from fear associated with its knowledge. The *Taittiriya* inquiry moves through increasingly inward accounts of the person and culminates in the teaching of Brahman as the source of bliss. The discrimination of the sheaths also separates conditioned experience from the Self that is disclosed through their examination (TU, 2.1–2.9; VC, v. 152).

### 2.3 Qualification and the transformation of attention

Discrimination, dispassion, the disciplines of restraint and concentration, and desire for liberation orient the student towards knowledge. They are cultivated through a life of study and discipline. Their educational role is to make understanding possible and stable, including the ability to distinguish the permanent from the impermanent and to inquire without being governed by attachment (BSB, 1.1.1; VC, vv. 17–20).

The divine qualities include fearlessness, purity of mind, generosity, self-control, truthfulness, compassion, and freedom from anger. These are positive qualities of conduct as well as conditions of spiritual preparation. Ethical formation develops capacities for living and inquiry within the empirical world (BG, 16.1–16.3).

### 2.4 Action without attachment

Prescribed action is to be performed, and bodily maintenance itself depends on action. Action without attachment is taught as a discipline leading towards the highest good. The example of Janaka and the instruction concerning *loka-sangraha*, the holding together of the world, also give action a social purpose. The wise are to consider the effect of their example on those still engaged in action (BG, 3.8, 3.19–3.20, 3.25–3.26).

Shankara's commentary distinguishes the qualifications and purposes of agents within these instructions. Action can prepare the mind for knowledge, while action associated with the welfare and guidance of others can continue without the realised person's identification as an individual doer. Liberation remains knowledge of an already accomplished reality (BG with Shankara's commentary, 3.19–3.26; BSB, 1.1.4).

### 2.5 Value knowledge and its evidence

Scriptural teaching, reasoned inquiry, and the removal of mistaken identification are central to knowledge of the Self. An experience of pleasure or an outwardly successful undertaking has a different evidential role from the knowledge that removes ignorance. The account therefore connects ethical discipline and inquiry while distinguishing their fruits from liberation itself (BU, 2.4.5; BSB, Introduction and 1.1.1).

### 2.6 Empirical life and ultimate fulfilment

The distinction between empirical and ultimate standpoints allows duties, relationships, and spiritual preparation to retain their force while identification with a separate limited self is questioned. Advaita's ultimate account of fulfilment concerns the knowledge of non-duality. Its treatment of finite goods must be read together with its teachings on duty, compassion, discipline, and action for the world's maintenance (BU, 2.4.5; BG, 3.19–3.26, 16.1–16.3).

## 3. Modern Western Philosophy on Value

Modern Western value theory contains competing accounts of what is good, what bears value, and how value is known. Metaethics examines the status of normative judgments; welfare theory examines what makes life go well for a person; practical philosophy examines reasons, duties, and conflicts. These inquiries overlap without yielding a single Western doctrine (SEP Value Theory).

### 3.1 Intrinsic, final and instrumental value

Something can be valued as a means to another good or for its own sake. This final–instrumental distinction differs from the distinction between value grounded in intrinsic features and value dependent on relations or context. A historically significant object, for example, may be valued for its own sake partly because of its history (SEP Intrinsic vs Extrinsic Value).

Moore asks what would be good considered in isolation, but also argues that the value of a whole need not equal the sum of its parts' values. His principle of organic unities makes the configuration of a valuable whole matter to assessment. Intrinsic value therefore does not invariably entail simple addition of separately valued constituents (Moore 1903, §§18–22).

### 3.2 Natural facts and normative conclusions

Hume calls attention to the unexplained transition from statements about what is to conclusions about what ought to be. Moore's open-question argument challenges proposed definitions of goodness in terms of some other property: understanding that something has the property appears to leave open whether it is good (Hume 1739, III.1.1; Moore 1903, §§10–14).

The two arguments address different problems. Hume's passage concerns inference; Moore's concerns the analysis of goodness. Neither by itself settles every form of ethical naturalism. A theory may propose a substantive rather than definitional identity, or argue for normative standards through the characteristic flourishing of living beings. Such proposals require arguments for the identification and for its practical authority (SEP Natural Law Ethics; SEP Moral Realism).

### 3.3 Realism, construction and anti-realism

Moral realists hold that some moral judgments are true and that their truth is not exhausted by the speaker's approval. Naturalist and non-naturalist versions disagree about the relation between moral facts and the natural world. Expressivists explain moral language through practical attitudes and commitments; error theorists argue that ordinary moral claims systematically fail in what they purport to assert (SEP Moral Realism; SEP Moral Anti-Realism).

Constructivist approaches connect normative standards with an appropriate procedure of practical reasoning. Their relation to realism depends on the particular account of construction and objectivity. Expressivist theories likewise develop accounts of disagreement, consistency, and practical criticism. The categories identify research programmes whose adequacy turns on their detailed treatment of truth, reasons, and judgment (SEP Moral Anti-Realism).

### 3.4 Fitting attitudes and reasons

Fitting-attitude accounts analyse value through the attitudes it is appropriate to have towards an object: admiration, love, desire, or respect, for example. The relevant attitude is fitting, rather than merely actual or widely shared. A central difficulty is distinguishing reasons that bear on an object's value from incentives or threats that make an attitude advantageous to its holder (SEP Fitting Attitude Theories).

Scanlon's buck-passing account explains the value of something through other properties that give reasons for appropriate responses. Value identifies the presence of such reasons rather than supplying an additional independent reason. The account shifts inquiry towards what makes a response warranted (Scanlon 1998, ch. 2).

### 3.5 Pleasure, desire and objective goods

Hedonistic theories make pleasure and the absence of pain central to welfare. Desire theories locate welfare in the fulfilment of desires, often with qualifications concerning information, rationality, or the scope of the person's concern. Objective-list theories hold that some things can benefit a person independently of current desire or pleasure, such as knowledge, achievement, or worthwhile relationships (Parfit 1984, Appendix I; SEP Well-Being).

Nozick's experience machine asks whether an entirely satisfying simulated experience would supply everything that matters in life. The thought experiment tests whether doing things, being a certain person, and contact with reality matter beyond experience. Its force depends on reflection about the case and the intuitions it elicits (Nozick 1974, pp. 42–45).

### 3.6 Plurality, comparison and trade-offs

Value pluralism holds that more than one irreducible kind of value matters. It need not imply that every pair of options is incomparable. Ranking, comparison through a common measure, compensation for a loss, and practical permission to make a trade-off are distinct questions. Some accounts allow comparison without a common numerical unit; others identify cases where familiar comparative relations fail (Berlin 1969; Raz 1986; Chang 1997; SEP Value Pluralism; SEP Incommensurable Values).

Kant distinguishes price, which permits an equivalent, from dignity, which admits no equivalent. Anderson examines how different goods call for different modes of valuation and how market valuation can be inappropriate in some relations. These positions concern the standards governing valuation and exchange, including cases in which willingness to pay does not decide what should be done (Kant 1785, Section II; Anderson 1993).

### 3.7 Scheler and Hartmann on ordered values

Scheler treats value as objective and disclosed through intentional feeling, with a hierarchy of agreeable, vital, spiritual, and holy values. This feeling is directed towards value rather than being identical with bodily sensation. His account includes distorted valuation and the transformation of attention through love and openness. Hartmann also develops a differentiated order of values and distinguishes the height of a value from the strength of its demand. Ordered value consequently raises questions both of recognition and of what takes priority in action (Scheler 1913–16; Hartmann 1932; SEP Scheler).

### 3.8 Economic value and human development

Marx distinguishes use-value, the usefulness of a thing, from value in commodity production and its expression in exchange-value. Concrete useful labour produces particular use-values; abstract human labour supplies the account of commodity value, whose magnitude depends on socially necessary labour time. Nature contributes to material wealth, and some useful things have no value in this specific commodity sense. These distinctions prevent a simple identification of usefulness, labour expended by an individual, and exchange price (Marx 1867, ch. 1).

Marginal analysis examines the significance of an additional unit within particular conditions of choice. A thing's physical capacity alone does not determine its marginal benefit or exchange conditions. The capability approach further distinguishes resources and reported satisfaction from people's real opportunities to live and act. Sen and Nussbaum make this distinction central to evaluating development, while differing in how capabilities should be specified (Sen 1999; Nussbaum 2011; SEP Capability Approach).

### 3.9 Care, recognition and trust

Care ethics examines dependence, attentiveness, responsiveness, and the concrete relationship between one who cares and one who receives care. Recognition theory examines the social relations through which practical identity and self-realisation are sustained. These approaches bring the conditions of human development into the account of value; a requirement for social recognition need not make a person's basic worth depend on whether others currently recognise it (Noddings 1984; Honneth 1995; SEP Recognition).

Philosophical accounts of trust examine dependence under vulnerability, expectations of another, and the distinction between reliance and trustworthiness. They ask when trust is reasonable and what betrayal damages. Trust is therefore a normative relationship whose success cannot be identified simply with confidence or successful prediction (SEP Trust).

### 3.10 The range of the inquiry

These approaches offer different resources for explaining value's bearers, grounds, recognition, and practical force. A claim about the status of moral truth does not alone determine a theory of welfare; a theory of welfare does not alone determine just distribution. Western value theory makes these distinctions explicit and investigates how they can be joined (SEP Value Theory; SEP Well-Being).

## 4. The Natural and Social Sciences on Valuation

The natural and social sciences study how organisms learn, choose, cooperate, report well-being, and organise collective life. They investigate both evaluative processes and the conditions and consequences of conduct. Scientific findings can inform practical judgment when joined to stated purposes and normative commitments. Their evidential strength depends on the design, population, measures, and causal assumptions of each investigation.

### 4.1 Neural valuation and learning

Neuroscience distinguishes processes of representing options, learning from outcomes, and selecting actions. Research on value-based choice identifies neural systems through which different options can be compared for a decision. Dopamine reward-prediction-error research concerns differences between expected and received reward and their role in learning (Rangel, Camerer, and Montague 2008; Schultz 2016).

The neural comparison of options and the learning signals that update expectations perform different functions. A shared neural scale within a task concerns how a decision is implemented. It does not establish that every moral or welfare consideration has an objectively equivalent numerical amount. Moral decisions themselves recruit interacting systems whose contributions depend on the task (Crockett 2013).

### 4.2 Cooperation and moral regularities

Evolutionary explanations investigate how cooperation can develop under conditions such as repeated interaction and reciprocity. These mechanisms can explain patterns of behaviour without assuming that an organism formulates a moral principle (Trivers 1971).

A comparative study of ethnographic records from sixty societies found broadly positive moral evaluations of seven forms of cooperation, including helping kin, reciprocity, bravery, fair division, and respect for prior possession. The finding concerns coded evidence for these cooperative forms. It does not establish unanimous agreement about every action, equal coverage of every form in every society, or the correctness of every norm associated with cooperation (Curry, Mullins, and Whitehouse 2019).

### 4.3 Income, needs and well-being

Well-being research distinguishes judgments about one's life from reports of emotional experience. Kahneman and Deaton's 2010 United States data associated higher income with improved life evaluation while finding a plateau in their emotional well-being measure. Killingsworth's 2021 experience-sampling study found experienced well-being rising with income beyond the earlier threshold (Kahneman and Deaton 2010; Killingsworth 2021).

A 2023 joint reanalysis located the flattening pattern among the least happy participants, while other parts of the happiness distribution showed continued increases. Measurement and variation between people help explain the earlier disagreement. These associations do not establish a universal income threshold or a causal guarantee that raising income produces a particular gain in happiness (Killingsworth, Kahneman, and Mellers 2023).

Income and well-being also raise different questions at individual and national levels, and over different periods (Easterlin 1974). Self-determination theory studies autonomy, competence, and relatedness as basic psychological needs. Its findings support examining several conditions of well-being together; they do not by themselves establish a metaphysical classification of values or a theorem that no gain can compensate for any loss (Ryan and Deci 2000).

### 4.4 Trust and social outcomes

Economic and social research examines associations between generalised trust, institutions, investment, and growth. Such work makes the consequences of trust available for investigation, but a reported association requires further assumptions or research designs before it establishes a causal effect. The meaning of a survey response about trust also requires care when it is compared with a philosophical account of trustworthiness (Zak and Knack 2001).

### 4.5 Measuring welfare and development

Economic output measures important aspects of production without measuring every feature of a good life. Work on social progress distinguishes material living standards, health, education, relationships, political participation, environmental conditions, and insecurity. The choice of indicators and their aggregation requires explicit decisions about what the assessment is intended to represent (Stiglitz, Sen, and Fitoussi 2009).

### 4.6 Specified objectives and artificial intelligence

Artificial intelligence research documents cases in which an agent satisfies a specified objective through behaviour that defeats the designer's purpose. Specification gaming exposes a mismatch between an objective and the intended outcome. It motivates better specifications, oversight, and evaluation of effects beyond the rewarded signal (Amodei et al. 2016; Krakovna et al. 2020).

Such failure can occur even when only one underlying goal matters and its proxy is incomplete. It is therefore evidence about specification and optimisation, rather than an independent proof of value pluralism. Whether several goods are irreducible requires a further argument.

### 4.7 Evidence and normative assessment

Science can test claims about learning, health, provision, relationships, institutions, and the consequences of alternative practices. Those results can change what practical judgment recommends. Deciding that a particular outcome should govern judgment also requires an account of why it matters and how competing claims should be treated. Empirical investigation and normative reasoning can therefore inform one another while retaining distinct responsibilities.

## 5. Comparison of the Answers

The traditions differ both in what they call value and in what they ask an account of value to explain. Comparison is most useful when it follows specific questions about grounds, recognition, development, evidence, what endures or changes, and the scope of comparison. A single label such as objective, spiritual, or scientific leaves many of these differences unresolved.

### 5.2 Essentiality and normative authority

Madhyasth Darshan identifies value with essentiality and connects human essentiality with happiness and humane conduct. Its ground is the unit's essential participation in coexistence. This relational ground is independent of an observer's approval. It does not directly coincide with either the intrinsic–extrinsic distinction about grounds or the final–instrumental distinction about purposes (§3.1). Hume's challenge still asks what warrants the move from a description of participation to a requirement to participate in a particular way. The stated identity therefore calls for examination of both its meaning and its normative authority (§7.4).

Natural-law and realist approaches make this comparison less isolated than a contrast with subjectivism would suggest. The substantive questions are which account of human nature is correct, how its normative implications are justified, and whether it can address dependency, disagreement, and institutional failure.

### 5.3 Activity, properties and evaluative response

Madhyasth Darshan understands a unit as activity whose form, properties, essential nature, and *dharma* are distinguishable aspects of its participation. Value names the essentiality of that participation. Human recognition and evaluation are further activities of *jeevan*, through which a person can understand and fulfil what already has value. The ontological claim concerns what the unit is and how it participates; the structural claim specifies how the activities of *jeevan* constitute understanding, harmony, and fulfilment. Constitutional capacity, achieved harmony, and evidence of its achievement consequently have different places in the explanation (§§1.1–1.4).

Western theories often ask what properties make an activity, relationship, or outcome good. Such theories can recognise activities as bearers of value and allow relational grounds of goodness. The sharper disagreement concerns whether essentiality and the specified structure of *jeevan* explain what makes participation good and its evaluation correct. Fitting-attitude theories likewise distinguish actual approval from appropriate response. The comparison therefore turns on the standards of correctness and their warrant. Madhyasth Darshan's further claim is that awakened understanding is continuously expressed and evidenced through activity.

### 5.4 Establishment, expression and learning

The one-to-one relation between established values in *bal/sthiti* and expressed values in *gati* gives the darshan a specific account of inward acceptance and outward fulfilment as aspects of activity. Establishment concerns the understanding through which a relationship value can be continuously fulfilled. It does not create the essentiality of the other unit or make the relationship's value depend on the evaluator. A learner may fulfil a practical responsibility while still developing this understanding. Establishment involves the whole conscious unit: visualisation at *chitta* must accord with contemplation, understanding at *buddhi*, and realisation at *atma*. Practice, explanation, and self-evaluation can support development, while an outward performance supplies evidence to examine rather than a sufficient demonstration of the inner achievement.

Advaita likewise gives discipline and conduct a preparatory role in knowledge. Western accounts of character, care, and moral education also investigate changes in the evaluator. The distinctive comparison concerns what counts as establishment, how it is recognised, and what warrants continuity. An educational route beginning with meaningful outward participation remains an interpretation to test against the darshan's account of learning and evidence (§7.8).

### 5.5 Happiness, bliss and material provision

Madhyasth Darshan identifies happiness, peace, contentment, and bliss with harmonies between *mun–vritti*, *vritti–chitta*, *chitta–buddhi*, and *buddhi–atma*. These are constitutive relations between activities of one conscious unit. Their cumulative fulfilment in living is expressed through resolution; resolution and prosperity; these with fearlessness; and these with coexistence (§1.2). Advaita gives liberation a status distinct from produced conditions, while retaining duties and ethical preparation in empirical life. The disagreement concerns the final status of human participation and the relation between fulfilment and worldly order.

The four harmonies differ from the constructs measured as pleasure or life satisfaction. A report of feeling satisfied can be evidence concerning a person's experience, while the claim that a specified faculty harmony is present also requires examination of its constitutive content. Well-being research can investigate related experiences and conditions, but their correspondence requires operational definitions and evidence. Neither four named harmonies nor their cumulative fulfilment alone settles commensurability, compensation, or practical trade-offs (§7.1).

### 5.6 Ordering and value recognition

Scheler and Hartmann make objective value ordering a substantial position within Western philosophy. Madhyasth Darshan distinguishes several relations within its ordering (§1.6). Dedication concerns what serves what: usefulness serves need, need serves expressed value, expressed value serves established value, established value serves human value, and human value serves *jeevan* value (MAD, reader p. 34). Endurance supplies a higher–lower criterion concerning what lasts or remains non-transforming. Inclusion concerns what the more comprehensive contains. Commonness and completeness have a specific relational application: trust is the common relationship value and love the complete value (MAD, reader pp. 30, 43). These relations concern purpose, persistence, comprehensiveness, and relational fulfilment respectively; their application requires attention to the value class and relation being assessed.

This differentiation permits a more exact comparison with Western discussions of ranking, commensurability, and fitting valuation. The dedication of useful things to relationships supplies no numerical exchange rate between them. Inclusion cannot simply be read as permission to compensate damaged trust with additional output, and love's completeness does not name a commodity price. Endurance likewise needs an account of the domain in which it establishes priority. A comparison with Scheler's hierarchy and Hartmann's distinction between height and strength of demand should therefore examine how the respective criteria guide action. Mature recognition and a stated order still require worked procedures for disputed obligations (§§7.1, 7.7).

### 5.7 Useful things, labour and symbolic value

Madhyasth Darshan and Marx both distinguish usefulness from monetary appearance and give labour a central role in production and exchange. Their concepts of value differ. Marx analyses the social form and magnitude of commodity value; the darshan evaluates usefulness, aesthetic addition, labour, and symbolic value within an order of need, useful production, prosperity, and right-use (§1.5). A criticism of exploitation belongs to both accounts, although its explanation and proposed remedy differ.

Usefulness, realised benefit, labour assessment, and price answer distinguishable questions. Separating a thing's capacities from their deployment for a particular need may help investigate the difference between the darshan's constancy and temporality claims. This is an analytical proposal whose adequacy remains to be tested against the passages (§7.5). The capability approach further asks whether resources become real opportunities for differently situated people. Such distinctions expose concrete questions for a labour-valued exchange order without providing a ready-made reconciliation of its textual claims (§7.11).

### 5.8 Relationships, agreement and conflict

Justice in Madhyasth Darshan requires recognised responsibilities and actual fulfilment before mutual satisfaction. Care and recognition theories offer parallel questions about dependency, responsiveness, and practical standing.

The darshan holds that quarrelling involves error on at least one side and that correctly understanding parties do not quarrel (SB, p. 146). Applying this harmony claim to scarce resources, absent parties, and competing legitimate needs requires an account of the allocation to make (§7.7).

### 5.9 Qualitative evaluation in two applications

Consider a teacher helping a learner and a family producing food. Both are analytical illustrations of the value map. In teaching, guidance and care are established relationship values; human values such as generosity, kindness, grace, and compassion inform the provision of time, suitable content, and conditions for learning. The learner's developing understanding is an achievement whose evidence must be examined. The responsibility to guide endures, while the explanation, pace, and resources appropriate to its fulfilment change with the learner's receptivity (§§1.2–1.4).

Dedication relates useful teaching materials and expressed conduct to the relationship they serve and to the purpose of resolved living (§1.6). An enjoyable lesson or grateful response does not alone establish that guidance was fulfilled. Assessment should ask whether the learner can explain the meaning and apply it in a new situation, and whether the teacher's assistance met the actual difficulty. Such evidence can support a claim of learning while leaving continuity of understanding and wider awakening open to examination (§§1.4, 1.8).

In food production, usefulness concerns what the produce can fulfil in nourishment. Labour develops that usefulness through cultivation and preparation; aesthetic addition is meaningful where it adds convenience to usefulness. Labour assessment concerns exchange, while price records symbolic valuation. Quantities produced and prices received therefore require interpretation through assessed need, actual provision, and the responsibilities fulfilled, including care for those unable to produce (§§1.3, 1.5).

Dedication gives useful production its place in relationships and humane living. A harvest obtained through deception or degradation of soil can exhibit productive competence while leaving trust or responsibility for regeneration unfulfilled. Assessment must examine nourishment, labour, relationships, and continued productive conditions together. Need, harvest seasons, storage, and regeneration introduce temporal circumstances: what is available and how it should be used can change. Distinguishing these circumstances from the produce's capacities may help investigate the constancy and temporality claims about usefulness, but remains a proposed distinction whose textual adequacy requires examination (§§1.5–1.6, 7.5). Specifying sustainable yields and fair allocation still requires factual investigation and practical reasoning (§§7.9, 7.11).

## 6. Critical Review

### 6.1 Madhyasth Darshan — participation as the standard of value

**Strengths.**

- The account identifies value with the essentiality of active units and distinguishes this ground from its human recognition and fulfilment. Its objectivity claim thereby has definite content in participation, while allowing error in understanding and limitations in the means of fulfilment.
- The five-faculty account supplies constitutive relations for the four harmonies and connects established values with their expressions in conduct. It distinguishes constitutional capacity, understood fulfilment, and evidence of achievement, so attraction, skilled performance, and a claim of inward assurance can each be assessed in its proper role.
- Justice requires normative standards, actual fulfilment, and mutual satisfaction. Kindness, grace, and compassion address need and receptivity; labour evaluation, right-use, and rightful receiving address material conditions.
- The sixfold articulation of knowledge connects lawful operation and balance with justice, resolution, and truth. It allows technical effectiveness, relational fulfilment, and overall purpose to be examined together while preserving their different responsibilities.
- Self-examination and evidence in relationships and work supply criteria of evaluation. The texts acknowledge learning within a disordered tradition and distinguish reported achievements from a wider institutional order still to be realised.

**Weaknesses.**

- The identity of essentiality and value requires a defence that an interlocutor can assess without first accepting the whole ontology. It remains necessary to explain why a proposed form of participation has normative authority and how competing accounts can be adjudicated.
- Complete correctness is connected with awakening, while awakening is recognised through correct understanding and conduct. The account has educational entry points, but a public method for resolving disagreement about an individual's claimed establishment still needs specification.
- Dedication, endurance, inclusion, and completeness distinguish ways values are related, but they do not yet determine allocation in every case of scarce resources and legitimate competing claims. Their domains and practical implications need inspectable decisions without treating disagreement itself as proof of inferior standing.
- Reports of successful education and living are evidence to investigate, rather than independent demonstrations of universal efficacy. The claims concerning *jeevan*, continuity, and institutional scale need distinct forms of verification.
- Saturation and constitutional completeness are philosophical commitments whose relation to contemporary physical and biological explanations remains to be established. An activity model can clarify their connections but does not independently verify them or explain a transition to anubhav merely by naming a feedback process.

Madhyasth Darshan offers a connected theory of value in participation; its further development depends on publicly assessable warrants and worked applications of its own standards.

### 6.2 Advaita Vedanta — liberation and the limits of acquisition

**Strengths.**

- The distinction between liberation and produced attainments provides a sustained account of why acquisition and pleasurable experience may fail to answer the deepest question of fulfilment.
- Ethical preparation and action without attachment give the transformation of the evaluator a practical role. Teachings on compassion and *loka-sangraha* preserve responsibilities within empirical life.
- The examination of identification challenges the assumption that self-interest is adequately understood as the interests of a presently conceived individual ego.

**Weaknesses.**

- The account's ultimate explanation depends on non-duality and the authority of the means of knowledge through which it is disclosed. Those commitments require argument in a comparison that does not presuppose them.
- The relation between empirical responsibilities and ultimate non-doership needs careful interpretation when used to guide institutions, conflict resolution, or distribution. The cited teachings give ethical direction without supplying every institutional detail.
- The claim that produced goods cannot provide ultimate fulfilment does not alone determine their comparative importance within finite lives. Further practical reasoning is needed to assess particular relationships and material needs.

Advaita Vedanta provides a developed account of non-attached action and liberation whose social application must preserve the force of its empirical duties.

### 6.3 Western value theory — distinctions and competing explanations

**Strengths.**

- The distinctions between intrinsic and final value, welfare and moral rightness, ranking and commensurability, and actual and fitting attitudes prevent several different questions from being answered by one ambiguous use of value.
- Realist, constructivist, phenomenological, care, and capability approaches supply substantive alternatives to simple preference accounts. Evaluator development and the social conditions of fulfilment are well represented within this range.
- Arguments concerning dignity, exploitation, recognition, and capabilities make distribution and vulnerability central concerns rather than consequences to be considered only after an abstract theory is complete.

**Weaknesses.**

- Rival accounts disagree about the grounds of value and the standards of correct response. No single position can be assessed as the settled Western answer.
- The separation of metaethics, welfare, action, and political economy can leave difficult work in explaining how their results fit together. Conceptual clarity within one inquiry does not automatically produce a coherent account of living.
- Each proposal must show how its standards are known and applied. Appeals to appropriate attitudes, ideal reasoning, flourishing, or intuition relocate this evidential burden rather than removing it.

Western value theory supplies exact distinctions and substantial rival explanations that a comparative account must address individually.

### 6.4 The sciences — mechanisms, outcomes and explanatory limits

**Strengths.**

- Empirical methods make claims about learning, provision, health, trust, and institutions answerable to evidence beyond an advocate's testimony.
- The distinction between reported experience, life evaluation, behaviour, and measured conditions enables more discriminating tests of claims about well-being.
- Reanalysis and conflicting findings can improve an account, as the income–well-being debate illustrates. This capacity for correction is a resource for studying value education and institutional proposals.

**Weaknesses.**

- Operational measures can omit what a philosophical account regards as essential. Neither a reward signal nor a well-being questionnaire automatically measures established relationship values or awakened harmony.
- Associations do not establish causes, and findings in particular populations do not immediately establish universal regularities. Claims about education or prosperity need appropriate designs and limits.
- Empirical success in predicting a choice does not settle whether the choice is justified. Selecting and weighting the outcomes of an assessment still requires normative reasoning.

The sciences can test many consequences of a value theory while leaving the justification of its fundamental standards to a wider argument.

## 7. Open Problems

### 7.1 What ordering do the values permit?

The darshan's relations of dedication, endurance, inclusion, and common or complete value already provide distinct grounds for discussing order (§1.6). Dedication identifies a purpose served; endurance concerns persistence or non-transformation; inclusion concerns comprehensiveness; commonness and completeness characterise relationship values. The four harmonies also have cumulative fulfilment in living. The remaining question is what decision rules these relations support, individually and together. Their distinct domains must be preserved before they can guide an allocation or establish priority.

A gain in productive capacity alongside damaged trust is a useful test: what requires repair, how should resources support that repair, and what would count as restored fulfilment? Further cases should test whether endurance determines priority between competing needs, what inclusion entails for the fulfilment of an included value, and how love's completeness guides a particular responsibility. These questions concern the practical consequences of the stated relations; a numerical scale of exchange or a general rule of compensation cannot be assumed from their presence.

### 7.2 How can claimed awakening be assessed without circular authority?

Self-evaluation, examination of both sides of a relationship, study, and observable conduct are already available resources (MVD, p. 87; JV, pp. 98, 139). The accounts of *sanskar*, learning through meaningful participation, and study of meaning supply educational entry points (§1.8). They answer the strongest claim that no learning is possible before complete awakening.

The unresolved question concerns contested authority. When a teacher claims established understanding and a learner finds an apparent error, what shared procedure determines whether the difficulty lies in the teaching, the learner, or the application? The distinction between knowing the more developed and measuring it by an inadequate existing measure allows inquiry; it should not make a claim of development immune to correction (KD, p. 37). Documented explanations, outcomes, counterexamples, and the learner's freedom to question should be made part of a worked account.

The four harmonies have definite constitutive relations within the darshan (§1.2). Uncertainty about whether an observer has established their presence in a particular person concerns evidence. It does not make those relations indeterminate. Equally, a person's report of pleasure, calm, satisfaction, or bliss must be interpreted in context before it can establish the specified faculty harmony. A useful assessment should examine the person's reasons, freedom from contradiction in the relevant activity, fulfilled responsibilities, and continuity across circumstances, while keeping ordinary self-description distinct from the full claim of realised understanding.

### 7.3 How does mutual satisfaction include everyone affected?

Justice requires recognised relationships, value-fulfilment, evaluation, and mutual satisfaction. It already asks more than whether two people consent. A further account should show how to assess relationships where one person cannot communicate a judgment, lacks relevant information, or depends on the evaluator for care.

The appropriate tests include a young child, an adult requiring continuing support, and an agreement whose effects fall on absent neighbours. Actual provision and fulfilled responsibilities must matter alongside expressed assent. Satisfaction within the immediate pair cannot alone establish that obligations to other affected people have been fulfilled (§1.4).

### 7.4 What warrants the identification of essentiality and value?

The texts explicitly identify essentiality, essential nature, and value, and connect human *dharma* with happiness and humane participation (MVD, pp. 47, 124–125; SB, pp. 50, 87). The question is therefore the warrant for a stated identification, rather than the recovery of a missing definition.

A further argument should explain how essential participation is distinguished from a merely recurrent behaviour and why that participation provides a reason for action. It should also explain what would count against an asserted identification. Otherwise a dispute about the good can be redescribed as a dispute about essentiality without making the original disagreement easier to resolve.

The effort–motion–result account makes this question sharper. Effective power and persistent activity occur in both humane and inhumane conduct. The further warrant must concern the character and fulfilment of that activity, rather than its efficacy alone. Likewise, natural lawfulness and balance cannot establish a particular human obligation without the intervening account of relationships, knowledge, and humane purpose (§§1.1, 1.8).

### 7.5 In what sense is usefulness constant?

The primary passages contain a difference that a complete account must address. Usefulness is described as definite through the example of a kilogram of wheat across a hundred years, together with a claim that interference can reduce its value (JV, p. 123). Object value is also called constant through the sesame example (JV, pp. 137–138). Usefulness and beauty are expressly called temporal (*samayik*) in relation to changing needs, use, exchange, and deployment, and contrasted with enduring established, human, and *jeevan* values (MAD, reader p. 51). Both claims are stated in §1.5. The task is first textual: identify the sense and conditions of each claim before proposing their reconciliation.

Distinguishing a thing's capacities, its transformation through labour or interference, the benefit realised in a situation, and its price may help analyse the difference. A quantity of grain can retain a nutritive capacity while its condition, access, and the need it serves change. These distinctions are analytical proposals; the cited passages do not expressly supply them as their reconciliation. A fuller treatment should test the proposed reading against both passages and further uses of the terms, including what remains constant when interference reduces usefulness.

### 7.6 How complete are the value lists and pairings?

The nine principal established relationship values and their nine corresponding expressions have explicit primary support in MAD reader p. 30. The principal classification need not remain uncertain because JV p. 108 introduces its list with illustrative wording. The remaining work concerns the relation of this classification to other contextual lists and to the larger activity enumeration.

The 122 activity members do not establish 122 distinct names or 122 separate relationship values. A concordance should preserve the difference between a value class, an activity position, a paired expression, and evidence in conduct. Repeated terms such as generosity and contextual uses of happiness or bliss require definitions and context. A named position at one faculty also cannot be read as a claim that understanding the corresponding value needs that faculty alone (§§1.2–1.3).

### 7.7 How are rival legitimate obligations resolved?

The claim that correct parties do not quarrel supplies the darshan's position on harmony (SB, p. 146). Recognition, mutual fulfilment, and common resolution are its proposed direction. The difficult case is a conflict between legitimate needs when existing means are insufficient: two patients need the only available treatment, or protecting one community shifts a burden to another.

A worked account should identify relevant facts, responsibilities, available alternatives, and the reason for its allocation. It should distinguish a remediable error from a material limit that remains after misunderstanding is corrected. This would show how the harmony claim guides action and how unavoidable loss is acknowledged, rather than treating the existence of disagreement as a completed diagnosis.

### 7.8 How does outward learning become establishment and continuity?

The texts connect *sanskar*, understood meaning, practice, self-evaluation, and the acceptance of evidenced conduct with further expression (MVD, pp. 83, 90, 94; KD, pp. 84, 112–113). They distinguish intermittent meaningful conduct from continuity. These resources support a positive educational account across the activities of *jeevan*, including the learner's visualisation, desire, understanding, and resolve (§1.8).

The further task is to document the transition. Constitutional completeness identifies an inherent capacity; establishment concerns understood value in state; fulfilment expresses it through activity; continuity concerns its sustained evidence across circumstances. What differentiates instructed performance, independently understood fulfilment, and such continuity? How are relapse, conflicting incentives, and mistaken self-assessment recognised? An educational route beginning with outward participation should be examined as a pedagogical interpretation, without presenting it as a prescribed sequence or repetition as a sufficient cause of awakening. Evidence should include the learner's explanation and self-examination as well as conduct and its effects, with each claim assessed against the faculty activity or relationship it concerns.

The continuity from action through evaluation to revised understanding also requires discrimination between several outcomes. A learner can correct a technique, recognise a responsibility, clarify a concept, or claim *anubhav*; each calls for appropriate evidence. An account of qualitative refinement should explain how such changes affect subsequent participation. It should preserve the distinction between a definite constitutive relation of harmony and the process through which that harmony is achieved and evidenced in a particular person.

### 7.9 How does value across the four orders guide ecological decisions?

The darshan attributes value to every unit, directs humans towards nurture, and relates resource use to regeneration. It also describes vitalising and devitalising effects in relation to living bodies, including human bodies (SB, pp. 141, 179–182, 200–201). These commitments cannot be reduced either to unrestricted human preference or to a fully specified theory of non-human rights.

Further work should examine habitat loss, competing forms of life, restoration times, and effects on future people. The account must explain how participation and regeneration are assessed when there is no single immediate beneficiary and when one useful activity disrupts another form of coexistence.

### 7.10 What evidence supports the educational and institutional claims?

Self-examination, fulfilled relationships, useful work, and reported educational results supply different kinds of evidence. Nagraj's accounts include learning and self-experimentation, while the discussion of exchange in *Adhyatmvad* distinguishes reported achievements in production, prosperity, and justice from the wider exchange order still awaited at the time of writing (JV, pp. 98, 139; SB, p. 294; AVD, p. 52).

These historical reports should not be presented as proof that the full institutional order was implemented or as a statement of its present reach. Longitudinal studies could examine learning, sustained conduct, provision, and relationships, with explicit measures and comparison conditions. Institutional evaluation should separately examine distribution, ecological effects, dependency, and the ability to correct failures. Such research would test specified claims without assuming that a questionnaire exhausts *jeevan* or that inward evaluation forbids external investigation.

### 7.11 How should labour be evaluated and provision distributed?

Labour deployment, labour-value assessment, protection from exploitation, and profit-and-loss-free exchange are explicit commitments (MVD, pp. 260, 268–269; SB, p. 237). The next questions concern their implementation: how should differences of skill, effort, training, risk, tools, time, quality, and productivity enter an assessment? What happens when socially necessary care yields no exchangeable product, or when a useful product depends on accumulated knowledge contributed by many people?

Remuneration, gift, and award already widen the account of rightful wealth beyond an individual's immediate production (AVD, p. 170). A developed allocation procedure should explain provision for children, older people, people with disabilities, and anyone temporarily unable to work. It should also distinguish the evaluation of labour from the urgency of need and from the basic standing of the person who receives support.

### 7.12 How are equal human standing and unequal development related?

The account recognises common human purpose and capacity while distinguishing levels of understanding and conduct. Developmental differences can matter when assigning a task or assessing competence. They do not by themselves justify differences in basic worth, protection, or the right to question a judgment.

A full treatment should show how conferred rights and duties are applied to less experienced learners and people whose capacities differ. It should make clear how recognition and care protect a person's standing while their understanding or productive ability is still developing. This is especially important wherever claims of higher value or awakening carry authority within a family, educational setting, or institution.

## 8. Conclusion

Madhyasth Darshan identifies value with essentiality in coexistence. Units are activity, and their form, properties, essential nature, and *dharma* concern aspects of that participation. A unit's value is present through its essentiality; human recognition can understand it correctly or mistake it. Awakening concerns the development of understanding and fulfilment in the active conscious unit, *jeevan*.

The structural account specifies what this fulfilment comprises. Happiness, peace, contentment, and bliss are harmonies between defined faculty activities. Established values in state have corresponding expressions in motion, while justice joins recognition of relationships, value-fulfilment, evaluation, and mutual satisfaction. Constitutional capacity, achieved harmony, and evidence of attainment remain distinct. Their distinction allows education to acknowledge meaningful participation while examining the understanding and continuity it demonstrates.

The value classes connect these harmonies with human conduct, relationships, useful things, aesthetic addition, and symbolic valuation. Their ordering involves dedication, endurance, inclusion, and common or complete relationship value, each with its own domain. Evaluation must identify which value is at issue, what fulfilment it calls for, and in what sense time or comparison applies. The difference between the texts' constancy and temporality claims about usefulness remains a question for further collation and argument.

Advaita, Western theories, and the sciences sharpen the questions of normative warrant, disputed authority, dependency, allocation, and public evidence. The darshan's contribution is a connected account of value's ground, its constitution in activity, and its fulfilment in living. Further work must show how its distinct ordering relations guide difficult decisions and how claims of established understanding are assessed through explanation, conduct, and sustained participation.

## Appendix: Structural formalisation of value

### A.1 Harmony as a constitutive relation

The following notation makes two relations in §§1.2–1.4 explicit: what constitutes a named *jeevan* value, and what fulfils value in a relationship. The definitions come from the primary texts; the predicates and their logical organisation are an analytical reconstruction (MVD, pp. 327–328, 331, 333, 346; JV, p. 138; MAD, reader p. 47).

Let $j$ denote a *jeevan* in its current activity, and $m,v,c,b,a$ its faculties *mun*, *vritti*, *chitta*, *buddhi*, and *atma*. Write $H_{xy}(j)$ for the source-defined harmony between faculties $x$ and $y$, including its understood content. Thus *vritti–chitta* harmony requires resolved, just thought and desire in accord. Agreement between mistaken beliefs is insufficient. The four naming relations are:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{Sukh}(j) &\equiv H_{mv}(j), & \mathrm{Shanti}(j) &\equiv H_{vc}(j), \\
\mathrm{Santosh}(j) &\equiv H_{cb}(j), & \mathrm{Anand}(j) &\equiv H_{ba}(j).
\end{aligned}
$$

Here $\equiv$ records a constitutive identification: the named value is the harmony lived and felt in these activities. Structural definiteness concerns what the value consists in. Whether a particular person presently fulfils that relation is a further question, and what evidence establishes that fulfilment is a third. The constitution of *jeevan* supplies the capacities whose coordinated, awakened activity fulfils the harmonies (§1.2).

Write $A_{\Sigma}(j)$ for realisation of coexistence in *atma*, where $\Sigma$ denotes *satta*. Ultimate bliss, *paramanand*, belongs to this realisation and its evidence (MVD, pp. 327–328). Eternal saturation and concurrence with *satta* concern the ontological relation already present; $A_{\Sigma}(j)$ concerns its realisation in awakened activity. Evaluation and feeling belong to the sentient unit saturated in actionless *chetana* (MVD, pp. 35, 74–75; SB, pp. 63–64). This distinction keeps inherent capacity, actual awakening, and the reality realised separately identifiable within their connection.

### A.2 Definite expectations and evaluated fulfilment

Let $r$ denote an actual relationship between participating persons and $N_r$ its value requirements towards completeness. These requirements are grounded in the relationship and its established values. A participant can desire something that misrepresents them. The resources, abilities, and concrete acts needed for fulfilment depend on circumstances. In a voluntary contact, the specified expectations also enter the assessment and remain answerable to justice (MVD, pp. 61–62; MAD, reader p. 51).

Four predicates preserve the stated structure of justice:

| Predicate | Meaning in an instance of participation |
|---|---|
| $R_r$ | The relationship and its value requirements are correctly recognised. |
| $F_r$ | Actual participation fulfils those requirements through appropriate acts and means. |
| $E_r$ | Evaluation correctly determines what has been fulfilled, examining both sides. |
| $M_r$ | Mutual satisfaction is present in the recognised and evaluated fulfilment. |

The complete criterion is represented as:

$$
J_r:=R_r\land F_r\land E_r\land M_r.
$$

The conjunction records the four requirements together. Their practical order remains recognition, fulfilment, evaluation, and mutual satisfaction. Deliberation before action assesses the responsibility, available means, and proposed contribution; evaluation of accomplished participation examines actual fulfilment (MVD, pp. 311, 336; JV, pp. 98, 138–139). A coherent plan may correctly identify the responsibility while $F_r$ remains unfulfilled. Reported satisfaction likewise needs examination alongside recognition, conduct, and its result.

The same *jeevan* recognises, evaluates, and tastes value. Inner harmony and fulfilled mutuality are therefore connected through the activity of the participating persons. The texts place happiness, peace, contentment, and bliss in the experience of established values (JV, pp. 138–139; MAD, reader p. 51). One fulfilled relationship provides evidence about that participation; continuity and comprehensive awakening require evidence across the wider field of living. The criterion keeps the understood basis, the contribution actually made, and its evaluated fulfilment available for separate examination.

### A.3 Orderliness, continuity and evidence

Fulfilled value extends into work and social participation. Law, regulation, and balance concern the organisation of means; justice, *dharma*, and truth concern recognised relationships, resolved purpose, and realised understanding (MVD, pp. 80, 137, 156, 174). Inner harmony has its evidence in resolution, family prosperity, social fearlessness, and participation in universal orderliness (JV, pp. 53, 62, 140; AVD, p. 228; KD, pp. 112–113). Each field calls for its own examination: a person's assurance, a family's adequate provision, and society-wide fearlessness make claims of different scope.

Evaluation can clarify the basis of subsequent participation. An unfulfilled responsibility may disclose mistaken recognition, inadequate means, or a conflict requiring resolution; successful participation may strengthen an understood acceptance (§§1.4, 1.8). These changes concern understanding and conduct within the constitutionally complete *jeevan*. The notation specifies the content that development must fulfil. Explaining how learning proceeds, how long it takes, and how realisation occurs requires a further account of development (§7.8).

Evidence about a harmony also differs from the harmony itself. An observer may have incomplete evidence about $H_{xy}(j)$ while its meaning remains definite. A record can mark a claim as supported, contradicted, or not yet established; these describe the assessment of a claim. First-person examination, explanations, actual conduct, fulfilled responsibilities, and continuity across relevant circumstances contribute distinct evidence. Examining them together connects the definite account of value with the assessment of particular persons and practices (§§7.2, 7.8, 7.10).

## Editorial Notes

### Texts, translations and page conventions

This revision uses a targeted comparison of the primary passages cited below, including Hindi where English wording or pagination changes the argument. It is not a complete collation of Nagraj's corpus. MVD, SB, JV, and AVD page numbers denote PDF positions in the linked files; MVD's visible page footer agrees with that convention. In JV the printed number is one lower than the PDF position. SB's retained contents pagination differs from the bilingual PDF pagination.

KD citations use printed body pages and chapter numbers where specified. The Hindi body page is twenty-five below its PDF position. The working translation's `[p. N]` markers introduce the page. Front-matter numbering must be identified separately because it overlaps with body numbering. KD is a working English translation, and AVD is also provisional; neither status is removed by its use here. Important activity and continuity passages in KD pp. 37, 84, and 112–113 were checked against the Hindi page images. MAD denotes the Hindi *Manav Abhyas Darshan* in the linked native reader. Its references use the displayed page numbers in the 222-page version, not PDF positions or assumed printed-page offsets. The English formulations here paraphrase the Hindi; they are not attributed to an English edition.

### Essentiality, activity and the enumeration

The identification of essentiality with value and its connection with essential nature and *dharma* are explicit in MVD pp. 47, 124–125, SB pp. 50, 87, and AVD p. 184. The exposition retains this relation without adding a mechanism in which form and properties must first align with the other aspects to produce value. The map in §1.3 organises the principal uses of *mulya* relevant to human living. It separates usefulness and aesthetic value for explanation and includes symbolic value for comparison with them; MVD p. 306 groups usefulness and aesthetics together, while symbolic value is named separately at p. 183. The map is not an assertion that the corpus supplies seven mutually exclusive classes.

MVD p. 203 uses *bhav-kriya* broadly for values and evaluation. MVD p. 338 and AVD p. 92 also identify a particular *bhav/samveg* pair at *vritti*. *Bhav* is defined through essentiality, value, responsibility, and participation; *samveg* is the impetus associated with meaningful union, whose direction can be towards advancement or decline (MVD, pp. 43, 338). The shared term does not make all value-activity one position at *vritti*, nor does the pairing establish a universal causal sequence from sentiment to every outward action.

The awakened enumeration has 122 activity members in 61 pairs: two at *atma*, four at *buddhi*, sixteen at *chitta*, thirty-six at *vritti*, and sixty-four at *mun* (MVD, p. 323; AVD, p. 91). This counts activity positions, not 122 distinct names or 122 relationship values. The nine established–expressed pairs in §1.3 are a specified part of the account; the enumeration does not classify every activity pair as such a relationship-value pair.

### Established and expressed values

The exposition adopts the one-to-one correspondence of established values in *bal/sthiti* and expressed, civic, or *shishta* values in *gati*. The table retains the paired positions in AVD pp. 91–94 and the definitions in MVD pp. 328–348. The educational formulation that outward expressions can be learnt before establishment in state is an interpretive articulation of the learning route, read with the distinction of state and motion and the accounts of education and evidenced conduct (KD, pp. 18–19, 84, 112–113; SB, pp. 237, 294). It is not presented as a newly located verbatim sentence or a necessary sequence for every learner.

MAD reader p. 30 explicitly identifies the principal nine established values, their corresponding nine expressions, trust as common value, and love as complete value. This supplies direct classification evidence beyond JV p. 108, which introduces the nine relationship values as examples. KD p. 26 uses an illustrative list including *daya*, *sneha*, *udarta*, *gaurav*, *adar*, *vatsalya*, *shraddha*, *prem*, and *kritagyata*. Six Hindi terms coincide directly with the JV list; *adar* and *samman* should not silently be counted as identical. The principal nine-value classification is retained; the illustrative KD list is not silently substituted for it or treated as proof that the principal taxonomy is uncertain. This does not assert that every occurrence of *mulya* throughout the corpus belongs to those nine values. Naturalness, concordance, humility, and devoutness are running English terms for the relevant expressed values; ordinary English associations do not exhaust their source definitions.

### Activity, saturation and the four aspects

The effort–motion–result alignment follows SB pp. 60–62. The text places effort/strength with dharma and svabhava, motion with svabhava and guna, and result with roop and amaratva. The compressed account in §1.1 concerns aspects of the active unit; *amaratva* is retained here with its surrounding constitutional-completeness context, rather than generalised into the immortality of every present material configuration. The overlap of svabhava means this is not a one-to-one derivation or a three-stage chronology. No physical potential, numerical force law, or transition equation is inferred from it.

The account of relative power and characteristic participation draws on [*Technical Note: Four Aspects of the Active Unit*](../The-Ontology-of-Coexistence/Technical-Note-Roop-Guna-Svabhava-Dharma.pdf), in its discussions of unit activity, the four aspects, order-wise examples, and the limits of a process interpretation. Its rendering of usefulness as functional significance is analytical and does not make every actual effect morally good. The interpretation of dharma as order-specific innateness must distinguish change within an order from a transition between orders. The human case also distinguishes the constitution of jeevan from its current understanding and conduct.

### Chetana, chaitanya and knowledge

MVD p. 35 names knowledge as chetana in relation to chaitanya; pp. 74–75 distinguish actionless, non-transforming consciousness/knowledge from transformations in nature and identify chaitanya as constitutionally complete jeevan. The exposition does not treat satta as becoming a unit, performing evaluation, or receiving anubhav. Knowledge as omnipresent ground and knowledge realised and evidenced in human living are connected uses, not an additional substance and its transmission. Sentience includes evaluation but is not exhaustively defined by it, and constitutional completeness does not guarantee awakened evaluation. SB pp. 63–64 and 249 supply the distinctions between natural mutuality, sentient capacities, and correct human recognition and fulfilment.

### The faculties, qualitative refinement and evidence

The five-faculty table follows KD §3.8, p. 84's Hindi account of *sthiti/gati*. In AVD pp. 91–94 the English strength/power headings are reversed relative to the Hindi; §1.2 follows the checked Hindi relation. *Chitta* names the faculty, *chitran* its visualising activity, and *chintan* its contemplative activity. The integrated exposition describes dependencies of meaning and expression; it does not posit a neural mechanism or a fixed temporal passage through five successive compartments.

MVD p. 83 presents interdependent relations among language, meaning, evaluation, worldview, and the perceiver's capacities, returning to language. Words communicate meanings concerning existing realities; what the person apprehends depends on view, evaluation, capacity, ability, and receptivity. These relations concern activities of *chitta*, whose adequacy depends on understanding. Study of meaning can reach understanding and realisation and become available for renewed expression (MVD, p. 83; KD, pp. 112–113). This is a cycle of apprehension and communication, not a linear causal sequence that generates the value of the indicated unit. Constitutional completeness identifies the capacity of *jeevan*; it does not establish that a present evaluation is correct.

The realisation–behaviour–application evidences (MVD, p. 12) and the knowledge–law–regulation–balance–justice–dharma–truth passage (p. 174) connect understanding and living without making their terms interchangeable outside that argument. MAD reader p. 45 grounds the account of *aapyayan* and *parimarjan*; neither is equated with memorisation or habit formation alone.

MVD pp. 99–100 distinguish realisation-oriented understanding in study from realisation-based understanding and its expression. Page 156 connects atma with realisation, buddhi–chitta with resolution, and vritti–mun with just behaviour. These relations are not exclusive ownership of justice, dharma, or truth by individual faculties: all three are accepted in buddhi and participate in contemplation and deliberation. The eight dimensions of visualisation on p. 327 describe an activity of chitta, not the production of the imaged unit by a perceiver.

### Sixfold knowledge and continuing evaluation

The continuity of knowledge, law, regulation, balance, justice, dharma, and truth is directly stated in MVD p. 174, with evidence of all six through realisation on p. 80. The natural-order conformance descriptions follow SB pp. 236–237. Their particular illustrations do not allocate each term exclusively to one order. Dharma as innateness and dharma as the humane perspective of resolution in thought are related uses whose distinctions remain necessary (MVD, pp. 125, 137, 156, 174).

[*A State-Dynamic Model of Coexistence*](../A-State-Dynamic-Model-Of-Coexistence/A-State-Dynamic-Model-Of-Coexistence.pdf), §§1–3, 7–8, and 10–11, develops the analytical connection between unit activity, human orientation, consequences, and later evaluation.

The present paper uses that connection to clarify continuing evaluation and learning in §§1.4 and 1.8. The model's distinction of actual and operatively recognised mutuality and its grouping of consequences into internal, bodily, relational, and natural domains are analytical reconstructions. They do not enlarge configurational parinam into a source-stated definition of every human consequence, or make feedback a sufficient cause of anubhav. The applications in §5.9 use the forms of value, dedication, and temporal distinctions developed in this study.

The model's grouping into two coupled triads and its projections of niyam–nyaya, niyantran–dharma, and santulan–satya are likewise analytical, non-exclusive mappings. They are not adopted as primary-text identities or faculty assignments. A family or institution is an arrangement of persons, relationships, and work; the evaluators are the participating human beings. No collective jeevan or new ontological unit is inferred from social orderliness.

### Harmonies and contextual uses of jeevan values

The faculty-harmony account in JV p. 138 and MVD p. 327, and the cumulative fulfilment in KD §3.12, p. 112, supply the two columns of the harmony table. The social column preserves the cumulative sequence; it does not assign fearlessness or coexistence alone as an exclusive outward equivalent. Their distinctness supports separate examination, but does not alone prove the impossibility of every aggregate assessment. The formal appendix keeps the named relations distinct because the cited texts supply no numerical scale or exchange rates.

The scope of fulfilment described in §§1.2 and A.3 connects inner satisfaction with self-governed conduct in relationships and wider orderliness (JV, pp. 53, 62, 140; AVD, p. 228; KD, pp. 112–113). This connection does not replace the cumulative fulfilment table with four exclusive correspondences or make a family or society the bearer of a collective *jeevan*.

MVD p. 328 locates the named *anand* activity at *buddhi*, while p. 323 also uses bliss language at *atma*. The table follows the specific named positions rather than asserting an exclusive location for every use of bliss. KD p. 37 also speaks of resolution and prosperity as *jeevan* values in its context. The fourfold list is retained where the four harmonies are the subject; contextual lists are not silently expanded or reduced to force one enumeration. Contentment in MVD p. 331 includes readiness for production beyond need as well as absence of deficiency-sentiment.

MAD reader p. 47 explicitly equates absence of opposition in each adjacent faculty pair with a named value and with a quality of behaviour, thought, desire, or understanding. This strengthens the constitutive reading adopted in §§1.2 and A.1. Its *aparadh-hin* is rendered behaviour free of wrongdoing; it is not confined to compliance with a prevailing penal code. Page 51 connects the four harmonies with the experience of established values and describes established values as enduring across time and place. The study's term *structural* names these definite relations of activity, rather than a claim about measured anatomy or a new spatial arrangement of the *jeevan* atom.

MVD pp. 343 and 346 distinguish sensory pleasantness from happiness defined as resolution. The pedagogical progression from pleasant contact to resolved thought, an understood whole, and realised existence is therefore developed through the specified faculty relations, not adopted as four independent satisfactions. Absence of confusion alone is insufficient if the person's beliefs misrecognise reality; merely drawing a comprehensive image is insufficient for *chitta–buddhi* harmony. The eight dimensions of visualisation and the definition of contentment have practical implications for planning; a successful plan alone is not proof of *santosh*. Feeling the four harmonies is attributed to *jeevan*, while their named positions remain distinct; the *asvadan* activity at *mun* is not made the exclusive location of all four.

The appendix's harmony predicates, relationship requirements, and conjunction for justice are analytical notation. Their substantive content comes from the cited definitions, not from the symbols themselves. They distinguish constitutive definiteness, an actual person's state, and an observer's evidence. No stochastic rule, numerical utility function, automatic passage from logical consistency to fulfilment, or deterministic law of awakening is attributed to the primary texts.

### Evaluation errors and non-attachment

The Hindi sentence on correct evaluation in MVD p. 203 names under- and over-evaluation, whereas its English adds mis-evaluation. The triadic account in §1.7 is therefore cited to MVD pp. 152, 306 and SB p. 146. The illustrations in §1.7 come from SB PDF p. 146, which gives both the rope–snake and iron–gold comparisons in Hindi and English. MVD p. 306 prints *nirmulyan* (निर्मूल्यन); occurrences of *amulyan* elsewhere should retain their own source wording. MVD pp. 198–199 distinguishes *ativyapti* as more, *avyapti* as other than, and *anavyapti* as less. English list order is not a safe basis for matching these terms.

The headword translated as dispassion in MVD p. 152 is *nirakarshan* (निराकर्षण). The exposition uses that headword. It should not be substituted for *vairagya* in Advaita or for KD's distinct uses of *virag*, *vairagya*, and *para-vairagya*. The related discussion of non-doership concerns wrongful action, while KD p. 37 describes completeness as continuity of activity.

### Human development and relational fulfilment

MVD pp. 60–61 fixes the humane and higher-humane triads and the conditions distinguishing kindness, grace, and compassion. SB p. 180 reverses kindness and compassion in the English ordering relative to its Hindi; the exposition follows the checked definitions. SB p. 189 defines *heenta* through deceit, fraud, duplicity, and hypocrisy. That evaluative term should not be read as a person's disability or lower productive capacity.

The three motives in MVD p. 319 are *eshanas*, not the four *purusharthas* of the Advaita comparison. MVD p. 250's account of sibling evaluation concerns a pre-adolescent child in an awakened family; it does not establish a universal first onset of evaluation.

The relationship names in MVD p. 190 and the purposes in JV pp. 108–109 are retained without inventing a seven-to-six one-to-one purpose table. In particular, the orderliness relationship is not defined here as a relationship with an abstract entity, nor is master–associate reduced without argument to a modern employment contract. SB p. 237 compresses the justice sequence; MVD pp. 311, 336 supplies the four-part formulation used in §1.4.

### Ordering, production and temporal claims

The relations distinguished in §1.6 come from different arguments. MAD reader p. 34 supplies the dedication of usefulness to need, need to expressed value, expressed to established value, established to human value, and human to *jeevan* value. The source explicitly names *avashyakta mulya*. Treating need through the assessment connecting useful means and fulfilled values is this study's organising choice; its omission as a separate row does not deny that terminology. The usefulness comparison in §1.3 likewise concerns adequacy for a specified need, rather than asserting a numerical measure of essentiality or a rule for comparison across every class. MVD p. 43 defines *guru mulyan* through long-lasting result or non-transformability. Its inclusion claim at p. 47 concerns the four orders, while pp. 219–222 apply higher and lower value in the account of social participation and followership. MAD reader pp. 30 and 43 identify trust as common value and love as complete value within relationships. These passages warrant distinguishable relations; they do not by themselves supply a single numerical scale, an exchange rate between classes, or a universal inclusion theorem for every row in §1.3. MVD pp. 58 and 67 use higher-for-lower exchange to define acquisitive profit, whose direction must remain distinct from dedication and advancement.

JV p. 123 calls the usefulness of material objects definite and compares a kilogram of wheat with its counterpart a century earlier, while adding that interference has brought its value down. The sesame example crosses PDF pp. 137–138, calls object value constant, and explicitly identifies it as usefulness value. The alignment in §1.2 and the glossary follows that passage without adding a class to the map. MAD reader p. 51 calls usefulness and beauty temporal, grounding the claim in the temporality of needs, use, exchange, and deployment. §1.5 presents the claims separately and §7.5 examines the textual problem. Describing the first claim as constancy of a kind, or distinguishing inherent capacity from situated use, is a possible interpretation; neither is a reconciliation expressly supplied by these passages. The temporality question also distinguishes a value's definite content from a person's present recognition and from the changing means and occasions of its fulfilment.

MVD p. 56's endeavour-until-success definition is *prayog*, translated there as experimentation and rendered application here; the following definition concerns production. SB p. 237's Hindi differentiates evaluation in justice, labour-value assessment in exchange, and labour deployment in production; its English does not fully reproduce that paragraph.

The extended fear-and-temptation discussion is SB chapter 9, item 11, PDF pp. 290–295, ending before item 12 on p. 295. It is not chapter 11 beginning at PDF p. 199. Its political and educational claims, like MVD p. 199's account of sentient atoms, are treated as the darshan's positions rather than independently verified scientific or historical facts. AVD p. 52 is a dated report distinguishing experienced production and justice from the wider exchange system then still awaited.

### Comparative terms and verification

Value in a theory of normative properties, value as essentiality, economic commodity value, and a neural decision variable are not interchangeable senses. The comparisons examine relations between the accounts without treating a shared English word as proof of a shared concept.

BG 3.8 concerns prescribed action and bodily maintenance; 3.19 supplies the explicit instruction to act without attachment. The *Taittiriya* bliss teaching is cited to 2.7–2.9 as well as the preceding inward inquiry, rather than identifying the *anandamaya* sheath with the absolute without qualification. The *Vivekachudamani* is used as a pedagogical witness traditionally attributed to Shankara, with its disputed authorship retained.

The scientific discussion separates reward prediction error from decision valuation, cross-cultural regularity from exceptionless moral agreement, association from causation, and proxy failure from a proof of irreducible plurality. The 2010 income finding is read with the 2021 study and 2023 reanalysis. The critical questions concern the passages and proposals examined here; they do not assert that the wider Madhyasth Darshan literature contains no further treatment of education, constitution, economics, or institutional practice.
