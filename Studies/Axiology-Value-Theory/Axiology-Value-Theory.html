Axiology: Value Theory

Author: AnalyticMadhyasthDarshan.org — a group of people studying philosophy. Source repository: raghavamohan/AnalyticMadhyasthDarshan.

Edited on: September 5, 2026, 9:44 AM IST

The question: What is a value, where does value reside, how is it recognised and fulfilled, and what distinguishes correct evaluation from error? How does the Co-existentialism of Shri A. Nagraj compare with Advaita Vedanta, modern Western value theory, and the natural and social sciences?

This study examines value, evaluation, and the ordering of values in Madhyasth Darshan (Co-existentialism), as presented by Shri A. Nagraj. Its central problem is the relation between the value of a unit in coexistence, the activities through which recognises and evaluates value, and the conduct through which human beings fulfil it. Objectivity, evaluative error, education, useful production, and just relationships all depend on how these relations are understood.

The Ontology of Coexistence supplies the account of Omnipresence, units, form, properties, essential nature, , the four orders, and jeevan. The Epistemology of Coexistence examines knowing, believing, recognising, fulfilling, and evaluating. The present study asks what makes something valuable, how an evaluator can mistake it, and how understanding becomes reliable participation. The distinction between a value established in state and its outward expression makes the problem of learning especially important: meaningful conduct, understanding of its value, and continuity of fulfilment must be examined together.

Ethics, aesthetics, prosperity, family relationships, and education develop particular applications of this account. Axiology must nevertheless explain their common basis. It cannot leave labour, material provision, or educational practice outside its argument when fulfilment and evaluation depend on them. The comparison with Advaita, Western philosophy, and science tests both this explanatory reach and the warrant for its claims.

Standpoint and scope

These studies are written from the standpoint of a scientist and technologist — someone trained to graduate-level physics and mathematics, at home with contemporary cosmology, quantum theory, conservation laws, and the logic of formal models.

From that background, value can appear to be something minds add to an otherwise valueless world: a preference, a reward signal, or a social convention. Scientific accounts of mechanism and measurement do not by themselves settle whether this appearance is correct. The status of well-being, the passage from factual description to obligation, and the possibility of goods independent of actual preference remain questions for investigation.

This paper reads the primary texts, states the account they develop, and compares it with Advaita Vedanta, modern Western philosophy, and the natural and social sciences. Physics and mathematics are one leg of the comparison. The purpose is rigorous comparative understanding through definitions, arguments, textual accuracy, and fit with public knowledge, rather than persuasion or devotional endorsement.

The philosophy is presented in clear, checkable prose. An optional appendix records the structural relations of value through qualitative predicates, distinguishing primary definitions from the assumptions of a formal reconstruction. A fuller mathematical treatment may follow as definitions become stable across the studies; understanding the exposition requires no mathematical formalism.

1. The Madhyasth Darshan Answer

holds that value belongs to existence as coexistence. A unit has value through its essentiality and participation, whether anyone approves of it or recognises it correctly. Human beings encounter this value through , whose activities include recognition, evaluation, and the fulfilment of values in relationships and work. Awakening brings these activities into accordance with reality and makes their fulfilment continuous in living.

1.1 Essentiality and value in coexistence

Every unit is active in coexistence and has form (roop), properties (guna), essential nature (swabhav), and . Form concerns its bounded configuration; properties concern its relative powers and their effects in mutuality; essential nature concerns the characteristic conduct and usefulness of those powers in its order; dharma concerns the innateness it bears. Essentiality (maulikta) is expressed through essential nature and dharma in the unit’s order and plane of existence. Value (mulya) names this essentiality in coexistence (MVD, pp. 47, 124–125; SB, p. 87; AVD, p. 184).

“Entire beingness implies the essentiality of units in every plane and order. Essentiality refers to value.” — SB, p. 50

Complementarity, mutual recognition, and impression are ways in which units participate with one another. A unit’s value is therefore relational while remaining independent of an observer’s preference. Essential nature manifests as values in both sentient and insentient units, and dharma remains inseparable from the unit. The understanding of value requires attention to cause, effect, and participation as well as measurable properties (SB, pp. 50, 87).

Value is qualitative in this precise sense: it concerns what an activity means within the relations that constitute coexistence. Properties include the relative effects of units upon one another; essential nature concerns the usefulness of these properties in participation; dharma concerns the innateness sustained in that participation. A quantitative description can establish dimensions, amounts, or rates, while qualitative understanding identifies nourishment, protection, complementarity, or resolution as the significance of the activity. Both concern the same existing unit. The qualitative aspect therefore has consequences available for examination in its relationships and effects (MVD, p. 47; SB, p. 87).

Existence as coexistence is the ground of this participation. Units are soaked, submerged, and surrounded in Omnipresence, . This saturation, sampriktata, is their energy-fullness, urja-sampannata, and the basis of their forcefulness and continuous activity. Strength, bal, is present in state, and power, shakti, in motion. Absolute energy is omnipresent and actionless; relative power becomes apparent through the mutuality of active units. Their inherent activeness and their particular relational effects belong together in the explanation of participation (MVD, pp. 35, 40, 74–75; SB, pp. 61–62).

Basic impulsion, mool cheshta, is the activity of effort, motion, and result: . Effort is aligned with strength and with dharma and essential nature; motion with essential nature and properties; result with form. Essential nature participates in both the strength borne in activity and its expression as power. The configured unit, its operative effects, the character of its participation, and the innateness it bears are thus aspects of the same activity. Effort, motion, and result remain inseparable as that activity continues through development and mutuality (MVD, p. 40; SB, pp. 60–62).

The qualitative character of an interaction is manifest through this activity. Relative powers have generative, dissolving, and mediative expressions, while their significance differs with the order and relation in which they operate. Integration and disintegration participate in material processes; vitalising and devitalising effects concern pranic organisation; humane conduct concerns understood responsibilities and their fulfilment. Both formation and dissolution have their place in natural participation. Evaluation must therefore recognise the unit, its powers, and what their exercise amounts to in the actual mutuality (MVD, pp. 47, 50–51; SB, pp. 179–182, 249).

The unit’s value is already present through its essentiality. What develops in the human being is the capacity to know that value and to participate accordingly. Labour can give material a useful configuration; awakening can establish understanding and a corresponding continuity of conduct. Configuration, power-expression, and human essential nature are open to the changes appropriate to their order, while dharma remains the order-specific innateness whose fulfilment is at issue. Human happiness remains the purpose through both mistaken and resolved attempts to attain it (MVD, pp. 47, 115, 124–125; SB, pp. 50, 87).

This account extends through all four orders. The material order participates through integration and disintegration. The pranic order adds growth and vitalising or devitalising effects. The animal order includes bodily existence and growth together with jeevan and hope; the knowledge order brings the human possibility of understanding and the purpose of happiness. The characteristic essential natures differ across these orders while each has value in coexistence. Mutual recognition at the material and pranic orders concerns definite reciprocal participation; it does not attribute human reflection or the five faculties to those units. Human recognition can be informed by knowing and believing, and its fulfilment can be examined through self-evaluation (SB, pp. 50, 179–182, 249; JV, p. 139).

Human essential nature is expressed as fortitude, courage, generosity, kindness, grace, and compassion; human dharma is happiness. Human beings fulfil their value through orderliness in themselves and participation in wider orderliness. Responsibility, participation, outcome, and evaluation are consequently connected with essentiality rather than appended to it as an optional moral vocabulary (MVD, pp. 15, 125).

Evaluation has its own place within this ontology. Animals recognise essential nature through friendliness and opposition, and this is a form of evaluation. Humans can understand and evaluate the more comprehensive relations of form, properties, essential nature, and dharma. They can also misrecognise them. Conscious responsibility arises with this capacity: human beings are to use natural resources in accordance with usefulness and regeneration, and to participate as nurturers in the other orders (SB, pp. 141, 200–201, 249).

1.2 Value as activity in jeevan

Sentient nature, chaitanya, is present as constitutionally complete jeevan. The omnipresent reality in which it is saturated is named knowledge, , and, in relation to sentient nature, chetana. Omnipresence remains actionless and changeless; development occurs in nature saturated in it, and realisation is an activity of jeevan. Jeevan bears inexhaustible strength and power, with reflective and projective activities through which recognising and fulfilling become possible. Human awakening concerns the development of this activity towards knowing, believing, recognising, and fulfilling in accordance with reality (MVD, pp. 35, 74–75; SB, pp. 61–64).

The faculties of jeevan are atma, , , , and . Values inherent in jeevan are present as activity. Happiness, peace, contentment, and bliss are harmonies of activity; recognition and fulfilment of relationship values occur through activity; evaluation itself is sentiment activity, bhav-, directed either by awakening or by delusion. Correct evaluation involves the coordination of the whole conscious unit (MVD, p. 203; JV, pp. 138–139).

Sensory contact and projection disclose form and properties, while reflection recognises values, essential nature, and dharma. Cognisance and sensitivity together make recognising and fulfilling comprehensive. Realisation in coexistence, anubhav, is the unfolding of knowledge in the human knower, with wisdom and science as its evidence. The human need is consequently to understand and realise the reality in which activity already occurs, and to make that understanding effective in thought, behaviour, and work (MVD, pp. 35, 97–100; SB, pp. 63–64).

State (sthiti) and motion (gati) are inseparable aspects of activity. State is strength (bal); motion is power (shakti). In awakened jeevan, the relation appears as realisation and its evidence, understanding and resolve, contemplation and visualisation, deliberation and analysis, and tasting and selection. State carries the understood basis of the activity; motion expresses it. This relation gives established value an active meaning: what is understood and accepted becomes available for fulfilment in living (KD, §3.8, p. 84).

The two aspects remain inseparable across these faculties (KD, §3.8, p. 84). The faculty chitta and its activity chitran have distinct meanings. Visualisation gives form to meaning; contemplation and direct apprehension concern the reality understood through it. A person can visualise a desirable result while still misunderstanding the relationship involved. Complete recognition requires that the image, deliberation, and selection be informed by understanding and realisation.

Visualisation at chitta encompasses form, property, count, time, expanse, effort, motion, and result. The activity of a unit is therefore apprehended with its configuration, relational powers, duration, and consequences. Meaningful visualisation brings these into accordance with the reality contemplated and understood. Realisation-based understanding at buddhi continues as resolve; contemplation and visualisation give it expression in thought; deliberation and analysis clarify its implications; tasting and selection make it available in the recognition and fulfilment of relationships (MVD, pp. 99–100, 327).

At vritti, evaluation can operate through pleasant–unpleasant (priya–apriya), beneficial–harmful (hita–ahita), and gain–loss (labha–alabha). These perspectives direct attention towards sensory attraction, bodily advantage, and acquisition. The humane perspectives are justice–injustice (–anyaya), dharma–adharma, and truth–untruth (–asatya). Justice concerns fulfilled values in relationships; human dharma concerns resolution and happiness; truth concerns coexistence as it is. The development of evaluation changes the basis on which a person deliberates, analyses, and chooses. Pleasure and health retain their place, while prosperity replaces acquisitive profit as the economic purpose (JV, pp. 139–140; MVD, pp. 58, 66, 336–337).

Mun can taste what is attractive and select it, and vritti can analyse how to obtain it, without the person having understood whether its pursuit fulfils the relationship. Analysis takes its direction from what has been visualised and accepted. Under awakening, contemplation of justice, dharma, and truth informs visualisation; understanding and realisation supply its ground. The resulting analysis clarifies value, and selection expresses that clarification. True value therefore cannot be established by preference or calculation at mun and vritti alone. The activities of chitta, buddhi, and atma belong to the understanding that makes such evaluation correct (KD, p. 84; MVD, pp. 83, 203).

This coordination also connects language with value. Words communicate meanings concerning existing realities. The meaning a person apprehends depends on the person’s view, evaluation, capacity, ability, and receptivity. These relations are activities of chitta, successful or unsuccessful according to awakening. Education must consequently develop understanding of what the words indicate, as well as the ability to repeat or visualise them. Study of meaning reaches understanding and realisation and becomes available for further expression (MVD, p. 83; KD, pp. 112–113).

The detailed account of awakened jeevan enumerates 122 activity members in 61 pairs: two members at atma, four at buddhi, sixteen at chitta, thirty-six at vritti, and sixty-four at mun. One named pair at vritti is bhav and samveg. Bhav, rendered sentiment, is defined through essentiality, value, responsibility, and participation; samveg is the impetus associated with meaningful union. Its direction can be towards advancement or decline. These named activities locate value and its expression within the more extensive activity of jeevan (MVD, pp. 43, 323, 338; AVD, pp. 91–94).

The constitutional completeness of jeevan identifies the conscious unit and its inherent capacity. Activity completeness concerns the fulfilled operation of that capacity; conduct completeness concerns its continuous evidence in living. Constitutional completeness therefore does not establish that a particular person’s present evaluation is correct. Learning addresses the development from incomplete understanding towards awakened activity and conduct. Completeness is continuity of meaningful activity, with understanding available in thought, behaviour, and work (MVD, p. 83; KD, pp. 37, 84, 112–113).

The realisation of coexistence in atma, resolution in thought through buddhi and chitta, and justice in behaviour through vritti and mun describe the encompassing coherence of awakened living. Justice, dharma, and truth are accepted in understanding and expressed through the related activities of the faculties. This coherence gives established value its continuity: the person can recognise and fulfil a relationship because its meaning is understood and its place in coexistence is realised (MVD, pp. 99–100, 156).

1.3 Forms of value in human living

Human living involves jeevan values, human values, established values, expressed or civic values, and usefulness–aesthetic values. Object value is present in the world of units; the values inherent in jeevan are understood and evaluated by jeevan. Their fulfilment becomes manifest through recognised relationships and purposeful work (MVD, p. 306; JV, pp. 43, 55, 138–139).

The four jeevan values are happiness (sukh), peace (shanti), contentment (santosh), and bliss (anand). They name definite harmonies between faculties. Jeevan lives and feels these harmonies through its own activity. Their structural basis is the concurrence, samatva, or absence of opposition, nirvirodh, between the related faculties, with the meaning of that concurrence evident in understanding and conduct. Their fulfilment is also expressed through resolution, prosperity, fearlessness, and coexistence in living (MVD, p. 327; JV, p. 138; MAD, reader p. 47; KD, §3.12, p. 112).

Jeevan value Harmony between faculties Cumulative fulfilment in living
Happiness (sukh) mun and vritti Resolution
Peace (shanti) vritti and chitta Resolution and prosperity
Contentment (santosh) chitta and buddhi Resolution, prosperity, and fearlessness
Bliss (anand) buddhi and atma Resolution, prosperity, fearlessness, and coexistence

These harmonies have definite qualitative content. Happiness is resolution, borne at mun in its harmony with vritti, and connected with behaviour free of wrongdoing. Peace is the consequence of resolved thought, including the absence of resistance between desire and thought; the harmony of vritti and chitta is expressed in thought free of injustice. Contentment concerns the harmony of chitta and buddhi, with desire free of attachment. Bliss concerns the harmony of buddhi and atma, with understanding free of ignorance. The named values thus express how tasting, deliberation, contemplation, understanding, and realisation accord within one conscious unit (MVD, pp. 328, 331, 333, 346; MAD, reader p. 47).

Sensory pleasantness concerns the conducive contact of sound, touch, sight, taste, and smell with the sense organs in relation to bodily wellness. Tasting and selection belong to mun, while deliberation evaluates what is sought. A pleasant sensory occasion can therefore have a place in human satisfaction, and its significance is examined through these activities of jeevan. Under humane understanding, pleasure and health are included within the perspective of justice, with prosperity replacing acquisitive profit. Happiness as resolution locates their fulfilment within the understood purposes of living (MVD, pp. 327, 343, 346; JV, pp. 139–140).

Contentment includes freedom from the sentiment of lack together with readiness to evidence production beyond need through science aligned with wisdom. Its paired expression is abundance, shree. The harmony of chitta and buddhi connects the image of what is to be done with the understanding and resolve that give it direction. Visualising form, properties, count, time, expanse, effort, motion, and result makes planning an activity informed by that understanding. Needs, means, and their fulfilment consequently have a practical place in the account of contentment (MVD, pp. 99–100, 327, 331; KD, p. 84).

Bliss, borne at buddhi, is uninterrupted celebration in the acceptance of awakening. Realisation in coexistence at atma is ultimate bliss, paramanand, with authenticity as its expression and evidence. Atma and Omnipresence are eternally in concurrence; realisation brings this reality into the awakened activity of jeevan. The four faculty harmonies are effortlessly present in living based on realisation. Their experienced character and their structural definiteness belong together: the person feels the harmony that the specified activities themselves constitute (MVD, pp. 327–328; JV, p. 138).

Human values articulate the essential nature appropriate to this fulfilment. Fortitude (dhirata) is firmness towards justice; courage (veerta) deploys intellectual and physical powers to make justice available; generosity (udarta) offers body, mind, and wealth as needed for awakening, health, and prosperity. The humane expression is characterised by fortitude, courage, and generosity, and the higher-humane expression by kindness, grace, and compassion (MVD, pp. 52, 60–61, 125, 340).

Kindness (daya) makes content available where receptivity is present but content is lacking. Grace (kripa) develops receptivity where content is available but the recipient lacks receptivity. Compassion (karuna) works to make both available where both are absent. Fulfilling these values requires recognition of the recipient’s situation and the means to respond to it. Love includes their combined expression (MVD, pp. 61, 331).

Established values (sthapit mulya) are the values in strength, bal, or state, sthiti. Expressed values, also called civic or shishta values, are their corresponding outward expressions in motion, gati, on the power or shakti side. There is a one-to-one correspondence between the established and expressed members of these value pairs. The nine principal established relationship values are care, guidance, trust, affection, gratitude, glory, love, reverence, and respect. Trust is the common value (samya mulya) in relationships and love the complete value (purna mulya). Their corresponding expressions and faculty locations are as follows (MAD, reader p. 30; JV, p. 108; AVD, pp. 91–94; MVD, pp. 331–340).

Care accepts the other with affinity and sustains nurture and protection; generosity offers available body, mind, and wealth according to need. Guidance sustains nurture towards comprehensive development, while naturalness expresses clarity and coherence across behaviour, thought, and realisation. Affection is the absence of resistance in just behaviour and continuity of meeting for satisfaction; dedication sustains effort towards the understood goal of awakening (MVD, pp. 331–332, 339–340).

Gratitude accepts the help received towards development and awakening, with humility expressed as voluntary self-restraint and civility. Glory concerns the willing acceptance of an example for emulation; simplicity is its expression without inward complication and tension. Reverence is movement towards qualitative development, awakening, and conduct completeness; devoutness is the activity directed towards that development. Respect accepts excellence in personality and talent and its balanced expression; cordiality faithfully presents what has been accepted in conception, realisation, memory, and received understanding (MVD, pp. 332, 335, 340).

Trust is fulfilled mutual value, together with understanding of orderliness and communication of resolution. Its expression, concordance, consists in cooperation, participation, and complementarity. Love includes realisation of completeness and the combined expression of kindness, grace, and compassion. Non-otherness gives it expression through complementary activity with human beings and the rest of nature, continuity of resolution, and help towards another’s awakening. Love as complete value accordingly reaches the whole field of participation (MVD, pp. 331, 336; MAD, reader pp. 30, 43).

The faculty column identifies named activity positions within awakened jeevan. Each relationship value nevertheless depends on the coordinated understanding described in §1.2. Locating care at mun or trust at vritti does not make either faculty independently sufficient for its true understanding. The significance of the relationship, the recognition of need, and the grounds of fulfilment involve the whole conscious unit (KD, p. 84; MVD, p. 203).

1.4 Recognition, fulfilment and evaluation

A relationship () is a mutuality whose expectations are predetermined in the sense of completeness. A contact (sampark) is a mutuality whose expectations are voluntarily determined. Both involve value and are answerable to justice. The freedom to agree on the expectations of a contact carries responsibility for what is agreed and how it is fulfilled (MVD, pp. 61–62; KD, p. 20).

The established values inherent in relationships endure across proximity and distance, past, present, and future. Their definite content supplies the basis for recognising what is due in a relationship. The intellectual and material means of fulfilment are assessed in the actual circumstances: nurturing a child, making understanding available to a learner, or acknowledging help received calls for the appropriate resources, ability, and receptivity. Definite value and situated fulfilment are therefore joined through evaluation (MAD, reader p. 51; MVD, pp. 61–62, 331–340).

The relationship actually present and the person’s recognition of it can differ. A responsibility can remain unfulfilled because the person has misunderstood the other, the purpose of the relationship, or the means appropriate to its fulfilment. Knowing and believing concern this correctness of recognition; fulfilment gives it expression in the actual mutuality. Evaluation consequently examines both what was recognised and what the resulting participation accomplished (MVD, pp. 97, 311; JV, pp. 98, 139).

The named relationships include parent–child, sibling, spousal, master–associate, teacher–student, friendship, and orderliness relationships. Their fulfilment is associated with comprehensive resolution, nurturing, protection, usefulness or complementarity, right-use, and purposeful-use. Family, society, and orderliness also confer rights within participation (MVD, p. 190; JV, pp. 108–109).

Recognising a relationship makes its values available for fulfilment. Care involves provision and protection; guidance develops understanding; trust supports assurance in mutuality; gratitude acknowledges the contribution of another. These values become evident in acts and in their effect on the relationship. Humane duty consists in acts that fulfil values, and fulfilment depends on means, resources, and the receptivity required by the situation (MVD, pp. 61–62, 73, 331–340).

Justice comprises recognition of relationships, fulfilment of their values, evaluation, and mutual satisfaction. Evaluation asks whether the recognised responsibility has been fulfilled and whether the outcome accords with human essentiality. Mutual satisfaction is the result of this sequence, with the relationship and its values supplying the standard of assessment (MVD, pp. 311, 336).

Evaluation also informs deliberation before action: the person must recognise what the relationship calls for, assess the available means, and select an appropriate contribution. Fulfilment then provides the outcome to evaluate. In justice, the stated order is recognition, fulfilment, evaluation, and mutual satisfaction; within the activity of jeevan, evaluative understanding also guides the selection that makes fulfilment possible. The relationship remains the reference throughout. Recognition makes its values available for expression, and evaluation determines whether their intended meaning has been borne in conduct (JV, p. 138; KD, p. 84; MVD, pp. 61–62, 336).

The reality recognised, the evaluator’s understanding, and the ability to fulfil must therefore be distinguished. A unit’s essentiality does not depend on being correctly recognised. Recognition can become clearer while resources or skill remain inadequate for a particular responsibility. An effective contribution can also occur before the learner understands its full basis. Orderliness in human living requires the conjunction: correct recognition, adequate capacity and means, and fulfilled participation whose result can be evaluated (SB, p. 87; MVD, pp. 61, 94, 203).

Self-examination has a definite role here. The individual can examine whether justice is present on both sides and whether conduct fulfils the recognised relationship. Evaluation of values inherent in jeevan is performed by jeevan through examining itself. The resulting conduct also becomes evident to those with whom the individual lives and works (JV, pp. 98, 139).

The recognition, acceptance, and fulfilment of relationship values give content to the taste activity of mun. Happiness, peace, contentment, and bliss are present in the experience of established values. Inward harmony and outward fulfilment thus belong to one meaningful participation: understanding makes the relationship recognisable, evaluation guides and examines its fulfilment, and jeevan experiences the value in that activity. This experienced value is part of the orderliness being fulfilled (JV, pp. 138–139; MAD, reader p. 51).

Outward proficiency and establishment in state must be distinguished during this assessment. An act can fulfil a practical responsibility while the learner is still developing the understanding that would make such conduct continuous. Conversely, a claim to established care requires examination of the responsibilities actually fulfilled. Education can value the learner’s real contribution while continuing to examine understanding, consistency, resources, and outcomes (§1.7).

The consequences of participation become available for further thought and evaluation. What was expressed through action can be examined and settled again in conception, and understood meaning becomes the basis of renewed expression. Evaluation thus participates in the continuity of activity: reflection on a fulfilled or unfulfilled responsibility can clarify an acceptance, correct a mistaken recognition, or strengthen the understanding already present. The constitutional capacity of jeevan continues through these changes in its understanding and conduct (MVD, pp. 83, 218; KD, pp. 112–113).

1.5 Delusion, awakening and the ordering of value

Delusion involves over-evaluation, under-evaluation, and mis-evaluation. Something may be assigned a significance greater or smaller than its essentiality warrants, or recognised as something other than what it is. Familiar examples include mistaking a rope for a snake and assigning the worth of gold to iron. At the human level these errors affect self-assessment, relationships, acquisition, and the use of resources (MVD, pp. 152, 306; SB, p. 146).

Correct evaluation develops with awakening. Nirakarshan, freedom from attraction or attachment in the relevant definition, is freedom from the three evaluative errors. It permits conduct governed by essentiality rather than imposed estimation. Non-doership likewise concerns freedom from wrongful action within a life that remains active (MVD, p. 152). Self-evaluation is the first step in the study of awakening, and complete correctness belongs to the coordinated activities of awakened jeevan (MVD, pp. 87, 203).

Fear and temptation direct relationships through threatened harm or promised acquisition. They cannot serve as relationship values and cannot be evaluated as fulfilled values. The alternative is recognition of the relationship, its responsibilities, and mutual satisfaction. The darshan’s account of their origin connects fear with retraction and temptation with attraction in its description of sentient activity (JV, pp. 109–110; MVD, p. 199; SB, ch. 9, item 11, pp. 290–295).

Human beings deploy body, mind, and wealth towards what they regard as most valuable. Under delusion the four basic instincts are treated as the highest purpose; discerning evaluation can place the three eshanas — progeny, wealth, and reputation — highest; delusionless evaluation places freedom from delusion highest. Evaluation in the deluded, discerning, and delusionless states is correspondingly named attachment, awakening, and reality. Awakening reorders desire through understanding, self-evaluation, and humane purposes (MVD, pp. 60, 319). The bodily instrument serves nourishment, protection, and participation, while the purpose of jeevan is fulfilled through understanding and happiness (JV, pp. 93–94, 123).

The value classes have an explicit relation of dedication (samarpan): usefulness serves need; need serves shishta or expressed value; expressed value serves established value; established value serves human value; and human value serves jeevan value. Production and material provision are thereby directed towards fulfilled relationships and humane living, whose purpose is the harmony of jeevan. Understanding of this purpose guides the organisation of production, use, right-use, and distribution (MAD, reader p. 34).

Higher and lower value have several connected senses. More enduring value, more comprehensive resolution, and development towards fulfilment support ascent. Relations between the four orders also involve inclusion and differences of development. Mutual ascent supports the other in development, whereas descent and aggression carry contrary effects. Knowing a more developed activity is possible even where the incomplete cannot measure the complete by its own existing measure (MVD, pp. 43, 47, 110, 126, 219–222, 291; KD, p. 37).

The profit perspective has a different direction. It seeks greater worth in return for lesser worth or labour; the other party may surrender the greater through helplessness, willingness, or ignorance. Justice, dharma, and truth reorient living towards resolution and prosperity. Pleasure and bodily health find their place within humane living, while prosperity replaces acquisitive profit as the purpose of production and exchange (MVD, pp. 58, 67; JV, pp. 139–140).

1.6 Value in production, exchange and right-use

Useful things enable nourishment, bodily protection, and participation in society. Food, housing, and adornment figure among common aspirations, with transport and communication among special aspirations. Understanding human purposes and family needs guides the assessment of what should be produced, acquired, and used (MVD, pp. 56–57; JV, p. 123).

Usefulness value (upyogita mulya) concerns what a thing can do in such participation. Useful production depends on the properties of things, human need, and appropriate application. Human labour transforms natural abundance into useful and aesthetically qualified products, and aesthetic addition is meaningful in relation to usefulness and convenience. Use, exchange, and the needs met through them have temporal circumstances; evaluation must identify what is needed and what the thing can fulfil in that circumstance (JV, pp. 123, 137–138; MVD, pp. 56, 268; MAD, reader p. 51).

Application (prayog) is the endeavour, continued until success, to establish usefulness and aesthetic value through labour in response to need. Production (utpadan) standardises the successful process. Production–work includes the skills, resources, agriculture, and industry through which families can produce beyond their assessed needs. Prosperity therefore requires the ability and readiness to produce an adequate material provision (MVD, pp. 56, 260, 268; MVD, p. 331).

Use deploys means in production and service. Right-use (sadupyog) directs body, mind, and wealth towards participation in family orderliness. Purposeful-use (prayojansheelta) serves awakening, undivided society, and universal orderliness. These purposes guide production and consumption together, connecting the assessment of an object with the work and relationships in which it will be used (MVD, p. 57).

Exchange requires evaluation of labour and of the goods through which labour’s results become available. The proposed order protects labour and its fruits from exploitation, supports exchange of labour-valued goods, and seeks exchange free from profit and loss. Labour deployment belongs to production; labour-value assessment belongs to exchange; evaluation of established and expressed values belongs to justice. Each has a distinct responsibility within the same social order (MVD, pp. 260, 268–269; SB, p. 237).

Rightful wealth can be received through remuneration, gift, and award. Material provision and the fulfilment of relationships therefore include legitimate receiving as well as producing. This is significant for the connection between economic participation, family responsibility, and the care of people whose needs exceed their current productive capacity (AVD, p. 170; MVD, pp. 61, 340).

Money is symbolic value. An increase in its quantity or in the price assigned to a good does not itself increase the good’s usefulness. Manufactured scarcity can increase symbolic valuation while obstructing the availability of things people need. The assessment of prosperity accordingly turns on understanding need, useful production, access, and right-use (MVD, p. 183; SB, pp. 211–212).

Production and exchange also affect the other orders. The use of natural abundance is governed by usefulness and regeneration, and human participation is to nourish the conditions of coexistence. This places the evaluation of resources, work, and outcomes within a wider responsibility than the immediate exchange between two people (SB, pp. 141, 200–201).

1.7 Knowledge, education and continuity of value

Knowledge is evident through law, regulation, balance, justice, dharma, and truth: niyam–niyantran–santulan–nyaya–dharma–satya. Omnipresence is knowledge; realisation in knowledge is understanding, whose evidence is knowledge, wisdom, and science. Law is manifest in regulation, regulation in balance, balanced human living in justice, and just participation in universal orderliness in dharma or resolution. Realisation in coexistence is truth. These relations connect the orderliness in which units are active with the understanding and fulfilment required of the human being (MVD, pp. 35, 80, 174).

Law, niyam, is the basis for regulating conduct and activity and the way of their protection and discipline. Regulation, niyantran, concerns the operation of that law; balance, santulan, concerns the ordered participation sustained through it. Material lawfulness is evident through result-conformance, pranic regulation through seed-conformance, and animal balance through species-conformance. Human orderliness has the possibility of an understood tradition through -conformance. Its fulfilment includes the work, means, and natural conditions through which human purposes are accomplished (MVD, pp. 34, 70, 174; SB, pp. 236–237).

Justice regulates human behaviour through the recognition and fulfilment of relationships. Dharma disciplines thought towards resolution and participation in overall orderliness. Truth is coexistence realised in atma. Realised understanding becomes evident through these interconnected dimensions of living: the values of a relationship receive fulfilment, its purpose accords with resolution, and the understanding expressed through it accords with reality. Knowledge consequently has responsibilities in both the organisation of work and the orientation of its purposes (MVD, pp. 100, 137, 156, 174).

Balance in human ethical and behavioural participation includes restraint of excess. The lawful organisation of means, the regulation of their use, and their balanced participation are therefore necessary to fulfil understood value. Justice, resolution, and truth give this participation its humane orientation. The continuity of knowledge in living is evidenced as realisation, anubhav, behaviour, vyavahar, and application, prayog, with each dimension contributing to the evidence of understanding (MVD, pp. 12, 80, 102, 174).

Consciousness-development value education develops knowing, believing, recognising, and fulfilling. Its purpose is understanding that can be evidenced in conduct. Study, instruction, practice, and self-evaluation provide activities through which a learner can examine propositions, recognise relationships, and participate with increasing correctness (SB, p. 237; KD, pp. 18–19; MVD, pp. 87, 90, 94).

Sanskar concerns acceptances towards completeness, the knowledge, wisdom, and science available for resolution, and the tendencies and understanding through which meaningful activity is expressed. Its scope includes bodily, verbal, and mental participation through doing, enabling another’s doing, and consenting. Susanskar supports awakening and resolved, prosperous living; kusanskar supports decline. The formation of value therefore includes what a learner accepts and desires, the examples and meanings available in the environment, and the conduct through which those acceptances are examined (MVD, pp. 90, 94).

Intermittent generosity and subsequent miserliness illustrate meaningful activity without continuity under delusion. Learning from an awakened person’s conduct can occasion rethinking, but a particular outward act does not establish the learner’s corresponding value in state. Education develops the understanding of relationship and purpose that can make fulfilment self-directed and sustained. Its work includes reflection on errors, clarification of meaning, and renewed participation, with differences in the learner’s capacity and receptivity taken into account (MVD, pp. 61, 94; KD, pp. 84, 112–113).

Internal reception or aapyayan concerns welcoming, tasting, and taking value into oneself, evidenced through evaluation. Qualitative refinement, parimarjan, concerns change in hope, thought, desire, and resolve. Establishment thus involves the learner’s own acceptance and transformation across the activities of jeevan, together with the capacity to evaluate the meaning accepted. Human dharma remains happiness, while the comprehension and conduct through which it is sought develop towards resolution. The strength and power inherent in jeevan become expressed through an increasingly understood purpose (MAD, reader p. 45; MVD, pp. 99–100, 125; SB, pp. 61–64).

Evidenced work has a further role once it is understood. What is projected and assessed in living is accepted in bodh and anubhav, understanding and realisation, and becomes the basis for further expression. Teaching and study are connected with this continuity. Completeness is thus active and self-evidencing, with understanding repeatedly available in thought, behaviour, and work (KD, pp. 37, 112–113).

An individual can develop beyond the disorder of an inherited tradition. Nagraj describes learning and self-experimentation within existing social circumstances, and the account identifies six merits of education: self-confidence, respect for excellence, balance in talent, balance in personality, social behaviour, and self-reliance in vocation. These concern both the learner’s understanding and the capacity to live with others (SB, p. 294; JV, pp. 101, 140).

The five dimensions of social orderliness have corresponding criteria of fulfilment. Education–sanskar succeeds in understood meaning. Justice–preservation succeeds through value-fulfilment, evaluation, and mutual satisfaction. Production–work supports family production beyond need. Exchange–storage evaluates labour and enables exchange. Health–restraint maintains the body as capable of evidencing awakening. The institutions are responsibilities whose success is to be examined in their work (MVD, p. 260).

Value also belongs with character and ethics in the definition of humane conduct. Character concerns rightful wealth, marital fidelity, and kindness in behaviour and work; ethics concerns the protection and right-use of body, mind, and wealth. Values supply the understood purposes, while these determinations of conduct give them continuing expression in the use of means and in relationships. Claims to right feeling consequently remain answerable to the way a person lives and works (JV, p. 55).

This participation extends through the levels of family orderliness. Authenticity is evaluated in the dimensions of living and the systems through which conduct is evidenced. A claim to understanding is therefore connected with what a person can explain, fulfil, produce, and sustain in mutuality (AVD, pp. 97, 265).

1.8 The structure of qualitative orderliness

The theoretical framework connects the existence of value, its understanding, and its fulfilment. Saturation grounds the strength and power of active units; effort, motion, and result express the continuity in which their form, properties, essential nature, and dharma are present. Essentiality has qualitative significance in this participation. Sentient jeevan recognises and evaluates, while human awakening brings this activity into accordance with knowledge realised as coexistence. Established and expressed values give understood meaning continuity in conduct. Law, regulation, balance, justice, resolution, and truth connect that conduct with overall orderliness (MVD, pp. 35, 124–125, 174, 203; SB, pp. 61–64, 249; KD, p. 84).

2. The Advaita Vedanta Answer

Advaita Vedanta understands liberation through knowledge of the identity of and . Ignorance supports identification with limited bodies, minds, and possessions; knowledge removes that misidentification. This account gives liberation a distinctive place among human purposes while retaining a domain of duties, disciplined action, and ethical preparation in empirical life (BSB, Introduction and 1.1.4).

2.1 Human purposes and the status of liberation

The four purusharthas are , artha, kama, and moksha: right living, material means, enjoyment, and liberation. Material acquisition and enjoyment have their proper place under dharma. Liberation differs from an acquired possession or produced experience because knowledge discloses the Self whose reality does not depend on production. Discrimination between enduring reality and transient attainments therefore guides the aspirant’s priorities (BSB, 1.1.4; VC, vv. 17–20).

2.2 The Self and dearness

The Maitreyi teaching examines why husband, wife, children, wealth, and other things are dear. Their dearness is for the sake of the Self, and the inquiry proceeds through hearing, reflection, and contemplation of the Self. In Advaita this teaching directs attention beyond identification with the individual ego towards the reality through which dearness is intelligible (BU, 2.4.5).

Bliss belongs to the Upanishadic account of Brahman and the freedom from fear associated with its knowledge. The Taittiriya inquiry moves through increasingly inward accounts of the person and culminates in the teaching of Brahman as the source of bliss. The discrimination of the sheaths also separates conditioned experience from the Self that is disclosed through their examination (TU, 2.1–2.9; VC, v. 152).

2.3 Qualification and the transformation of attention

Discrimination, dispassion, the disciplines of restraint and concentration, and desire for liberation orient the student towards knowledge. They are cultivated through a life of study and discipline. Their educational role is to make understanding possible and stable, including the ability to distinguish the permanent from the impermanent and to inquire without being governed by attachment (BSB, 1.1.1; VC, vv. 17–20).

The divine qualities include fearlessness, purity of mind, generosity, self-control, truthfulness, compassion, and freedom from anger. These are positive qualities of conduct as well as conditions of spiritual preparation. Ethical formation develops capacities for living and inquiry within the empirical world (BG, 16.1–16.3).

2.4 Action without attachment

Prescribed action is to be performed, and bodily maintenance itself depends on action. Action without attachment is taught as a discipline leading towards the highest good. The example of Janaka and the instruction concerning loka-sangraha, the holding together of the world, also give action a social purpose. The wise are to consider the effect of their example on those still engaged in action (BG, 3.8, 3.19–3.20, 3.25–3.26).

Shankara’s commentary distinguishes the qualifications and purposes of agents within these instructions. Action can prepare the mind for knowledge, while action associated with the welfare and guidance of others can continue without the realised person’s identification as an individual doer. Liberation remains knowledge of an already accomplished reality (BG with Shankara’s commentary, 3.19–3.26; BSB, 1.1.4).

2.5 Value knowledge and its evidence

Scriptural teaching, reasoned inquiry, and the removal of mistaken identification are central to knowledge of the Self. An experience of pleasure or an outwardly successful undertaking has a different evidential role from the knowledge that removes ignorance. The account therefore connects ethical discipline and inquiry while distinguishing their fruits from liberation itself (BU, 2.4.5; BSB, Introduction and 1.1.1).

2.6 Empirical life and ultimate fulfilment

The distinction between empirical and ultimate standpoints allows duties, relationships, and spiritual preparation to retain their force while identification with a separate limited self is questioned. Advaita’s ultimate account of fulfilment concerns the knowledge of non-duality. Its treatment of finite goods must be read together with its teachings on duty, compassion, discipline, and action for the world’s maintenance (BU, 2.4.5; BG, 3.19–3.26, 16.1–16.3).

3. Modern Western Philosophy on Value

Modern Western value theory contains competing accounts of what is good, what bears value, and how value is known. Metaethics examines the status of normative judgments; welfare theory examines what makes life go well for a person; practical philosophy examines reasons, duties, and conflicts. These inquiries overlap without yielding a single Western doctrine (SEP Value Theory).

3.1 Intrinsic, final and instrumental value

Something can be valued as a means to another good or for its own sake. This final–instrumental distinction differs from the distinction between value grounded in intrinsic features and value dependent on relations or context. A historically significant object, for example, may be valued for its own sake partly because of its history (SEP Intrinsic vs Extrinsic Value).

Moore asks what would be good considered in isolation, but also argues that the value of a whole need not equal the sum of its parts’ values. His principle of organic unities makes the configuration of a valuable whole matter to assessment. Intrinsic value therefore does not invariably entail simple addition of separately valued constituents (Moore 1903, §§18–22).

3.2 Natural facts and normative conclusions

Hume calls attention to the unexplained transition from statements about what is to conclusions about what ought to be. Moore’s open-question argument challenges proposed definitions of goodness in terms of some other property: understanding that something has the property appears to leave open whether it is good (Hume 1739, III.1.1; Moore 1903, §§10–14).

The two arguments address different problems. Hume’s passage concerns inference; Moore’s concerns the analysis of goodness. Neither by itself settles every form of ethical naturalism. A theory may propose a substantive rather than definitional identity, or argue for normative standards through the characteristic flourishing of living beings. Such proposals require arguments for the identification and for its practical authority (SEP Natural Law Ethics; SEP Moral Realism).

3.3 Realism, construction and anti-realism

Moral realists hold that some moral judgments are true and that their truth is not exhausted by the speaker’s approval. Naturalist and non-naturalist versions disagree about the relation between moral facts and the natural world. Expressivists explain moral language through practical attitudes and commitments; error theorists argue that ordinary moral claims systematically fail in what they purport to assert (SEP Moral Realism; SEP Moral Anti-Realism).

Constructivist approaches connect normative standards with an appropriate procedure of practical reasoning. Their relation to realism depends on the particular account of construction and objectivity. Expressivist theories likewise develop accounts of disagreement, consistency, and practical criticism. The categories identify research programmes whose adequacy turns on their detailed treatment of truth, reasons, and judgment (SEP Moral Anti-Realism).

3.4 Fitting attitudes and reasons

Fitting-attitude accounts analyse value through the attitudes it is appropriate to have towards an object: admiration, love, desire, or respect, for example. The relevant attitude is fitting, rather than merely actual or widely shared. A central difficulty is distinguishing reasons that bear on an object’s value from incentives or threats that make an attitude advantageous to its holder (SEP Fitting Attitude Theories).

Scanlon’s buck-passing account explains the value of something through other properties that give reasons for appropriate responses. Value identifies the presence of such reasons rather than supplying an additional independent reason. The account shifts inquiry towards what makes a response warranted (Scanlon 1998, ch. 2).

3.5 Pleasure, desire and objective goods

Hedonistic theories make pleasure and the absence of pain central to welfare. Desire theories locate welfare in the fulfilment of desires, often with qualifications concerning information, rationality, or the scope of the person’s concern. Objective-list theories hold that some things can benefit a person independently of current desire or pleasure, such as knowledge, achievement, or worthwhile relationships (Parfit 1984, Appendix I; SEP Well-Being).

Nozick’s experience machine asks whether an entirely satisfying simulated experience would supply everything that matters in life. The thought experiment tests whether doing things, being a certain person, and contact with reality matter beyond experience. Its force depends on reflection about the case and the intuitions it elicits (Nozick 1974, pp. 42–45).

3.6 Plurality, comparison and trade-offs

Value pluralism holds that more than one irreducible kind of value matters. It need not imply that every pair of options is incomparable. Ranking, comparison through a common measure, compensation for a loss, and practical permission to make a trade-off are distinct questions. Some accounts allow comparison without a common numerical unit; others identify cases where familiar comparative relations fail (Berlin 1969; Raz 1986; Chang 1997; SEP Value Pluralism; SEP Incommensurable Values).

Kant distinguishes price, which permits an equivalent, from dignity, which admits no equivalent. Anderson examines how different goods call for different modes of valuation and how market valuation can be inappropriate in some relations. These positions concern the standards governing valuation and exchange, including cases in which willingness to pay does not decide what should be done (Kant 1785, Section II; Anderson 1993).

3.7 Scheler and Hartmann on ordered values

Scheler treats value as objective and disclosed through intentional feeling, with a hierarchy of agreeable, vital, spiritual, and holy values. This feeling is directed towards value rather than being identical with bodily sensation. His account includes distorted valuation and the transformation of attention through love and openness. Hartmann also develops a differentiated order of values and distinguishes the height of a value from the strength of its demand. Ordered value consequently raises questions both of recognition and of what takes priority in action (Scheler 1913–16; Hartmann 1932; SEP Scheler).

3.8 Economic value and human development

Marx distinguishes use-value, the usefulness of a thing, from value in commodity production and its expression in exchange-value. Concrete useful labour produces particular use-values; abstract human labour supplies the account of commodity value, whose magnitude depends on socially necessary labour time. Nature contributes to material wealth, and some useful things have no value in this specific commodity sense. These distinctions prevent a simple identification of usefulness, labour expended by an individual, and exchange price (Marx 1867, ch. 1).

Marginal analysis examines the significance of an additional unit within particular conditions of choice. A thing’s physical capacity alone does not determine its marginal benefit or exchange conditions. The capability approach further distinguishes resources and reported satisfaction from people’s real opportunities to live and act. Sen and Nussbaum make this distinction central to evaluating development, while differing in how capabilities should be specified (Sen 1999; Nussbaum 2011; SEP Capability Approach).

3.9 Care, recognition and trust

Care ethics examines dependence, attentiveness, responsiveness, and the concrete relationship between one who cares and one who receives care. Recognition theory examines the social relations through which practical identity and self-realisation are sustained. These approaches bring the conditions of human development into the account of value; a requirement for social recognition need not make a person’s basic worth depend on whether others currently recognise it (Noddings 1984; Honneth 1995; SEP Recognition).

Philosophical accounts of trust examine dependence under vulnerability, expectations of another, and the distinction between reliance and trustworthiness. They ask when trust is reasonable and what betrayal damages. Trust is therefore a normative relationship whose success cannot be identified simply with confidence or successful prediction (SEP Trust).

3.10 The range of the inquiry

These approaches offer different resources for explaining value’s bearers, grounds, recognition, and practical force. A claim about the status of moral truth does not alone determine a theory of welfare; a theory of welfare does not alone determine just distribution. Western value theory makes these distinctions explicit and investigates how they can be joined (SEP Value Theory; SEP Well-Being).

4. The Natural and Social Sciences on Valuation

The natural and social sciences study how organisms learn, choose, cooperate, report well-being, and organise collective life. They investigate both evaluative processes and the conditions and consequences of conduct. Scientific findings can inform practical judgment when joined to stated purposes and normative commitments. Their evidential strength depends on the design, population, measures, and causal assumptions of each investigation.

4.1 Neural valuation and learning

Neuroscience distinguishes processes of representing options, learning from outcomes, and selecting actions. Research on value-based choice identifies neural systems through which different options can be compared for a decision. Dopamine reward-prediction-error research concerns differences between expected and received reward and their role in learning (Rangel, Camerer, and Montague 2008; Schultz 2016).

The neural comparison of options and the learning signals that update expectations perform different functions. A shared neural scale within a task concerns how a decision is implemented. It does not establish that every moral or welfare consideration has an objectively equivalent numerical amount. Moral decisions themselves recruit interacting systems whose contributions depend on the task (Crockett 2013).

4.2 Cooperation and moral regularities

Evolutionary explanations investigate how cooperation can develop under conditions such as repeated interaction and reciprocity. These mechanisms can explain patterns of behaviour without assuming that an organism formulates a moral principle (Trivers 1971).

A comparative study of ethnographic records from sixty societies found broadly positive moral evaluations of seven forms of cooperation, including helping kin, reciprocity, bravery, fair division, and respect for prior possession. The finding concerns coded evidence for these cooperative forms. It does not establish unanimous agreement about every action, equal coverage of every form in every society, or the correctness of every norm associated with cooperation (Curry, Mullins, and Whitehouse 2019).

4.3 Income, needs and well-being

Well-being research distinguishes judgments about one’s life from reports of emotional experience. Kahneman and Deaton’s 2010 United States data associated higher income with improved life evaluation while finding a plateau in their emotional well-being measure. Killingsworth’s 2021 experience-sampling study found experienced well-being rising with income beyond the earlier threshold (Kahneman and Deaton 2010; Killingsworth 2021).

A 2023 joint reanalysis located the flattening pattern among the least happy participants, while other parts of the happiness distribution showed continued increases. Measurement and variation between people help explain the earlier disagreement. These associations do not establish a universal income threshold or a causal guarantee that raising income produces a particular gain in happiness (Killingsworth, Kahneman, and Mellers 2023).

Income and well-being also raise different questions at individual and national levels, and over different periods (Easterlin 1974). Self-determination theory studies autonomy, competence, and relatedness as basic psychological needs. Its findings support examining several conditions of well-being together; they do not by themselves establish a metaphysical classification of values or a theorem that no gain can compensate for any loss (Ryan and Deci 2000).

4.4 Trust and social outcomes

Economic and social research examines associations between generalised trust, institutions, investment, and growth. Such work makes the consequences of trust available for investigation, but a reported association requires further assumptions or research designs before it establishes a causal effect. The meaning of a survey response about trust also requires care when it is compared with a philosophical account of trustworthiness (Zak and Knack 2001).

4.5 Measuring welfare and development

Economic output measures important aspects of production without measuring every feature of a good life. Work on social progress distinguishes material living standards, health, education, relationships, political participation, environmental conditions, and insecurity. The choice of indicators and their aggregation requires explicit decisions about what the assessment is intended to represent (Stiglitz, Sen, and Fitoussi 2009).

4.6 Specified objectives and artificial intelligence

Artificial intelligence research documents cases in which an agent satisfies a specified objective through behaviour that defeats the designer’s purpose. Specification gaming exposes a mismatch between an objective and the intended outcome. It motivates better specifications, oversight, and evaluation of effects beyond the rewarded signal (Amodei et al. 2016; Krakovna et al. 2020).

Such failure can occur even when only one underlying goal matters and its proxy is incomplete. It is therefore evidence about specification and optimisation, rather than an independent proof of value pluralism. Whether several goods are irreducible requires a further argument.

4.7 Evidence and normative assessment

Science can test claims about learning, health, provision, relationships, institutions, and the consequences of alternative practices. Those results can change what practical judgment recommends. Deciding that a particular outcome should govern judgment also requires an account of why it matters and how competing claims should be treated. Empirical investigation and normative reasoning can therefore inform one another while retaining distinct responsibilities.

5. Comparison of the Answers

The traditions differ both in what they call value and in what they ask an account of value to explain. Comparison is most useful when it follows specific questions about grounds, recognition, development, action, and evidence. A single label such as objective, spiritual, or scientific leaves many of these differences unresolved.

5.2 Essentiality and normative authority

identifies value with essentiality and connects human essentiality with happiness and humane conduct. This provides a positive answer to the question of value’s ground. Hume’s challenge still asks what warrants the move from a description of participation to a requirement to participate in a particular way. An identity proposed by the darshan is an answer to be examined, rather than a conclusion invalidated merely by naming the is–ought problem or established merely by repeating the identity (§7.4).

Natural-law and realist approaches make this comparison less isolated than a contrast with subjectivism would suggest. The substantive questions are which account of human nature is correct, how its normative implications are justified, and whether it can address dependency, disagreement, and institutional failure.

5.3 Activity, properties and evaluative response

The activity account connects value in with understanding, harmonies, and fulfilment. Its distinctive explanatory structure joins an ontology of essential participation to a differentiated account of knowing and an account of orderliness in conduct. It asks what an activity contributes, how that significance is correctly understood, and how its fulfilment is sustained. Qualitative here concerns the meaning of participation and its correspondence with reality. Western theories often ask what properties make an activity, relationship, or outcome good. These descriptions need not exclude one another: an activity can itself be a bearer of value. The sharper disagreement concerns whether essentiality and the specified structure of jeevan explain what makes the activity good and its evaluation correct.

Fitting-attitude theories also distinguish actual approval from appropriate response. The comparison is therefore between accounts of correctness, not between objective value on one side and arbitrary liking on the other. Madhyasth Darshan’s further claim is that correct understanding becomes continuously evidenced through awakened activity.

5.4 Establishment, expression and learning

The one-to-one relation between established values in bal/sthiti and expressed values in gati gives the darshan a specific account of how value is outwardly borne. A learner may participate meaningfully while still developing the understanding that makes fulfilment continuous. Establishment requires the coordination of the whole conscious unit: visualisation at must be informed by contemplation, understanding at , and realisation at atma. Practice, explanation, and self-evaluation can support this development, but an outward performance alone cannot establish the inner achievement. The outward-first educational route remains an interpretation to test, as the Editorial Notes explain.

Advaita likewise gives discipline and conduct a preparatory role in knowledge. Western accounts of character, care, and moral education also investigate changes in the evaluator. The distinctive comparison concerns what counts as establishment, how it is recognised, and what warrants the promised continuity, rather than whether the other traditions permit transformation at all (§7.8).

5.5 Happiness, bliss and material provision

Madhyasth Darshan links four harmonies with cumulative fulfilment: resolution; resolution and prosperity; these with fearlessness; and these with coexistence. Advaita gives liberation a status distinct from produced conditions, while retaining duties and ethical preparation in empirical life. The resulting disagreement concerns the final status of human participation and the relation between fulfilment and worldly order.

The four harmonies are also different from measured pleasure or life satisfaction. Well-being research can investigate related experiences and conditions, but their correspondence requires operational definitions and evidence. The existence of several harmonies alone does not settle commensurability, compensation, or practical trade-offs (§7.1).

5.6 Ordering and value recognition

Scheler and Hartmann show that an objective hierarchy of values is a substantial position within Western philosophy. Madhyasth Darshan specifies dedication between value classes, from usefulness through need, expressed and established values, and human values to jeevan values (MAD, reader p. 34). This is an order of service and purpose. It does not by itself prescribe a numerical exchange rate between classes or establish that additional output compensates for unfulfilled trust. Its further criteria concern enduringness, resolution, development, and participation. A useful comparison examines how the criteria guide concrete decisions and how errors in recognition can be corrected. Neither a hierarchy nor the claim of mature insight alone supplies a complete procedure for resolving disputed obligations (§7.7).

5.7 Useful things, labour and symbolic value

Madhyasth Darshan and Marx both distinguish usefulness from monetary appearance and give labour a central role in production and exchange. Their concepts of value differ. Marx analyses the social form and magnitude of commodity value; the darshan evaluates labour and exchange within a proposed order of need, useful production, prosperity, and right-use. A criticism of exploitation belongs to both accounts, although its explanation and proposed remedy differ.

Usefulness, realised benefit, labour assessment, and price also answer different questions. A commodity’s physical capacity can persist while its availability or the urgency of a person’s need changes. The capability approach further asks whether resources become real opportunities for differently situated people. These distinctions expose concrete questions for a labour-valued exchange order (§§7.5, 7.11).

5.8 Relationships, agreement and conflict

Justice in Madhyasth Darshan requires recognised responsibilities and actual fulfilment before mutual satisfaction. Care and recognition theories offer parallel questions about dependency, responsiveness, and practical standing.

The darshan holds that quarrelling involves error on at least one side and that correctly understanding parties do not quarrel (SB, p. 146). Applying this harmony claim to scarce resources, absent parties, and competing legitimate needs requires an account of the allocation to make (§7.7).

5.9 Qualitative evaluation in two applications

A teacher helping a learner provides a concrete application of the framework. This is an analytical example, not a reported case from the primary texts. The relationship calls for recognition of the learner’s purpose and present receptivity. Guidance concerns development of understanding; care concerns nurture and protection. The distinctions between kindness, grace, and compassion direct attention to whether suitable content, receptivity, or both need to develop (§§1.3–1.4).

A lesson may be enjoyable and its exercises completed correctly while the learner’s understanding remains uncertain. can prefer the activity and can analyse a rehearsed problem; chitran can reproduce an image of the explanation. The fuller assessment concerns whether its meaning is apprehended and understood, whether the learner can explain it, and whether appropriate conduct follows in a new situation. A teacher’s claimed understanding similarly requires intelligible explanation and fulfilled responsibility. These are proposed ways of examining learning; they do not make a successful exercise an external measurement of atma or bodh.

Before teaching, evaluation guides the choice of content and method. After teaching, the contribution is examined against the recognised relationship. Satisfaction matters with that fulfilment: a grateful response alone cannot establish that the learner was helped. Continuing difficulty calls for renewed explanation, better conditions, or correction of an assumption. Qualitative evaluation identifies what the teaching is for and what would fulfil it; quantitative information about time or exercise results helps assess means and outcomes.

The activity account makes the case more specific. The teacher’s strength and its expression through explanation, demonstration, and response concern one continuing participation. Its characteristic conduct may sustain guidance or impose dependence. The resulting understanding or confusion becomes available for the next evaluation. What changes can include the learner’s visualisation, acceptance, or skill, and the teacher’s recognition of what is needed. Human remains happiness through resolution; the correction concerns how it is understood and fulfilled. An evaluation of this case would distinguish the value actually fulfilled from the lesson’s visible completion and the teacher’s intention.

The sixfold account differentiates the responsibilities involved. Niyam directs attention to the conditions and purpose of teaching for understanding; niyantran to whether explanation and correction keep the activity aligned with them; santulan to the relation between demands, receptivity, resources, and the learner’s wider life. concerns the actual teacher–learner relationship and its fulfilled values; dharma concerns resolution and the purpose of learning; concerns the reality that the explanation claims to make understood. These are analytical applications of §1.7. Classroom performance supplies evidence of learning, while a claim to realisation requires its own examination and cannot be established by a successful exercise alone.

A family producing food for its own use and exchange provides a second analytical application. Quantities of land, labour, water, and output concern production, while usefulness concerns what the produce can meet. Assessment of need and productive capacity gives prosperity material content. Labour evaluation addresses exchange; care and rightful receiving address provision for those who cannot presently produce. Soil, water, and other forms of life enter through responsibility for regeneration and nurture (§1.6).

More output is only one fact in the assessment. The framework also asks whether bodily needs are met, labour and its fruits are protected, relationships are fulfilled, and production can continue within regenerative conditions. Dedication of usefulness to higher purposes connects these questions (§1.5). A profitable yield obtained through deception or degrading future productive conditions would leave relevant responsibilities unfulfilled. Specifying sustainable yields, fair labour assessments, or an allocation still requires factual investigation and practical reasoning (§§7.9, 7.11). The qualitative account identifies the purposes against which that evidence is judged.

Here effort, power-expression, and results include cultivation, the use of tools, bodily work, and changes in the produce and soil. Lawful work requires knowledge of those processes; regulation concerns their actual organisation; balance concerns nourishment and regeneration across the affected relations. Justice addresses the people whose labour, provision, and responsibilities are involved, while dharma concerns the production’s place in resolved living. Truth requires that the account remain answerable to the actual activity and its consequences. The same harvest can therefore evidence productive competence and expose relational or ecological failure. Those consequences can occasion correction of the plan, its purpose, or the understanding behind it. Repetition by itself establishes neither correct evaluation nor awakening.

6. Critical Review

6.1 Madhyasth Darshan — participation as the standard of value

Strengths.

  • The account connects saturation and continuous unit activity with essentiality, recognition, responsibility, material provision, and evaluation. Its objectivity claim has definite content in participation: value, its recognition, and its fulfilment concern distinguishable aspects of the same active reality.
  • The five-faculty account explains why attraction, analysis, and skilled performance are insufficient criteria of true value. Meaning, understanding, realisation, and evidence belong to the theory of evaluation itself. The established–expressed correspondence connects this account with learning and continuous conduct.
  • Justice requires normative standards, actual fulfilment, and mutual satisfaction. Kindness, grace, and compassion address need and receptivity; labour evaluation, right-use, and rightful receiving address material conditions.
  • The sixfold articulation of knowledge connects lawful operation and balance with justice, resolution, and truth. It allows technical effectiveness, relational fulfilment, and overall purpose to be examined together while preserving their different responsibilities.
  • Self-examination and evidence in relationships and work supply criteria of evaluation. The texts acknowledge learning within a disordered tradition and distinguish reported achievements from a wider institutional order still to be realised.

Weaknesses.

  • The identity of essentiality and value requires a defence that an interlocutor can assess without first accepting the whole ontology. It remains necessary to explain why a proposed form of participation has normative authority and how competing accounts can be adjudicated.
  • Complete correctness is connected with awakening, while awakening is recognised through correct understanding and conduct. The account has educational entry points, but a public method for resolving disagreement about an individual’s claimed establishment still needs specification.
  • Criteria of higher value and the assertion of harmony do not yet determine the allocation in every case of scarce resources and legitimate competing claims. The named principles must be developed into inspectable decisions without treating disagreement itself as proof of inferior standing.
  • Reports of successful education and living are evidence to investigate, rather than independent demonstrations of universal efficacy. The claims concerning , continuity, and institutional scale need distinct forms of verification.
  • Saturation and constitutional completeness are philosophical commitments whose relation to contemporary physical and biological explanations remains to be established. An activity model can clarify their connections but does not independently verify them or explain a transition to anubhav merely by naming a feedback process.

offers a connected theory of value in participation; its further development depends on publicly assessable warrants and worked applications of its own standards.

6.2 Advaita Vedanta — liberation and the limits of acquisition

Strengths.

  • The distinction between liberation and produced attainments provides a sustained account of why acquisition and pleasurable experience may fail to answer the deepest question of fulfilment.
  • Ethical preparation and action without attachment give the transformation of the evaluator a practical role. Teachings on compassion and loka-sangraha preserve responsibilities within empirical life.
  • The examination of identification challenges the assumption that self-interest is adequately understood as the interests of a presently conceived individual ego.

Weaknesses.

  • The account’s ultimate explanation depends on non-duality and the authority of the means of knowledge through which it is disclosed. Those commitments require argument in a comparison that does not presuppose them.
  • The relation between empirical responsibilities and ultimate non-doership needs careful interpretation when used to guide institutions, conflict resolution, or distribution. The cited teachings give ethical direction without supplying every institutional detail.
  • The claim that produced goods cannot provide ultimate fulfilment does not alone determine their comparative importance within finite lives. Further practical reasoning is needed to assess particular relationships and material needs.

Advaita Vedanta provides a developed account of non-attached action and liberation whose social application must preserve the force of its empirical duties.

6.3 Western value theory — distinctions and competing explanations

Strengths.

  • The distinctions between intrinsic and final value, welfare and moral rightness, ranking and commensurability, and actual and fitting attitudes prevent several different questions from being answered by one ambiguous use of value.
  • Realist, constructivist, phenomenological, care, and capability approaches supply substantive alternatives to simple preference accounts. Evaluator development and the social conditions of fulfilment are well represented within this range.
  • Arguments concerning dignity, exploitation, recognition, and capabilities make distribution and vulnerability central concerns rather than consequences to be considered only after an abstract theory is complete.

Weaknesses.

  • Rival accounts disagree about the grounds of value and the standards of correct response. No single position can be assessed as the settled Western answer.
  • The separation of metaethics, welfare, action, and political economy can leave difficult work in explaining how their results fit together. Conceptual clarity within one inquiry does not automatically produce a coherent account of living.
  • Each proposal must show how its standards are known and applied. Appeals to appropriate attitudes, ideal reasoning, flourishing, or intuition relocate this evidential burden rather than removing it.

Western value theory supplies exact distinctions and substantial rival explanations that a comparative account must address individually.

6.4 The sciences — mechanisms, outcomes and explanatory limits

Strengths.

  • Empirical methods make claims about learning, provision, health, trust, and institutions answerable to evidence beyond an advocate’s testimony.
  • The distinction between reported experience, life evaluation, behaviour, and measured conditions enables more discriminating tests of claims about well-being.
  • Reanalysis and conflicting findings can improve an account, as the income–well-being debate illustrates. This capacity for correction is a resource for studying value education and institutional proposals.

Weaknesses.

  • Operational measures can omit what a philosophical account regards as essential. Neither a reward signal nor a well-being questionnaire automatically measures established relationship values or awakened harmony.
  • Associations do not establish causes, and findings in particular populations do not immediately establish universal regularities. Claims about education or prosperity need appropriate designs and limits.
  • Empirical success in predicting a choice does not settle whether the choice is justified. Selecting and weighting the outcomes of an assessment still requires normative reasoning.

The sciences can test many consequences of a value theory while leaving the justification of its fundamental standards to a wider argument.

7. Open Problems

7.1 What ordering do the values permit?

The darshan gives an explicit order of dedication between value classes as well as higher–lower criteria and four harmonies with cumulative fulfilment (MAD, reader p. 34; KD, p. 112). The remaining problem concerns the decision rules this ordering supports. Dedication identifies the purpose served; developmental order identifies increasing completeness; inclusion identifies what a more comprehensive fulfilment contains. None alone establishes a numerical scale of exchange or a rule for every competing claim. A gain in productive capacity alongside damaged trust is a useful test: what requires repair, how should resources support that repair, and what would count as restored fulfilment?

7.2 How can claimed awakening be assessed without circular authority?

Self-evaluation, examination of both sides of a relationship, study, and observable conduct are already available resources (MVD, p. 87; JV, pp. 98, 139). The accounts of , learning through meaningful participation, and study of meaning supply educational entry points (§1.7). They answer the strongest claim that no learning is possible before complete awakening.

The unresolved question concerns contested authority. When a teacher claims established understanding and a learner finds an apparent error, what shared procedure determines whether the difficulty lies in the teaching, the learner, or the application? The distinction between knowing the more developed and measuring it by an inadequate existing measure allows inquiry; it should not make a claim of development immune to correction (KD, p. 37). Documented explanations, outcomes, counterexamples, and the learner’s freedom to question should be made part of a worked account.

The four harmonies have definite constitutive relations within the darshan (§1.3). Uncertainty about whether an observer has established their presence in a particular person concerns evidence. It does not make those relations indeterminate. Equally, a person’s report of pleasure, calm, satisfaction, or bliss must be interpreted in context before it can establish the specified faculty harmony. A useful assessment should examine the person’s reasons, freedom from contradiction in the relevant activity, fulfilled responsibilities, and continuity across circumstances, while keeping ordinary self-description distinct from the full claim of realised understanding.

7.3 How does mutual satisfaction include everyone affected?

Justice requires recognised relationships, value-fulfilment, evaluation, and mutual satisfaction. It already asks more than whether two people consent. A further account should show how to assess relationships where one person cannot communicate a judgment, lacks relevant information, or depends on the evaluator for care.

The appropriate tests include a young child, an adult requiring continuing support, and an agreement whose effects fall on absent neighbours. Actual provision and fulfilled responsibilities must matter alongside expressed assent. Satisfaction within the immediate pair cannot alone establish that obligations to other affected people have been fulfilled (§1.4).

7.4 What warrants the identification of essentiality and value?

The texts explicitly identify essentiality, essential nature, and value, and connect human with happiness and humane participation (MVD, pp. 47, 124–125; SB, pp. 50, 87). The question is therefore the warrant for a stated identification, rather than the recovery of a missing definition.

A further argument should explain how essential participation is distinguished from a merely recurrent behaviour and why that participation provides a reason for action. It should also explain what would count against an asserted identification. Otherwise a dispute about the good can be redescribed as a dispute about essentiality without making the original disagreement easier to resolve.

The effort–motion–result account makes this question sharper. Effective power and persistent activity occur in both humane and inhumane conduct. The further warrant must concern the character and fulfilment of that activity, rather than its efficacy alone. Likewise, natural lawfulness and balance cannot establish a particular human obligation without the intervening account of relationships, knowledge, and humane purpose (§§1.1, 1.7).

7.5 In what sense is usefulness constant?

The primary passages contain a difference that a complete account must address. JV pp. 137–138 describes object value as constant through the example of sesame and its usefulness. MAD reader p. 51 explicitly calls usefulness and beauty temporal (samayik), referring to changing needs, use, exchange, and deployment, and contrasts them with enduring established, human, and values. The task is therefore first textual: identify the sense and conditions of each claim before proposing their reconciliation.

Distinguishing a thing’s capacities, its transformation through labour, the benefit realised in a situation, and its price may help analyse this difference. A quantity of grain can retain a nutritive capacity while access, condition, and need alter its significance. These distinctions are analytical proposals; the passages cited do not expressly supply them as their reconciliation. A fuller treatment should test the proposed reading against both passages and further uses of these terms. The general assessment of usefulness in §1.6 therefore does not assume an unconditional constancy claim.

7.6 How complete are the value lists and pairings?

The nine principal established relationship values and their nine corresponding expressions have explicit primary support in MAD reader p. 30. The principal classification need not remain uncertain because JV p. 108 introduces its list with illustrative wording. The remaining work concerns the relation of this classification to other contextual lists and to the larger activity enumeration.

The 122 activity members do not establish 122 distinct names or 122 separate relationship values. A concordance should preserve the difference between a value class, an activity position, a paired expression, and evidence in conduct. Repeated terms such as generosity and contextual uses of happiness or bliss require definitions and context. A named position at one faculty also cannot be read as a claim that understanding the corresponding value needs that faculty alone (§§1.2–1.3).

7.7 How are rival legitimate obligations resolved?

The claim that correct parties do not quarrel supplies the darshan’s position on harmony (SB, p. 146). Recognition, mutual fulfilment, and common resolution are its proposed direction. The difficult case is a conflict between legitimate needs when existing means are insufficient: two patients need the only available treatment, or protecting one community shifts a burden to another.

A worked account should identify relevant facts, responsibilities, available alternatives, and the reason for its allocation. It should distinguish a remediable error from a material limit that remains after misunderstanding is corrected. This would show how the harmony claim guides action and how unavoidable loss is acknowledged, rather than treating the existence of disagreement as a completed diagnosis.

7.8 How does outward learning become establishment and continuity?

The texts connect sanskar, understood meaning, practice, self-evaluation, and the acceptance of evidenced conduct with further expression (MVD, pp. 83, 90, 94; KD, pp. 84, 112–113). They distinguish intermittent meaningful conduct from continuity. These resources support a positive educational account across the activities of jeevan, including the learner’s visualisation, desire, understanding, and resolve.

The further task is to document the transition. What differentiates instructed performance, independently understood fulfilment, establishment in state, and continuity across changing circumstances? How are relapse, conflicting incentives, and mistaken self-assessment recognised? A possible outward-first route should be examined as a pedagogical interpretation, without presenting it as a prescribed sequence or repetition as a sufficient cause of awakening. Evidence should include the learner’s explanation and self-examination as well as conduct and its effects, while distinguishing claims about each faculty from what a particular assessment can establish.

The continuity from action through evaluation to revised understanding also requires discrimination between several outcomes. A learner can correct a technique, recognise a responsibility, clarify a concept, or claim anubhav; each calls for appropriate evidence. An account of qualitative refinement should explain how such changes affect subsequent participation while preserving the distinction between jeevan’s constitutional completeness and the development of its activity.

7.9 How does value across the four orders guide ecological decisions?

The darshan attributes value to every unit, directs humans towards nurture, and relates resource use to regeneration. It also describes vitalising and devitalising effects in relation to living bodies, including human bodies (SB, pp. 141, 179–182, 200–201). These commitments cannot be reduced either to unrestricted human preference or to a fully specified theory of non-human rights.

Further work should examine habitat loss, competing forms of life, restoration times, and effects on future people. The account must explain how participation and regeneration are assessed when there is no single immediate beneficiary and when one useful activity disrupts another form of coexistence.

7.10 What evidence supports the educational and institutional claims?

Self-examination, fulfilled relationships, useful work, and reported educational results supply different kinds of evidence. Nagraj’s accounts include learning and self-experimentation, while the discussion of exchange in Adhyatmvad distinguishes reported achievements in production, prosperity, and justice from the wider exchange order still awaited at the time of writing (JV, pp. 98, 139; SB, p. 294; AVD, p. 52).

These historical reports should not be presented as proof that the full institutional order was implemented or as a statement of its present reach. Longitudinal studies could examine learning, sustained conduct, provision, and relationships, with explicit measures and comparison conditions. Institutional evaluation should separately examine distribution, ecological effects, dependency, and the ability to correct failures. Such research would test specified claims without assuming that a questionnaire exhausts jeevan or that inward evaluation forbids external investigation.

7.11 How should labour be evaluated and provision distributed?

Labour deployment, labour-value assessment, protection from exploitation, and profit-and-loss-free exchange are explicit commitments (MVD, pp. 260, 268–269; SB, p. 237). The next questions concern their implementation: how should differences of skill, effort, training, risk, tools, time, quality, and productivity enter an assessment? What happens when socially necessary care yields no exchangeable product, or when a useful product depends on accumulated knowledge contributed by many people?

Remuneration, gift, and award already widen the account of rightful wealth beyond an individual’s immediate production (AVD, p. 170). A developed allocation procedure should explain provision for children, older people, people with disabilities, and anyone temporarily unable to work. It should also distinguish the evaluation of labour from the urgency of need and from the basic standing of the person who receives support.

7.12 How are equal human standing and unequal development related?

The account recognises common human purpose and capacity while distinguishing levels of understanding and conduct. Developmental differences can matter when assigning a task or assessing competence. They do not by themselves justify differences in basic worth, protection, or the right to question a judgment.

A full treatment should show how conferred rights and duties are applied to less experienced learners and people whose capacities differ. It should make clear how recognition and care protect a person’s standing while their understanding or productive ability is still developing. This is especially important wherever claims of higher value or awakening carry authority within a family, educational setting, or institution.

8. Conclusion

presents value as the qualitative significance of essential participation in coexistence. Every unit has value through its essential nature and . Human awakening concerns the understanding and fulfilment of this value, with evaluation answerable to what the relationship requires. The distinction between inherent value, its recognition, and the capacity to fulfil it makes both error and education intelligible within the account.

Saturation grounds the strength and power of units, whose effort, motion, and result are inseparable aspects of continuous activity. Qualitative development concerns how this activity is understood and expressed. Chetana as actionless knowledge, chaitanya as the sentient unit, and anubhav as the human realisation of coexistence have distinct roles in the explanation. Law, regulation, balance, justice, resolution, and truth connect the understanding of value with the work and relationships through which orderliness is sustained.

Its distinctive treatment joins this ontology to the whole activity of . Tasting and selection at , and deliberation and analysis at , receive their direction through the meaning contemplated and visualised at , understood at , and realised at atma. Established values and their paired expressions connect understanding with conduct; justice assesses actual fulfilment and mutual satisfaction. Character, ethics, useful production, and the five social systems give these purposes continuing practical content. The ordering of value classes directs material means towards humane living and the harmonies of jeevan.

Happiness, peace, contentment, and bliss make the structural character of this account explicit. They name the felt harmonies of specified faculties, with resolution, just thought, freedom from attachment, and understanding as their content. Enduring relationship values connect those harmonies with responsibilities whose fulfilment can be recognised, evaluated, and experienced. The formal appendix expresses these connections as qualitative relations and distinguishes their definiteness within the theory from the evidence needed to establish a particular instance.

This framework addresses grounds, qualitative meaning, knowledge, evaluative error, motivation, fulfilment, and continuity within a connected account. Its breadth is a philosophical contribution to examine through definitions and applications. Advaita, Western theories, and the sciences sharpen the questions of normative warrant, disputed authority, dependency, allocation, and public evidence. Further work must distinguish demonstrated learning from claimed continuity, examine the textual difference concerning usefulness, and show how the principles guide difficult decisions. Such inquiry tests the promised orderliness by the understanding and participation through which the darshan itself says value is evidenced.

A.3 Orderliness, continuity and evidence

Overall human orderliness connects realised understanding, inner harmony, just relationships, and purposeful work. The wider field of work requires law, regulation, and balance, while human participation is examined through justice, , and truth (MVD, pp. 80, 137, 156, 174). The conjunction of these responsibilities is structural: a gain in production does not by itself repair an unfulfilled relationship, and a person’s inward assurance does not itself provide the material means another person needs. A complete assessment must examine each relevant relation and its fulfilment.

Effort, motion, and result describe the continuing activity in which these relations are expressed. An occasion of fulfilled participation and evaluation can clarify the basis of later activity; a failure can disclose a mistaken recognition, inadequate means, or a contradiction requiring resolution. Such changes concern understanding and conduct, while constitutional completeness and the human purpose of happiness remain the reference (§§1.1–1.2, 1.4). The present formalisation constrains what counts as fulfilled value. It does not supply a transition equation predicting every choice, the time required for learning, or the occurrence of anubhav. Structural definiteness of value is a different claim from a complete deterministic law of development; leaving the latter unspecified does not assert randomness in the former.

Editorial Notes

Texts, translations and page conventions

This revision uses a targeted comparison of the primary passages cited below, including Hindi where English wording or pagination changes the argument. It is not a complete collation of Nagraj’s corpus. MVD, SB, JV, and AVD page numbers denote PDF positions in the linked files; MVD’s visible page footer agrees with that convention. In JV the printed number is one lower than the PDF position. SB’s retained contents pagination differs from the bilingual PDF pagination.

KD citations use printed body pages and chapter numbers where specified. The Hindi body page is twenty-five below its PDF position. The working translation’s [p. N] markers introduce the page. Front-matter numbering must be identified separately because it overlaps with body numbering. KD is a working English translation, and AVD is also provisional; neither status is removed by its use here. Important activity and continuity passages in KD pp. 37, 84, and 112–113 were checked against the Hindi page images. MAD denotes the Hindi Manav Abhyas Darshan in the linked native reader. Its references use the displayed page numbers in the 222-page version, not PDF positions or assumed printed-page offsets. The English formulations here paraphrase the Hindi; they are not attributed to an English edition.

Essentiality, activity and the enumeration

The identification of essentiality with value and its connection with essential nature and are explicit in MVD pp. 47, 124–125, SB pp. 50, 87, and AVD p. 184. The exposition therefore retains this relation without the additional mechanism that form and properties must first align with the other aspects to produce value.

MVD p. 203 uses bhav- broadly for values and evaluation. MVD p. 338 and AVD p. 92 also identify a particular bhav/samveg pair. The shared term does not make all value-activity one position at , nor does the pairing establish a universal causal sequence from sentiment to every outward action. The 122-member count in MVD p. 323 and AVD p. 91 concerns awakened activities arranged in 61 pairs; it does not establish 122 distinct names or classify every pair as established and expressed relationship values.

Established and expressed values

The exposition adopts the one-to-one correspondence of established values in bal/sthiti and expressed, civic, or shishta values in gati. The table retains the paired positions in AVD pp. 91–94 and the definitions in MVD pp. 328–348. The educational formulation that outward expressions can be learnt before establishment in state is an interpretive articulation of the learning route, read with the distinction of state and motion and the accounts of education and evidenced conduct (KD, pp. 18–19, 84, 112–113; SB, pp. 237, 294). It is not presented as a newly located verbatim sentence or a necessary sequence for every learner.

MAD reader p. 30 explicitly identifies the principal nine established values, their corresponding nine expressions, trust as common value, and love as complete value. This supplies direct classification evidence beyond JV p. 108, which introduces the nine relationship values as examples. KD p. 26 uses an illustrative list including daya, sneha, udarta, gaurav, adar, vatsalya, shraddha, prem, and kritagyata. Six Hindi terms coincide directly with the JV list; adar and samman should not silently be counted as identical. The principal nine-value classification is retained; the illustrative KD list is not silently substituted for it or treated as proof that the principal taxonomy is uncertain. This does not assert that every occurrence of mulya throughout the corpus belongs to those nine values. Naturalness, concordance, humility, and devoutness are running English terms for the relevant expressed values; ordinary English associations do not exhaust their source definitions.

Activity, saturation and the four aspects

The effort–motion–result alignment follows SB pp. 60–62. The text places effort/strength with dharma and svabhava, motion with svabhava and guna, and result with roop and amaratva. §1.1 presents the four-aspect correspondence; amaratva is retained here with its surrounding constitutional-completeness context, rather than generalised into the immortality of every present material configuration. The overlap of svabhava means this is not a one-to-one derivation or a three-stage chronology. No physical potential, numerical force law, or transition equation is inferred from it.

The account of relative power and characteristic participation draws on Technical Note: Four Aspects of the Active Unit, in its discussions of unit activity, the four aspects, order-wise examples, and the limits of a process interpretation. Its rendering of usefulness as functional significance is analytical and does not make every actual effect morally good. The interpretation of dharma as order-specific innateness must distinguish change within an order from a transition between orders. The human case also distinguishes the constitution of from its current understanding and conduct.

Chetana, chaitanya and knowledge

MVD p. 35 names knowledge as chetana in relation to chaitanya; pp. 74–75 distinguish actionless, non-transforming consciousness/knowledge from transformations in nature and identify chaitanya as constitutionally complete jeevan. The exposition does not treat as becoming a unit, performing evaluation, or receiving anubhav. Knowledge as omnipresent ground and knowledge realised and evidenced in human living are connected uses, not an additional substance and its transmission. Sentience includes evaluation but is not exhaustively defined by it, and constitutional completeness does not guarantee awakened evaluation. SB pp. 63–64 and 249 supply the distinctions between natural mutuality, sentient capacities, and correct human recognition and fulfilment.

The faculties, qualitative refinement and evidence

The five-faculty table follows KD §3.8, p. 84’s Hindi account of sthiti/gati. In AVD pp. 91–94 the English strength/power headings are reversed relative to the Hindi; §1.2 follows the checked Hindi relation. names the faculty, chitran its visualising activity, and chintan its contemplative activity. The integrated exposition describes dependencies of meaning and expression; it does not posit a neural mechanism or a fixed temporal passage through five successive compartments.

MVD p. 83 presents interdependent relations among language, meaning, evaluation, worldview, and the perceiver’s capacities, returning to language. It is not treated as a linear causal sequence that generates value. Likewise, the realisation–behaviour–application evidences (MVD, p. 12) and the knowledge–law–regulation–balance–justice–dharma–truth passage (p. 174) connect understanding and living without making their terms interchangeable outside that argument. MAD reader p. 45 grounds the account of aapyayan and parimarjan; neither is equated with memorisation or habit formation alone.

MVD pp. 99–100 distinguish realisation-oriented understanding in study from realisation-based understanding and its expression. Page 156 connects atma with realisation, –chitta with resolution, and vritti– with just behaviour. These relations are not exclusive ownership of justice, dharma, or truth by individual faculties: all three are accepted in buddhi and participate in contemplation and deliberation. The eight dimensions of visualisation on p. 327 describe an activity of chitta, not the production of the imaged unit by a perceiver.

Sixfold knowledge and continuing evaluation

The continuity of knowledge, law, regulation, balance, justice, dharma, and truth is directly stated in MVD p. 174, with evidence of all six through realisation on p. 80. The natural-order conformance descriptions follow SB pp. 236–237. Their particular illustrations do not allocate each term exclusively to one order. Dharma as innateness and dharma as the humane perspective of resolution in thought are related uses whose distinctions remain necessary (MVD, pp. 125, 137, 156, 174).

A State-Dynamic Model of Coexistence, §§1–3, 7–8, and 10–11, develops the analytical connection between unit activity, human orientation, consequences, and later evaluation.

The present paper uses that connection to clarify learning and the examples in §5.9. The distinction of actual and operatively recognised mutuality, the grouping of consequences into internal, bodily, relational, and natural domains, and the sixfold practical questions are analytical reconstructions. They do not enlarge configurational parinam into a source-stated definition of every human consequence, or make feedback a sufficient cause of anubhav.

The model’s grouping into two coupled triads and its projections of niyam–, niyantran–dharma, and santulan– are likewise analytical, non-exclusive mappings. They are not adopted as primary-text identities or faculty assignments. A family or institution is an arrangement of persons, relationships, and work; the evaluators are the participating human beings. No collective jeevan or new ontological unit is inferred from social orderliness.

Harmonies and contextual uses of jeevan values

The faculty-harmony account in JV p. 138 and MVD p. 327, and the cumulative fulfilment in KD §3.12, p. 112, supply the two columns of the harmony table. The social column preserves the cumulative sequence; it does not assign fearlessness or coexistence alone as an exclusive outward equivalent. Their distinctness supports separate examination, but does not alone prove the impossibility of every aggregate assessment. The formal appendix uses a profile of distinct relations because the cited texts supply no numerical scale or exchange rates.

The person–family–society–world profile in §A.1 synthesises the individual and family relation in AVD p. 228, the social purpose of fearlessness in MVD p. 108, the levels of participation in MVD p. 161, and the extension of just living to universal orderliness in JV pp. 53 and 62. JV p. 62 directly connects inner satisfaction with self-governed conduct across the levels of living; AVD p. 228 describes jeevan and human expectations as mutually evidencing, and KD pp. 112–113 connects their fulfilment through projection and reflection. The profile is analytical notation for these connected claims. It does not replace KD’s cumulative content with four isolated correspondences, or make a family or society the bearer of a separate collective jeevan. Global coexistence denotes human participation in an already coexistent reality.

MVD p. 328 locates the named anand activity at buddhi, while p. 323 also uses bliss language at atma. The table follows the specific named positions rather than asserting an exclusive location for every use of bliss. KD p. 37 also speaks of resolution and prosperity as jeevan values in its context. The fourfold list is retained where the four harmonies are the subject; contextual lists are not silently expanded or reduced to force one enumeration. Contentment in MVD p. 331 includes readiness for production beyond need as well as absence of deficiency-sentiment.

MAD reader p. 47 explicitly equates absence of opposition in each adjacent faculty pair with a named value and with a quality of behaviour, thought, desire, or understanding. This strengthens the constitutive reading adopted in §§1.3 and A.1. Its aparadh-hin is rendered behaviour free of wrongdoing; it is not confined to compliance with a prevailing penal code. Page 51 connects the four harmonies with the experience of established values and describes established values as enduring across time and place. The study’s term structural names these definite relations of activity, rather than a claim about measured anatomy or a new spatial arrangement of the jeevan atom.

MVD pp. 343 and 346 distinguish sensory pleasantness from happiness defined as resolution. The pedagogical progression from pleasant contact to resolved thought, an understood whole, and realised existence is therefore developed through the specified faculty relations, not adopted as four independent satisfactions. Absence of confusion alone is insufficient if the person’s beliefs misrecognise reality; merely drawing a comprehensive image is insufficient for chitta–buddhi harmony. The discussion of planning connects the already cited activities and contentment definition without treating every successful plan as proof of santosh. Feeling the four harmonies is attributed to jeevan, while their named positions remain distinct; the asvadan activity at mun is not made the exclusive location of all four.

The appendix’s harmony predicates, joint profile, relationship requirements, and conjunction for justice are analytical notation. Their substantive content comes from the cited definitions, not from the symbols themselves. They distinguish constitutive definiteness, an actual person’s state, and an observer’s evidence. No stochastic rule, numerical utility function, automatic passage from logical consistency to fulfilment, or deterministic law of awakening is attributed to the primary texts.

Evaluation errors and non-attachment

The Hindi sentence on correct evaluation in MVD p. 203 names under- and over-evaluation, whereas its English adds mis-evaluation. The triadic account in §1.5 is therefore cited to MVD pp. 152, 306 and SB p. 146. MVD p. 306 prints nirmulyan (निर्मूल्यन); occurrences of amulyan elsewhere should retain their own source wording. MVD pp. 198–199 distinguishes ativyapti as more, avyapti as other than, and anavyapti as less. English list order is not a safe basis for matching these terms.

The headword translated as dispassion in MVD p. 152 is nirakarshan (निराकर्षण). The exposition uses that headword. It should not be substituted for vairagya in Advaita or for KD’s distinct uses of virag, vairagya, and para-vairagya. The related discussion of non-doership concerns wrongful action, while KD p. 37 describes completeness as continuity of activity.

Human development and relational fulfilment

MVD pp. 60–61 fixes the humane and higher-humane triads and the conditions distinguishing kindness, grace, and compassion. SB p. 180 reverses kindness and compassion in the English ordering relative to its Hindi; the exposition follows the checked definitions. SB p. 189 defines heenta through deceit, fraud, duplicity, and hypocrisy. That evaluative term should not be read as a person’s disability or lower productive capacity.

The three motives in MVD p. 319 are eshanas, not the four purusharthas of the Advaita comparison. MVD p. 250’s account of sibling evaluation concerns a pre-adolescent child in an awakened family; it does not establish a universal first onset of evaluation. The six educational merits in JV pp. 101, 140 count personality and talent separately.

The relationship names in MVD p. 190 and the purposes in JV pp. 108–109 are retained without inventing a seven-to-six one-to-one purpose table. SB p. 237 compresses the justice sequence; MVD pp. 311, 336 supplies the four-part formulation used in §1.4.

Production, exchange and historical evidence

MVD p. 56’s endeavour-until-success definition is prayog, translated there as experimentation and rendered application here; the following definition concerns production. MVD pp. 58, 67’s higher-for-lower exchange explains acquisitive profit. Affirmative ascent is grounded separately in pp. 43, 110, 126 and 219–222.

MAD reader p. 34 supplies the order of dedication used in §1.5; this is not converted into an unquoted compensation or scalar-ranking theorem. SB p. 237’s Hindi differentiates evaluation in justice, labour-value assessment in exchange, and labour deployment in production; its English does not fully reproduce that paragraph.

The extended fear-and-temptation discussion is SB chapter 9, item 11, PDF pp. 290–295, ending before item 12 on p. 295. It is not chapter 11 beginning at PDF p. 199. Its political and educational claims, like MVD p. 199’s account of sentient atoms, are treated as the darshan’s positions rather than independently verified scientific or historical facts. AVD p. 52 is a dated report distinguishing experienced production and justice from the wider exchange system then still awaited.

Comparative terms and verification

Value in a theory of normative properties, value as essentiality, economic commodity value, and a neural decision variable are not interchangeable senses. The comparisons examine relations between the accounts without treating a shared English word as proof of a shared concept.

BG 3.8 concerns prescribed action and bodily maintenance; 3.19 supplies the explicit instruction to act without attachment. The Taittiriya bliss teaching is cited to 2.7–2.9 as well as the preceding inward inquiry, rather than identifying the anandamaya sheath with the absolute without qualification. The Vivekachudamani is used as a pedagogical witness traditionally attributed to Shankara, with its disputed authorship retained.

The scientific discussion separates reward prediction error from decision valuation, cross-cultural regularity from exceptionless moral agreement, association from causation, and proxy failure from a proof of irreducible plurality. The 2010 income finding is read with the 2021 study and 2023 reanalysis. The critical questions concern the passages and proposals examined here; they do not assert that the wider literature contains no further treatment of education, constitution, economics, or institutional practice.
