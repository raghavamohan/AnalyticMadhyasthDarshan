          Axiology: Value Theory

          Author: AnalyticMadhyasthDarshan.org — a group of people studying philosophy. Source repository: raghavamohan/AnalyticMadhyasthDarshan.

          Edited on: September 7, 2026, 9:38 AM IST

          The question: How does value belong to a unit as activity in coexistence, and how is it understood, established, and fulfilled in human living? What forms does value take, what endures or changes with time, and in what senses can values be compared? How does the Co-existentialism of Shri A. Nagraj compare with Advaita Vedanta, modern Western value theory, and the natural and social sciences?

          This study examines value, evaluation, and the ordering of values in Madhyasth Darshan (Co-existentialism), as presented by Shri A. Nagraj. Its central problem is how value belongs to active units in coexistence and takes different forms in human living. It asks what remains definite through changing circumstances, how those forms are related, and what warrants comparison and correct evaluation.

          The Ontology of Coexistence supplies the account of Omnipresence, units, form, properties, essential nature, , the four orders, and . The Epistemology of Coexistence examines knowing, believing, recognising, fulfilling, and evaluating. Axiology connects that ground and those activities with the forms of mulya: the harmonies of jeevan, humane conduct, relationship values and their expressions, and usefulness, aesthetic qualification, and symbolic valuation in things. Recognition and learning concern how their meanings become understood and reliably fulfilled.

          Ethics, aesthetics, prosperity, family relationships, and education develop particular applications of this account. Axiology must nevertheless explain their common basis. It cannot leave labour, material provision, or educational practice outside its argument when fulfilment and evaluation depend on them. The comparison with Advaita, Western philosophy, and science tests both this explanatory reach and the warrant for its claims.

          1. The Madhyasth Darshan Answer

          holds that value (mulya) is grounded in existence as coexistence (). Value belongs to the active unit as its essentiality (maulikta) and participation in orderliness (vyavastha mein bhaagidari). This participation has a definite content through the unit’s essential nature and innateness, whether human beings recognise it correctly or mistake it. Understanding value therefore concerns both what the unit is and how its activity participates in mutuality.

          In human living, value is understood, experienced, and evaluated through the conscious self, . Values and evaluation are sentiment activity (bhav-): activities of understanding, feeling, and fulfilment whose correctness depends on awakening. Human living unfolds through realisation (anubhav), thought (vichar), behaviour (vyavahar), and work (karya). Thirty core values articulate its fulfilment, from the harmonies of jeevan and humane character to relationships and useful things. Awakening brings understanding and its expression into accordance with reality and makes their fulfilment continuous in living (MVD, p. 203; PS, pp. 151, 154).

          1.1 The active unit and value as participation

          Essentiality concerns the unit’s characteristic participation and the innateness it bears in its order of existence. It belongs to the unit through its essential nature (swabhav) and , and remains the basis of evaluation through correct or mistaken recognition (MVD, pp. 47, 124–125; SB, pp. 50, 87).

          “Entire beingness implies the essentiality of units in every plane and order. Essentiality refers to value.” — SB, p. 50

          Every existing unit is a definite whole of activity. It is characterised by four inseparable aspects: form (roop), properties (guna), essential nature (swabhav), and innateness (dharma). Form concerns its bounded configuration; properties concern its relative powers and their effects in mutuality; essential nature concerns the characteristic conduct and usefulness of those powers in its order; dharma concerns its inseparable innateness. A quantitative description measures dimensions, amounts, or rates. Understanding essentiality identifies what the activity amounts to in participation, such as integration, nourishment, protection, or resolution. This qualitative significance is the subject of value (MVD, p. 47; SB, pp. 60–62, 87; AVD, p. 184).

          The unit’s activeness is grounded in its saturation in Omnipresence, : the all-pervading ground in which units exist and remain active. The Ontology of Coexistence, §1.2, develops saturation and effort–motion–result. Strength (bal) borne in state (sthiti) and power (shakti) expressed in motion (gati) are inseparable aspects of the unit’s activity. Effort is aligned with dharma and essential nature, motion with essential nature and properties, and result with form. These relations concern the same continuing activity in mutuality (MVD, pp. 35, 40; SB, pp. 60–62).

          The four orders differ in their characteristic participation. The material order manifests integration and disintegration. The pranic order adds growth and vitalising or devitalising conduct (sarak–marak). Animal living joins body and jeevan, with the hope to live and cruel or uncruel conduct (krur–akrur). The knowledge order brings the human possibility of understanding and the purpose of happiness. Formation and dissolution both participate in natural orderliness; favourable or adverse effects on a body depend on the mutuality involved. A plant’s essentiality, for example, remains definite even where its effects are poisonous to a particular organism (MVD, pp. 51–52; SB, pp. 179–182, 249; §7.9).

          Human essential nature is expressed through the human values of fortitude, courage, generosity, kindness, grace, and compassion; human dharma is happiness. Because human conduct is mediated by understanding rather than biological instinct alone, human beings can act inhumanely under delusion or humanely through understanding. Yet throughout all attempts to attain resolution, human dharma remains happiness, and human value remains the conscious fulfilment of orderliness in oneself, in society, and with nature (MVD, pp. 15, 97, 115, 125; SB, pp. 179–182, 249).

          Essentiality is already present in reality. Human labour can configure physical material into useful instruments, and human study can establish understanding in consciousness. Both activities take place within an existence whose units already possess inherent essentiality and mutuality (MVD, pp. 56, 83, 124–125; SB, p. 87).

          1.2 The structure of value in jeevan: values as sentiment-activities

          Jeevan is the constitutionally complete, conscious unit whose faculties are atma, , , , and . Their activities include realisation, understanding, contemplation, deliberation, and tasting. Values inherent in jeevan are understood, accepted, felt, evaluated, and expressed in living. Usefulness belongs to things, while human experience and evaluation of value belong to jeevan. Values and evaluation are sentiment activity, bhav-kriya, which can proceed through awakening or delusion (MVD, p. 203; JV, pp. 137–139).

          Actionless, changeless knowledge is named chetana in relation to sentient nature, chaitanya. Realisation and the understanding of value belong to the activity of jeevan saturated in that ground. The Epistemology of Coexistence, §1.1, develops this distinction (MVD, pp. 35, 74–75, 97–100; SB, pp. 63–64).

          The distinction between state (sthiti) and motion (gati) gives value an active structure within each faculty. State is the strength (bal) borne in the activity; motion is its meaningful expression as power (shakti). In awakened jeevan, these paired activities operate in harmony (KD, §3.8, p. 84):

          Faculty Activity in state / strength (bal) Activity in motion / power (shakti)
          Atma Realisation (anubhav) of coexistence Evidence (praman) in living
          Buddhi Understanding (bodh) of actuality, reality, and truth Resolve (sankalp) to express understanding
          Chitta Contemplation (chintan), with direct apprehension (sakshatkar) of justice, dharma, and truth Visualisation (chitran) of meaningful forms
          Vritti Deliberation (tulan) in justice, dharma, and truth Analysis (vishleshan) of relations and means
          Mun Tasting (asvadan) the accepted value Selection (chayan) for its meaningful fulfilment

          Tasting (asvadan) at mun has sensory, value-based, and goal-based forms: ruchi-mulak, mulya-mulak, and lakshya-mulak. Sensory tasting concerns sound, touch, form, taste, and smell through bodily contact. Value-based tasting concerns the fulfilment of values in recognised relationships. Goal-based tasting concerns the purpose towards which activity is directed; awakening makes the human goal definite as resolution, prosperity, fearlessness, and coexistence. Development changes the governing orientation from sensory attraction towards the fulfilment of relationships and the comprehensive human goal. Sensory enjoyment thereby finds its place within understood value and purpose. Learning to recognise relationships and their purposes changes what is accepted and relished in living (PS, pp. 40–41; MVD, pp. 124, 203, 343, 346; JV, pp. 138–140).

          Happiness (sukh), peace (shanti), contentment (santosh), and bliss (anand) name harmonies between the activities of adjacent faculties. Concurrence (samatva) or absence of opposition (nirvirodh) has a specified content in behaviour, thought, desire, and understanding. The value is the harmony lived and felt in those activities (MVD, pp. 327–328, 331, 333, 346; JV, p. 138; MAD, reader p. 47):

          Jeevan value Harmony between faculties Experiential content Cumulative fulfilment in living
          Happiness (sukh) Mun and vritti Resolution with behaviour free of wrongdoing (aparadh-hin) Resolution (samadhan)
          Peace (shanti) Vritti and chitta Thought free of injustice (anyaya-hin) Resolution and prosperity (samriddhi)
          Contentment (santosh) Chitta and buddhi Desire free of attachment (asakti-hin) Resolution, prosperity, and fearlessness (abhay)
          Bliss (anand) Buddhi and atma Understanding free of ignorance (avidya-hin) Resolution, prosperity, fearlessness, and coexistence (sah-astitva)

          The cumulative fulfilment connects these harmonies with human living: each wider fulfilment retains the preceding content (KD, §3.12, p. 112; The Ontology of Coexistence, §1.15). Contentment includes freedom from the sentiment of lack together with readiness to evidence production beyond need through science aligned with wisdom. Its paired expression is abundance, shree. Bliss is borne at buddhi; ultimate bliss (paramanand) belongs to realisation in atma, with authenticity () as its expression and evidence. The concurrence of atma and Omnipresence is eternally present, while realisation brings that reality into awakened living (MVD, pp. 327–328, 331; JV, p. 138).

          In unawakened living, evaluation at vritti is dominated by the sensory and acquisitive perspectives of pleasant–unpleasant (priya), beneficial–harmful (hit), and profit–loss (labh). These orient consciousness toward bodily gratification and accumulation. In awakened consciousness, these three perspectives are subordinated to justice–injustice (), orderliness/resolution (dharma), and truth as coexistence (). Under humane understanding, sensory pleasure and bodily health find their legitimate, balanced place within justice, while prosperity replaces profit as the economic purpose (MVD, pp. 58, 66, 336–337; JV, pp. 139–140).

          Correct evaluation involves the coordinated activity of the whole jeevan. Contemplation and direct apprehension at chitta concern the reality whose meaning is visualised; understanding at buddhi supplies certainty and resolve; realisation at atma grounds its evidence in living. Deliberation, analysis, tasting, and selection express that understood meaning. A person can visualise a desirable result and calculate how to obtain it while misunderstanding the relationship involved. Constitutional completeness is jeevan’s settled constitution, activity completeness its freedom from delusion, and conduct completeness the continuity of that understanding in living (MVD, pp. 83, 99–100, 156, 203; KD, pp. 37, 84).

          1.3 The canon of thirty values and the four dimensions of living

          The canon of thirty values (tees mulya) comprises four jeevan values, six human values, nine established values, nine expressed values, and two object values. Their principal expression maps onto the four dimensions of living: realisation (anubhav), thought (vichar), behaviour (vyavahar), and work (karya). Jeevan values articulate experiential fulfilment in realisation-based living; human values give thought its humane orientation; established and expressed values articulate relationships in behaviour; object values are established and fulfilled through work with material things (PS, pp. 78, 93, 149, 151, 154, 179; MVD, pp. 18, 125, 137, 156, 306; JV, p. 138).

          Value category Count Specific values Principal dimension of living
          Jeevan values 4 Happiness (sukh), peace (shanti), contentment (santosh), bliss (anand) Realisation (anubhav): harmony and experienced fulfilment
          Human values (manav mulya) 6 Fortitude (dhirata), courage (veerta), generosity (udarta); kindness (daya), grace (kripa), compassion (karuna) Thought (vichar): humane disposition and direction of participation
          Established values (sthapit mulya) 9 Trust, respect, affection, care, guidance, reverence, glory, gratitude, love Behaviour (vyavahar): understood values in relationships
          Expressed values (shishta mulya) 9 Concordance, cordiality, dedication, generosity, naturalness, devoutness, simplicity, humility, non-otherness Behaviour (vyavahar): corresponding expressions in conduct
          Object values (vastu mulya) 2 Usefulness value (upyogita mulya), aesthetic value (kala/saundarya mulya) Work (karya): useful and aesthetically qualified material provision

          These dimensions form one connected activity of living. Realisation grounds understanding and sustains the harmony of jeevan; thought directs participation; behaviour fulfils relationships; work supplies and refines the material means of fulfilment. Care for a child, for example, involves the caregiver’s understood purpose, humane intention, relational conduct, and useful provision together. The map identifies the principal role of each value group within this coordinated whole (MVD, pp. 56, 94, 137, 156; JV, pp. 137–139; PS, pp. 149, 179).

          Human values articulate the essential nature of humane character. The humane triad comprises fortitude (dhirata), firmness in justice; courage (veerta), actively making justice accessible to others; and generosity (udarta), dedicating body, mind, and wealth to awakening, health, and prosperity. The higher-humane triad concerns assistance appropriate to another’s receptivity and available content or means. Kindness (daya) makes the appropriate content available where receptivity is present; grace (kripa) develops humane receptivity where the content is available; compassion (karuna) provides both where both are lacking. Love encompasses their combined expression and its continuity (MVD, pp. 52, 60–61, 125, 331, 340).

          Established values (sthapit mulya) are borne as active strengths (bal) in state; expressed values (shishta mulya) are their corresponding powers (shakti) in motion. Their correspondence specifies how each value is expressed in conduct. The nine established relationship values and their nine paired expressions occupy definite positions across the faculties of jeevan (MAD, reader p. 30; JV, p. 108; AVD, pp. 91–94; MVD, pp. 331–340):

          Established value in state / strength (bal) Corresponding expressed value in motion / power (shakti) Faculty position Meaning of the established value and its expression
          Trust (vishwas) Concordance (saujanyata) Vritti Fulfilment of values in mutuality and communicated resolution; cooperation, participation, and complementarity
          Respect (samman) Cordiality (sauhardta) Mun Acceptance and balanced expression of personality and talent; faithful presentation of what is understood and accepted
          Affection (sneha) Dedication (nishtha) Mun Absence of opposition in just behaviour and continuity of meeting in satisfaction; sustained endeavour towards the understood goal of awakening
          Care (mamta) Generosity (udarta) Mun Nurturing and protection with affinity; willingly offering body, mind, and wealth for another’s awakening, health, and prosperity
          Guidance (vatsalya) Naturalness (sahajta) Chitta Continuity of nurturing and protection towards comprehensive resolution; clarity, authenticity, and coherence of behaviour, norm, thought, and realisation
          Reverence (shraddha) Devoutness (pujyata) Chitta Continuing movement towards awakening and conduct completeness; activeness for qualitative development and awakening

          Gratitude (kritagyata) Humility (saumyata) Vritti Acknowledging help received towards development and awakening; voluntary self-restraint and established civility
          Love (prem) Non-otherness (ananyata) Chitta Realisation of completeness and the combined expression of kindness, grace, and compassion; complementary participation and continuity of authenticity and resolution

          Trust is the common relationship value (samya mulya), whose fulfilment is required across relationships. It involves fulfilment of values, understanding orderliness, and communicating resolution. Love is the complete value (purna mulya): its non-otherness extends through human mutuality and participation with the rest of nature (MVD, pp. 331, 336; MAD, reader pp. 30, 43).

          Symbolic valuation (prateek mulya) concerns representation through money or assigned price. Its relation to usefulness and exchange is developed in §1.5 (MVD, p. 183; SB, pp. 211–212).

          1.4 Recognition, establishment, fulfilment and justice

          Human relationships are the living field where values are enacted. A relationship () is a mutuality whose expectations towards completeness are definite. Parent–child, teacher–student, sibling, spouse, friend, master–associate, and orderliness relationships name such mutualities. A contact (sampark) is a meeting in which expectations are voluntary. Both involve values and can succeed or fail through the justice or injustice of their fulfilment (MVD, pp. 61–62, 190; KD, p. 20; JV, pp. 108–109; Family Relationships and Values, §§1.1.3–1.2.1).

          Established relationship values endure across time, place, and changing circumstances. Care and guidance have definite content, while their recognition and fulfilment depend on the participants’ understanding, receptivity, and means. Nurturing an infant requires suitable food, warmth, and shelter; guiding a developing youth requires explanations and opportunities appropriate to what the learner can understand. The responsibility endures while the means of fulfilling it meet the recipient’s actual situation (MAD, reader p. 51; MVD, pp. 61–62, 331–340).

          Justice (nyaya) comprises recognition of relationships (pehchan), fulfilment of values (nirvah), evaluation (mulyankan), and mutual satisfaction (ubhay-tripti). Recognition identifies the relationship and its purpose; fulfilment supplies the appropriate bodily, material, and intellectual contributions. Evaluation examines whether the values have been fulfilled on both sides. Mutual satisfaction completes this activity through fulfilled responsibilities whose correctness the participants recognise (MVD, pp. 311, 336; JV, p. 98).

          Deliberation before action considers the relationship, available abilities and means, and the contribution to make. Examination of the resulting conduct asks whether justice was fulfilled on both sides. Self-examination concerns both one’s understanding and whether one’s conduct was free from exploitation; the evidence becomes available through shared living and work (JV, pp. 98, 138–139; KD, p. 84).

          Nourishing a child illustrates this active dynamic. The food possesses usefulness value (upyogita mulya) in its biological capacity to nourish the body. The caregiver acts from care (mamta), offering available resources through generosity (udarta). The caregiver’s understanding, the nutritional adequacy of the food, and the child’s actual health enter the evaluation. Price records a monetary transaction; justice concerns whether care was fulfilled and whether mutual satisfaction and healthy development resulted (§§1.3, 1.5).

          Understanding a responsibility can coexist with limited resources or skill. Conversely, an effective outward contribution may be performed while a learner is still developing the settled understanding that would sustain it independently. Education must therefore cultivate correct recognition, technical capacity, and continuity of fulfilment (MVD, pp. 61, 94, 203). Because the same jeevan recognises, evaluates, and relishes value, inward faculty harmony and outward relational justice are inseparable aspects of the same conscious living (JV, pp. 138–139; MAD, reader p. 51).

          1.5 The value of things: utility, labour and symbolic valuation

          Usefulness value (upyogita mulya) concerns what a physical thing can fulfil in bodily nourishment, protection, and participation. Human material needs include common aspirations (samanya akanksha) for food, shelter, and clothing or bodily provision (aahar, aavas, alankar), and special aspirations (mahatva akanksha) for communication and transportation (door-shravana, door-darshana, door-gamana). Their assessment concerns the means required for living and participation (MVD, pp. 56–57; JV, p. 123).

          Aesthetic value (kala mulya / saundarya mulya) qualifies useful things through human work. Its meaningfulness lies in adding convenience to usefulness, as in the design of a vehicle that makes travel more serviceable. Evaluation therefore asks what an aesthetic addition enables in the thing’s use (JV, pp. 137–138).

          Usefulness is definite in what a thing can fulfil: wheat and sesame seeds nourish, and a change in their monetary valuation does not itself change this capacity. The constancy of usefulness concerns this determinate relation between the thing and its function. Material condition, the quantity needed, and the circumstances of use can change; interference can also reduce the thing’s usefulness. Usefulness and beauty are temporal (samayik) in their material embodiment and fulfilment through needs, use, exchange, and deployment. Evaluation therefore attends both to what a thing can provide and to the changing conditions in which it provides it (JV, pp. 123, 137–138; MAD, reader p. 51; §§1.6, 7.5).

          Human labour (shram) establishes usefulness and aesthetic value upon natural abundance. Application (prayog) is endeavour continued until success in response to need; production (utpadan) generalises the successful application through an organised process of labour, making useful things available in abundance. Prosperity (samriddhi) involves assessing needs, producing beyond them, and recognising the resulting availability. This fulfilment takes place in the family and supports right-use and participation in wider orderliness (MVD, pp. 56, 260, 268, 331; JV, pp. 137–138).

          Money represents symbolic value (prateek mulya), through which goods are accounted for in exchange. A rise in the monetary valuation of a thing can occur without a corresponding increase in its usefulness. Scarcity and accumulation can raise symbolic valuations while leaving actual needs unfulfilled. Prosperity therefore requires understanding needs, developing productive capacity, and ensuring availability for right-use (MVD, pp. 183, 331; SB, pp. 211–212).

          Use (upyog) deploys means for production and service. Right-use (sadupyog) directs resources in the form of body, mind, and wealth through participation in family-based self-governing orderliness. Purposeful-use (prayojansheelta) directs those resources, upon awakening, towards an undivided society (akhand samaj) and universal orderliness (sarvabhaum vyavastha) (MVD, p. 57; How Undivided Society Is Established, §1.1).

          Exchange involves assessment of labour-value (shram mulya) and the availability of goods through exchange free of profit and loss. Rightful wealth (svadhan) is received as the fruit or remuneration of labour (pratiphal), a freely conferred gift (paritosh), or an award (puraskar). The resulting wealth takes the form of useful and aesthetically qualified things, available for right-use and protection (AVD, p. 170; MVD, pp. 260, 268–269; SB, p. 237). Production must also sustain the natural sources on which it depends. Cyclical economics concerns provision together with regeneration, extending evaluation beyond immediate output to the continuity of productive conditions (SB, pp. 141, 200–201; §7.9).

          1.6 How values are ordered: dedication, endurance and inclusion

          Dedication (samarpan) orders values through the purposes they serve. Usefulness is dedicated to need (avashyakta mulya), need to expressed value, expressed value to established value, established value to human value, and human value to jeevan value. This is a qualitative hierarchy of fulfilment: material provision serves relationships, relationships express humane character, and humane participation fulfils the harmonies of jeevan. The more comprehensive purpose governs the use of its supporting means. Production gained through deception may supply a useful thing while failing the justice its production and exchange should serve (MAD, reader p. 34; MVD, pp. 56–58).

          Higher value (guru mulyan) is defined through a long-lasting result or non-transformability. Advancement concerns movement towards higher value and resolution; decline concerns movement towards lower value and delusion. Enduring relationship values and the changing means of their fulfilment must therefore be evaluated with attention to what is being sustained (MVD, pp. 43, 110, 126).

          Inclusion concerns the four orders: the more developed order includes the properties, essential nature, and dharma of the less developed within its wider participation. Growth retains existence; the hope to live retains growth and existence; human happiness retains these preceding contents. Cumulative fulfilment in human living also retains resolution in prosperity, resolution and prosperity in fearlessness, and all three in coexistence (§1.2; MVD, pp. 47, 51; KD, pp. 37, 112).

          Acquisitive profit seeks higher value in exchange for lower value, using another’s ignorance or helplessness. Its economic purpose differs from prosperity through production and exchange without exploitation (MVD, pp. 58, 67; JV, pp. 139–140). The relations of dedication, endurance, and inclusion raise distinct questions about comparison and practical choice (§§7.1, 7.7).

          1.7 Evaluative error and awakening

          Evaluation under delusion () can attribute more, less, or a different value than the unit’s essentiality warrants. Over-evaluation (adhimulyan) and under-evaluation (avamulyan) are illustrated by mistaking iron for gold or gold for iron. Mis-evaluation (nirmulyan) attributes a different identity, as when a rope is mistaken for a snake or a snake for a rope. These errors disturb the evaluator’s conduct and can produce suffering in relationships and the surrounding natural order (MVD, pp. 152, 306; SB, PDF p. 146).

          Self-evaluation (sva-mulyankan) examines one’s understanding and participation. Nirakarshan concerns freedom from delusive attachment and evaluative distortion, through which a unit is understood according to its essentiality. Its fulfilment develops from partial nirakarshan in the humane-human stage to complete nirakarshan in the divine-human stage (MVD, pp. 87, 152–153, 203).

          Fear and temptation (bhay-pralobhan) cannot serve as relationship values. When relationships are governed by fear of punishment or temptation of gain, evaluation is corrupted into compliance or transaction. Authentic relationships rest on voluntary recognition of mutual values and shared satisfaction (JV, pp. 109–110).

          Human development involves qualitative transformation of consciousness (chetana vikas). In animal consciousness (jeeva-chetana), human living centres on the four bodily instincts of eating, sleep, defence, and mating, evaluated through pleasantness, bodily advantage, and gain. In humane living, the body is understood as an instrument of jeevan and participation is guided by justice, resolution, and truth. The three eshanas—progeny-motive (putra-eshana), wealth-motive (vitta-eshana), and renown-motive (loka-eshana)—belong to humane tendencies directed through this understanding. The awakened humane human fulfils these motives within a limited sphere while seeking fuller purposeful participation. Higher-humane living extends this participation through deific-human and divine-human conduct (MVD, pp. 58, 60, 65, 304, 319, 321).

          1.8 Continuity of value in persons and tradition

          Continuity (nirantarata) concerns how understood value becomes a sustained basis of conduct across changing circumstances. An unawakened person may act generously on one occasion and withdraw cooperation on another. Awakening establishes understanding whose fulfilment becomes continuous in relationships and work (MVD, pp. 61, 94; KD, pp. 37, 84).

          concerns the acceptances and tendencies formed through doing (karana), causing to do (karavana), and consenting (anumodana). Their direction matters: wholesome formation (susanskar) supports awakening and resolved living, while unwholesome formation (kusanskar) sustains delusion and decline (MVD, pp. 90, 94).

          Education develops the reception and assimilation of value-conduct, aapyayan, together with qualitative refinement, parimarjan. Contact with a person who lives with understanding can awaken the learner’s aspiration to understand and live similarly. Participation gives meaningful conduct a concrete form to observe, practise, and examine; reflection on its purpose develops the acceptances through which conduct becomes independently understood. Hopes, thoughts, desires, and resolves change in this process. Study concerns realisation-oriented understanding; realisation-based understanding becomes the basis of further expression and evidence in living (MAD, reader p. 45; MVD, pp. 83, 94, 99–100; KD, pp. 18–19, 112–113).

          Evidence (praman) connects realisation, behaviour, and application. Knowledge of coexistence becomes evident through just relationships, resolved thought, and the right-use of means. The account of knowledge, law, regulation, balance, justice, dharma, and truth joins understanding with these responsibilities: effective production, fulfilled relationships, and continuity of resolution each contribute to the assessment of living (MVD, pp. 12, 80, 137, 156, 174; AVD, p. 265).

          Continuity across generations requires education and institutions that support these responsibilities. Education–sanskar, justice–security, production–work, exchange–storage, and health–restraint form the five systems of human orderliness (MVD, p. 260; AVD, p. 265; The Ontology of Coexistence, §1.15). Value education develops knowing, believing, recognising, and fulfilling through study, practice, and self-evaluation. An awakened tradition makes understanding available to each generation through such participation (KD, pp. 18–19; MVD, p. 87; Ethics and Morals in Human Beings, §§3.1, 3.5–3.6).

          Understood meaning, actual fulfilment, and public evidence remain distinguishable. Appendix A formalises their structural relations; §7.8 examines how educational learning matures into settled establishment and living continuity.

          2. The Advaita Vedanta Answer

          Advaita Vedanta relates the dearness of persons and goods to the Self, , whose identity with is known in liberation. Human purposes include material provision, enjoyment, and right conduct, while liberation concerns freedom from the ignorance through which finite attainments are sought as ultimate fulfilment. Value is consequently examined through dearness, the ends of life, and discrimination between enduring reality and changing goods (BU, 2.4.5 with Shankara’s commentary; BSB, 1.1.1, 1.1.4).

          2.1 The Self, dearness and fullness

          “It is not for the sake of all, my dear, that all is loved, but for one’s own sake that it is loved. The Self, my dear Maitreyī, should be realised…” — BU, 2.4.5

          The Self is primary in dearness; husband, wife, children, and wealth are dear for its sake. Inquiry into this dearness leads beyond the limited individual with whom one ordinarily identifies to knowledge of the Self (BU, 2.4.5 with Shankara’s commentary).

          The Upanishadic teaching identifies Brahman as truth, knowledge, and infinity (satyam jnanam anantam), and as the source of joy. Advaita’s later compact formulation—being, consciousness, and bliss, sat-cit-ananda—brings these descriptions together as names of its undivided nature. Finite pleasures depend on conditions and degrees of freedom from desire; Brahman’s bliss is its own nature, disclosed through knowledge (TU, 2.1.1, 2.7–2.9 with Shankara’s commentary). The Ontology of Coexistence, §2.2, develops these descriptions of Brahman.

          The dearness of a person, the nourishment provided by food, and the security sought through wealth have their place in empirical life. Their finite satisfactions lead the inquiry towards the Self as the ground of fulfilment. Ethical duties govern how such goods are pursued and how others are treated (BU, 2.4.5; BG, 3.8, 16.1–16.3).

          2.2 Human purposes and empirical virtues

          The four purusharthas distinguish purposes of human life: , right living and duty; artha, material means; kama, enjoyment; and moksha, liberation. Advaita distinguishes pursuit of the first three with attachment to their fruits from the inquiry into liberation as the highest human purpose (BG, 18.34; BSB, 1.1.1). Acquisition and enjoyment remain answerable to dharma: desire compatible with duty includes bodily maintenance, while accumulation through unjust means is condemned (BG, 7.11, 16.12 with Shankara’s commentary).

          Within empirical living, , duties and virtues guide action. Fearlessness, purity of mind, generosity, self-control, truthfulness, compassion, and freedom from anger are qualities of conduct that orient the person towards liberation (BG, 16.1–16.3). Discrimination of the enduring from the transient, nitya/anitya, concerns the reality and persistence of what is sought. Purposes, virtues, and this discrimination thus address different aspects of evaluation (BSB, 1.1.1; VC, vv. 17–20).

          2.3 Superimposition and mistaken valuation

          Ignorance involves adhyasa, mutual superimposition of Self and not-Self. Bodily and mental conditions are attributed to the Self, while the consciousness and selfhood of the Self are attributed to body and mind. The resulting identification sustains the experience of being a limited agent and enjoyer (BSB, Introduction). Its evaluative consequence is that changes in possessions, sensations, or standing can be taken to secure or diminish one’s very fulfilment. Dwelling on objects develops attachment, desire, and, when frustrated, anger and loss of discrimination (BG, 2.62–2.63).

          Correction therefore requires inquiry into what the Self is, alongside restraint of attachment in conduct. Ordinary knowledge can correct an error about an object; liberating knowledge removes the identification that gives finite acquisition its promise of ultimate completion (BSB, 1.1.4). The Epistemology of Coexistence, §2.3, develops superimposition, correction, and the distinction between empirical and ultimate reality.

          2.4 Enduring fulfilment and the ordering of purposes

          Acquired goods and pleasurable experiences arise under conditions and can cease. Liberation is unproduced: knowledge discloses the Self’s freedom rather than making it a new result of action. Its priority concerns enduring freedom from ignorance and dependence on further acquisition. The distinction between nitya and anitya directs aspiration towards this fulfilment (BSB, 1.1.1, 1.1.4; VC, vv. 17–20).

          The ordering has two connected relations: dharma governs the pursuit of finite goods, while liberation directs the inquiry into ultimate fulfilment. Material support and disciplined action can prepare a person for knowledge (BG, 3.8, 3.19, 7.11, 16.12; BSB, 1.1.1). Their usefulness in preparation differs from the freedom knowledge discloses. The practical implications of this ordering for comparison and allocation are examined in §5.6.

          2.5 Useful things, action and attachment

          Objects have empirical use: bodily maintenance requires action and provision, and engagement with necessary sensory objects can proceed with restraint, free from attraction and aversion (BG, 2.64, 3.8 with Shankara’s commentary). Attachment concerns the identification and craving through which their acquisition or loss governs the person (BSB, 1.1.4). At the ultimate standpoint, , objects have no reality independent of Brahman; empirical usefulness belongs to the domain in which needs, actions, and their consequences occur (BSB, 2.1.13–2.1.14).

          Action without attachment supports purification and knowledge. Loka-sangraha, the holding together of the world, also gives action a social purpose: the wise must consider the effect of their example on those engaged in action. Such activity can continue without the realised person’s identification as an individual doer (BG, 3.19–3.20, 3.25–3.26 with Shankara’s commentary). Goods and work consequently have practical and ethical significance, while liberation remains distinct from anything their production can accomplish.

          2.6 Knowledge, evidence and responsibility

          Knowledge of the Self proceeds through scriptural teaching, shruti, hearing, reflection, and contemplation. Discrimination, dispassion, restraint, and concentration qualify the inquirer (BU, 2.4.5; BSB, 1.1.1). The decisive issue is the removal of mistaken identification: pleasure and material success cannot by themselves establish knowledge of the Self (BSB, 1.1.4).

          Evaluation thus asks both whether finite goods serve legitimate empirical purposes and whether the fulfilment sought through them rests on a correct understanding of the Self. Compassion and concern for one’s effect on others have continuing significance in empirical conduct, including the example offered by the wise (BG, 3.25–3.26, 16.1–16.3). Empirical duty is assessed within vyavahara, while liberating knowledge concerns the Self’s already free nature (BSB, 1.1.4).

          3. Modern Western Philosophy on Value

          Modern Western value theory contains competing accounts of what is good, what bears value, and how value is known. The discussion moves from grounds and warranted judgment to well-being, value ordering, economic goods, and relationships. Metaethics examines the status of normative judgments; welfare theory examines what makes life go well for a person; practical philosophy examines reasons, duties, and conflicts. These inquiries overlap without yielding a single Western doctrine (SEP Value Theory).

          3.1 Intrinsic, final and instrumental value

          Something can be valued as a means to another good or for its own sake. This final–instrumental distinction differs from the distinction between value grounded in intrinsic features and value dependent on relations or context. A historically significant object, for example, may be valued for its own sake partly because of its history (SEP Intrinsic vs Extrinsic Value).

          Moore asks what would be good considered in isolation, but also argues that the value of a whole need not equal the sum of its parts’ values. His principle of organic unities makes the configuration of a valuable whole matter to assessment. Intrinsic value therefore does not invariably entail simple addition of separately valued constituents (Moore 1903, §§18–22).

          3.2 Natural facts and normative conclusions

          Hume calls attention to the unexplained transition from statements about what is to conclusions about what ought to be. Moore’s open-question argument challenges proposed definitions of goodness in terms of some other property: understanding that something has the property appears to leave open whether it is good (Hume 1739, III.1.1; Moore 1903, §§10–14).

          The two arguments address different problems. Hume’s passage concerns inference; Moore’s concerns the analysis of goodness. Neither by itself settles every form of ethical naturalism. A theory may propose a substantive rather than definitional identity, or argue for normative standards through the characteristic flourishing of living beings. Such proposals require arguments for the identification and for its practical authority (SEP Natural Law Ethics; SEP Moral Realism).

          3.3 Realism, construction and anti-realism

          Moral realists hold that some moral judgments are true and that their truth is not exhausted by the speaker’s approval. Naturalist and non-naturalist versions disagree about the relation between moral facts and the natural world. Expressivists explain moral language through practical attitudes and commitments; error theorists argue that ordinary moral claims systematically fail in what they purport to assert (SEP Moral Realism; SEP Moral Anti-Realism).

          Constructivist approaches connect normative standards with an appropriate procedure of practical reasoning. Their relation to realism depends on the particular account of construction and objectivity. Expressivist theories likewise develop accounts of disagreement, consistency, and practical criticism. The categories identify research programmes whose adequacy turns on their detailed treatment of truth, reasons, and judgment (SEP Moral Anti-Realism).

          3.4 Fitting attitudes and reasons

          Fitting-attitude accounts analyse value through the attitudes it is appropriate to have towards an object: admiration, love, desire, or respect, for example. The relevant attitude is fitting, rather than merely actual or widely shared. A central difficulty is distinguishing reasons that bear on an object’s value from incentives or threats that make an attitude advantageous to its holder (SEP Fitting Attitude Theories).

          Scanlon’s buck-passing account explains the value of something through other properties that give reasons for appropriate responses. Value identifies the presence of such reasons rather than supplying an additional independent reason. The account shifts inquiry towards what makes a response warranted (Scanlon 1998, ch. 2).

          3.5 Pleasure, desire and objective goods

          Hedonistic theories make pleasure and the absence of pain central to welfare. Desire theories locate welfare in the fulfilment of desires, often with qualifications concerning information, rationality, or the scope of the person’s concern. Objective-list theories hold that some things can benefit a person independently of current desire or pleasure, such as knowledge, achievement, or worthwhile relationships (Parfit 1984, Appendix I; SEP Well-Being). On virtue accounts grounded in flourishing, good character and its exercise partly constitute living well (SEP Well-Being).

          Nozick’s experience machine asks whether an entirely satisfying simulated experience would supply everything that matters in life. The thought experiment tests whether doing things, being a certain person, and contact with reality matter beyond experience. Its force depends on reflection about the case and the intuitions it elicits (Nozick 1974, pp. 42–45).

          3.6 Plurality, comparison and trade-offs

          Value pluralism holds that more than one irreducible kind of value matters. It need not imply that every pair of options is incomparable. Ranking, comparison through a common measure, compensation for a loss, and practical permission to make a trade-off are distinct questions. Some accounts allow comparison without a common numerical unit; others identify cases where familiar comparative relations fail (Berlin 1969; Raz 1986; Chang 1997; SEP Value Pluralism; SEP Incommensurable Values).

          Kant distinguishes price, which permits an equivalent, from dignity, which admits no equivalent. Anderson examines how different goods call for different modes of valuation and how market valuation can be inappropriate in some relations. These positions concern the standards governing valuation and exchange, including cases in which willingness to pay does not decide what should be done (Kant 1785, Section II; Anderson 1993).

          Questions of beauty, taste, and the validity of aesthetic judgment are developed in Aesthetics, §2.2.

          3.7 Scheler and Hartmann on ordered values

          Scheler treats value as objective and disclosed through intentional feeling, with a hierarchy of agreeable, vital, spiritual, and holy values. This feeling is directed towards value rather than being identical with bodily sensation. His account includes distorted valuation and the transformation of attention through love and openness. Hartmann also develops a differentiated order of values and distinguishes the height of a value from the strength of its demand. Ordered value consequently raises questions both of recognition and of what takes priority in action (Scheler 1913–16; Hartmann 1932; SEP Scheler).

          3.8 Economic value and human development

          Marx distinguishes use-value, the usefulness of a thing, from value in commodity production and its expression in exchange-value. Concrete useful labour produces particular use-values; abstract human labour supplies the account of commodity value, whose magnitude depends on socially necessary labour time. Nature contributes to material wealth, and some useful things have no value in this specific commodity sense. These distinctions prevent a simple identification of usefulness, labour expended by an individual, and exchange price (Marx 1867, ch. 1).

          Marginal analysis examines the significance of an additional unit within particular conditions of choice. A thing’s physical capacity alone does not determine its marginal benefit or exchange conditions. The capability approach further distinguishes resources and reported satisfaction from people’s real opportunities to live and act. Sen and Nussbaum make this distinction central to evaluating development, while differing in how capabilities should be specified (Sen 1999; Nussbaum 2011; SEP Capability Approach).

          3.9 Care, recognition and trust

          Care ethics examines dependence, attentiveness, responsiveness, and the concrete relationship between one who cares and one who receives care. Recognition theory examines the social relations through which practical identity and self-realisation are sustained. These approaches bring the conditions of human development into the account of value; a requirement for social recognition need not make a person’s basic worth depend on whether others currently recognise it (Noddings 1984; Honneth 1995; SEP Recognition).

          Philosophical accounts of trust examine dependence under vulnerability, expectations of another, and the distinction between reliance and trustworthiness. They ask when trust is reasonable and what betrayal damages. Trust is therefore a normative relationship whose success cannot be identified simply with confidence or successful prediction (SEP Trust).

          3.10 The range of the inquiry

          These approaches offer different resources for explaining value’s bearers, grounds, recognition, and practical force. A claim about the status of moral truth does not alone determine a theory of welfare; a theory of welfare does not alone determine just distribution. Western value theory makes these distinctions explicit and investigates how they can be joined (SEP Value Theory; SEP Well-Being).

          4. The Natural and Social Sciences on Valuation

          The natural and social sciences investigate valuation through mechanisms of learning and choice, reports of experience and expectation, and individual and aggregate outcomes such as cooperation, production, and well-being. These objects of inquiry require different measures and explanations. Empirical findings can inform practical judgment about provision, education, relationships, and institutions when joined to an account of which outcomes matter and why. Their evidential strength depends on the design, population, measures, and causal assumptions of each investigation. The studies reviewed here supply neither the darshan’s classes of mulya nor a usefulness-constancy doctrine or a measure of established relationship value.

          4.1 Neural valuation and learning

          Neuroscience distinguishes representing options, learning from outcomes, and selecting actions. Research on value-based choice identifies neural systems through which different options can be compared for a decision. Dopamine reward-prediction-error research concerns differences between expected and received reward and their role in updating expectations (Rangel, Camerer, and Montague 2008; Schultz 2016).

          The comparison of options and the learning signals that revise expectations perform different functions. A shared neural scale within a task concerns how a decision is implemented. Moral decisions recruit interacting systems whose contributions depend on the task (Crockett 2013).

          4.2 Cooperation and moral regularities

          Evolutionary explanations investigate how cooperation can develop through processes such as repeated interaction and reciprocity, including behaviour that does not depend on formulating a moral principle (Trivers 1971). Comparative ethnographic research identifies recurring patterns in moral assessment: coded records from sixty societies showed broadly positive evaluations of seven forms of cooperation, including helping kin, reciprocity, bravery, fair division, and respect for prior possession. The regularity concerns these cooperative forms within the available records (Curry, Mullins, and Whitehouse 2019).

          4.3 Income, needs and well-being

          Well-being research distinguishes evaluative judgments about one’s life from reports of experienced affect. Studies of income examine associations with both, with results that vary across measures and participants; the earlier disagreement about a plateau in emotional well-being illustrates the importance of those distinctions (Kahneman and Deaton 2010; Killingsworth 2021; Killingsworth, Kahneman, and Mellers 2023). Individual differences, national comparisons, and change over time also pose different empirical questions (Easterlin 1974).

          Self-determination theory examines autonomy, competence, and relatedness as basic psychological needs. It relates their satisfaction to intrinsic motivation, self-regulation, and well-being, and investigates how social conditions support or frustrate these processes. This supplies an account of how motivation and personal development depend on conditions of learning and participation, alongside the investigation of material resources and reported experience (Ryan and Deci 2000).

          4.4 Trust and social outcomes

          Economic and social research examines associations between generalised trust, institutions, investment, and growth. In the survey measure used by Zak and Knack, trust is the proportion of respondents in a country who agree that most people can be trusted. It records a general reported expectation about others, which can be compared with economic and institutional outcomes. Distinguishing the report from observed conduct clarifies what the measure contributes to an investigation of relationships (Zak and Knack 2001).

          4.5 Prices, production and welfare

          Economic measurement distinguishes prices, production aggregates such as GDP, and measures of household income and consumption. Assessing material living standards requires attention to household resources and their distribution; accounting for unpaid household work can also make otherwise omitted contributions visible. Wider assessments of social progress examine health, education, relationships, political participation, environmental conditions, and insecurity. The choice and aggregation of indicators depend on what the assessment is intended to represent (Stiglitz, Sen, and Fitoussi 2009). Prices and output thus answer questions about exchange and production, while welfare assessment asks how resources and other conditions bear on people’s lives. These measures have distinct meanings from usefulness-value as examined in §1.5.

          4.6 Specified objectives and artificial intelligence

          Artificial intelligence research documents cases in which an agent satisfies a specified objective through behaviour that defeats the designer’s purpose. Specification gaming exposes a mismatch between a rewarded proxy and the intended outcome. It motivates better specifications, oversight, and evaluation of effects beyond the rewarded signal (Amodei et al. 2016; Krakovna et al. 2020). Its relevance to the problem of evaluative error in §1.7 is a limited analogy: satisfying a stated objective need not amount to correct evaluation. The immediate question is whether the objective adequately represents the intended good.

          5. Comparison of the Answers

          The traditions differ both in what they call value and in what they ask an account of value to explain. Comparison is most useful when it follows specific questions about grounds, recognition, development, evidence, what endures or changes, and the scope of comparison. A single label such as objective, spiritual, or scientific leaves many of these differences unresolved.

          5.2 Essentiality and normative authority

          identifies value with the essentiality of active units in coexistence and grounds human fulfilment in humane participation (§1.1). Hume asks what warrants the transition from claims about what exists to conclusions about what ought to be done (§3.2). His question concerns the inference, including arguments that begin with God or human affairs (Hume 1739, III.1.1). The darshan’s account makes the relation between essential participation and obligation central to the comparison. Natural-law and realist approaches likewise seek grounds independent of actual approval, while differing over human nature and normative authority. The further question is why a particular account of participation should guide conduct and how it addresses dependency or disagreement (§7.4).

          Advaita locates primary dearness in the Self and ultimate fulfilment in knowledge of . This differs from locating value in each active unit’s essential participation. Derivative dearness concerns why persons and goods are loved; dependence on Brahman concerns their ultimate reality. Goods and work retain practical and ethical significance within empirical life in Advaita (§§2.1, 2.5). Madhyasth Darshan locates their essentiality within real coexistence. These different ontological grounds do not alone settle the Western question whether a finite good has intrinsic value within empirical life (§3.1).

          5.3 Activity, properties and evaluative response

          Madhyasth Darshan distinguishes the essentiality of the active unit from its recognition and evaluation through the sentiment-activities (bhav-) of (§§1.1–1.4). Fitting-attitude theories distinguish actual approval from the response a good warrants (§3.4). Scanlon explains value through other properties that provide reasons for appropriate attitudes and conduct, and includes activities and relationships among the things valued (Scanlon 1998, ch. 2, pp. 95–97). Both accounts make correctness a further question about evaluation. Their difference concerns how essential participation or reason-giving features ground that correctness, and how it is expressed in living.

          Neural valuation research examines how options are represented and compared, while reward-prediction-error research examines signals through which expectations are revised (§4.1). Multiple interacting systems contribute to selection and learning; a common scale within a decision task concerns their implementation (Rangel, Camerer, and Montague 2008; Crockett 2013). Such findings can inform an investigation of evaluative activity. They do not by themselves supply the normative standard for correct evaluation relative to essentiality or establish the faculty harmony specified by nirvirodh. Connecting these accounts requires both an account of what makes a response appropriate and evidence relating the proposed harmony to the processes measured.

          The rope–snake illustration makes the difference in correction concrete. Ordinary correction identifies the rope in both accounts. Madhyasth Darshan uses mistaking a rope for a snake, or a snake for a rope, to illustrate mis-evaluation relative to what the unit is (SB, PDF p. 146). Advaita also uses corrected rope–snake error to explain how knowledge removes error, extending the analogy to knowledge of the Self’s identity with Brahman (BSB, 1.1.4). The wider standards differ: correct recognition and participation within real coexistence, and removal of superimposed limitation through non-dual knowledge (§§1.7, 2.3).

          5.4 Establishment, expression and learning

          Establishment in Madhyasth Darshan concerns understood relationship value becoming a sustained basis of fulfilment (§§1.3–1.4, 1.8). Encountering and practising meaningful conduct, examining its purpose, and developing independent understanding connect outward learning with establishment in state. Study, self-evaluation, and qualitative refinement deepen this connection. Advaita gives virtues and disciplined conduct a preparatory role in knowledge; its purposes and dearness distinctions address different questions from the darshan’s established–expressed activity pairs (§§2.1–2.2, 2.6). Western accounts of character and care examine practical judgment, attention, and responsiveness (§§3.5, 3.9). These accounts differ over the understanding that conduct should express and the evidence of its development.

          Research on learning and psychological needs examines changing expectations and the motivational conditions of competence and participation (§§4.1, 4.3). It can inform investigation of value education through performance, reports, and the conditions under which learning persists. The darshan’s further claim to established understanding requires assessment of the meaning understood, responsibilities fulfilled, and their continuity across changing circumstances (§7.8).

          5.5 Happiness, bliss and material provision

          The four harmonies in Madhyasth Darshan (sukh, shanti, santosh, anand) concern the concordance of activities between the faculties of jeevan (§1.2), while Advaita’s ananda concerns Brahman’s undivided fullness disclosed through knowledge (§§2.1, 2.4). Their difference concerns what fulfilment is and how it relates to active participation in living. Western welfare theories further distinguish pleasure, desire satisfaction, and objective goods (§3.5).

          Well-being research separates evaluative judgments about life from experienced affect and examines their associations with income and psychological needs (§4.3). Improved reports can inform an assessment of living conditions; they do not alone establish a specified faculty harmony or knowledge of Brahman. Testing a proposed correspondence requires both a clear account of the claimed fulfilment and measures appropriate to it. Decisions about compensation among goods also require more than a measured rise in satisfaction (§7.1).

          5.6 Ordering and value recognition

          Madhyasth Darshan’s dedication (samarpan), endurance (guru mulyan), inclusion (samaveshan), and common or complete relationship value distinguish relations of purpose, persistence, comprehensiveness, and relational scope (§1.6). Together they articulate an ordered whole in which material means serve relationships and humane participation, and these fulfil jeevan. Scheler’s hierarchy and Hartmann’s distinction between height and strength of demand offer different criteria for ordering (§3.7). Their comparison turns on what each relation licenses in action. Dedication relates usefulness to need, need to expressed value, expressed to established value, established to human value, and human to jeevan value. This qualitative hierarchy gives direction to participation: greater output acquired through damaged trust fails the relational purpose that production should serve. Ranking, common measurement, and permissible compensation remain distinct questions (§3.6). The practical task is to apply this ordering to disputed obligations and constrained means (§§7.1, 7.7).

          Advaita orders human purposes through liberation’s unproduced freedom and the governance of finite pursuits by (§§2.2, 2.4). Liberation’s priority does not mean that it contains acquired wealth or enjoyment as the darshan’s more comprehensive fulfilment retains preceding content. Material means can support inquiry without reproducing the particular dedication sequence of usefulness, need, and relationship values. This is an ordering of purposes and their conditions, with no numerical equivalence between them. It can reject a pursuit incompatible with duty while leaving the allocation among legitimate material needs to further practical judgment.

          5.7 Useful things, labour and symbolic value

          Madhyasth Darshan and Marx distinguish usefulness from monetary appearance and give labour a central role in production and exchange (§§1.5, 3.8). Marx analyses the social form and magnitude of commodity value through abstract labour and socially necessary labour time; the darshan evaluates production and exchange through usefulness (upyogita mulya), labour assessment, family prosperity (samriddhi), and right-use (sadupyog). Money expresses symbolic valuation (prateek mulya); changes in its quantity or in the price of a good do not by themselves establish a corresponding change in usefulness. Their criticisms of exploitation accordingly differ in explanation and remedy.

          Advaita addresses useful things through empirical need, duty, and attachment (§2.5). Food can nourish and work can sustain life, although neither produces liberation. Objects’ dependence on Brahman does not cancel these empirical distinctions. The Advaita passages examined here supply no doctrine of usefulness-constancy and do not specify how labour measures economic value or determines price. Marx therefore supplies a comparison concerning commodity production and magnitude, while Advaita supplies a comparison concerning the ends of acquisition and the limits of the fulfilment sought through it.

          Economic measurement records prices and output, including GDP; these do not measure the darshan’s usefulness value (§4.5). The capability approach asks whether resources become real opportunities for differently situated people (§3.8). The darshan’s distinction between definite usefulness and its temporal fulfilment likewise directs attention to the thing’s capacities, their deployment, the need met, and the conditions of access. This provides a basis for comparison without equating usefulness with a capability or a price. Assessing quality, quantity, and distribution remains necessary for a labour-valued exchange order (§§7.5, 7.11).

          5.8 Relationships, agreement and conflict

          Justice in Madhyasth Darshan requires recognised responsibilities, actual fulfilment, evaluation, and mutual satisfaction. Care and recognition theories offer parallel questions about dependency, responsiveness, and practical standing.

          In Advaita, husband, wife, and child are dear for the Self, while relational duties retain their force within without independent ultimate status (BU, 2.4.5; §§2.2, 2.5–2.6). Family Relationships and Values, §2.1, develops the relation between householder discipline and renunciation.

          Survey trust concerns a reported expectation about other people (§4.4). The darshan’s trust is an established relationship value, common across relationships (samya mulya), whose fulfilment is assessed in justice (§§1.3–1.4). Research on reported trust and cooperation can examine social conditions and outcomes (§§4.2, 4.4); identifying those measures with established trust would require evidence that they capture its specified meaning and fulfilment.

          The darshan holds that quarrelling involves error on at least one side and that correctly understanding parties do not quarrel (SB, PDF p. 146). Applying this harmony claim to scarce resources, absent parties, and competing legitimate needs requires an account of the allocation to make (§7.7).

          5.9 Qualitative evaluation in two applications

          Consider a teacher helping a learner and a family producing food. Both are analytical illustrations of the value map. In teaching, guidance and care are established relationship values; human values such as generosity, kindness, grace, and compassion inform the provision of time, suitable content, and conditions for learning. The learner’s developing understanding is an achievement whose evidence must be examined. The responsibility to guide endures, while the explanation, pace, and resources appropriate to its fulfilment change with the learner’s receptivity (§§1.2–1.4).

          Dedication relates useful teaching materials and expressed conduct to the relationship they serve and to the purpose of resolved living (§1.6). An enjoyable lesson or grateful response does not alone establish that guidance was fulfilled. Assessment should ask whether the learner can explain the meaning and apply it in a new situation, and whether the teacher’s assistance met the actual difficulty. Such evidence can support a claim of learning while leaving continuity of understanding and wider awakening open to examination (§§1.4, 1.8).

          In food production, usefulness concerns what the produce can fulfil in nourishment. Labour develops that usefulness through cultivation and preparation; aesthetic addition is meaningful where it adds convenience to usefulness. Labour assessment concerns exchange, while price records symbolic valuation. Quantities produced and prices received therefore require interpretation through assessed need, actual provision, and the responsibilities fulfilled, including care for those unable to produce (§§1.3, 1.5).

          Dedication gives useful production its place in relationships and humane living. A harvest obtained through deception or degradation of soil can exhibit productive competence while leaving trust or responsibility for regeneration unfulfilled. Assessment must examine nourishment, labour, relationships, and continued productive conditions together. Nourishment is the definite purpose of the produce; the harvest, storage conditions, present need, and regeneration affect its availability and actual fulfilment. The four dimensions operate together: understood purpose, humane thought, just relationships, and useful work jointly determine the adequacy of the activity (§§1.3, 1.5–1.6). Specifying sustainable yields and fair allocation requires factual investigation and practical reasoning (§§7.5, 7.9, 7.11).

          The other accounts ask different questions of these scenes. Advaita examines whether teaching and provision fulfil empirical responsibilities without attachment; their performance can prepare for knowledge but does not produce liberation (§§2.4–2.5). Western approaches assess welfare and opportunity, dignity and permissible exchange, or care and responsiveness, according to the theory (§§3.5–3.6, 3.8–3.9). These assessments need not coincide with the darshan’s named relationship values. Scientific investigation can measure learning outcomes, yields, and their consequences under stated purposes (§§4.1, 4.3–4.5). Such evidence informs assessment of the lesson and harvest; whether guidance or care was fulfilled also depends on the applicable account of relationship and responsibility.

          6. Critical Review

          6.1 Madhyasth Darshan — participation as the standard of value

          Strengths.

          • The account identifies value with the essentiality of active units and distinguishes this ground from its human recognition and fulfilment. Its objectivity claim thereby has definite content in participation, while allowing error in understanding and limitations in the means of fulfilment.
          • The canon of thirty values connects realisation, humane thought, relational behaviour, and material work. The five-faculty account explains their inner activities and harmonies, while the four-dimension map explains their principal expression in living. Together they connect inward fulfilment with conduct and provision.
          • Justice requires normative standards, actual fulfilment, and mutual satisfaction. Kindness, grace, and compassion address need and receptivity; labour evaluation, right-use, and rightful receiving address material conditions.
          • The sixfold articulation of knowledge connects lawful operation and balance with justice, resolution, and truth. It allows technical effectiveness, relational fulfilment, and overall purpose to be examined together while preserving their different responsibilities.
          • Self-examination and evidence in relationships and work supply criteria of evaluation. The texts acknowledge learning within a disordered tradition and distinguish reported achievements from a wider institutional order still to be realised.

          Weaknesses.

          • The identity of essentiality and value requires a defence that an interlocutor can assess without first accepting the whole ontology. It remains necessary to explain why a proposed form of participation has normative authority and how competing accounts can be adjudicated.
          • Complete correctness is connected with awakening, while awakening is recognised through correct understanding and conduct. The account has educational entry points, but a public method for resolving disagreement about an individual’s claimed establishment still needs specification.
          • Dedication, endurance, inclusion, and completeness distinguish ways values are related, but they do not yet determine allocation in every case of scarce resources and legitimate competing claims. Their domains and practical implications need inspectable decisions without treating disagreement itself as proof of inferior standing.
          • Reports of successful education and living are evidence to investigate, rather than independent demonstrations of universal efficacy. The claims concerning , continuity, and institutional scale need distinct forms of verification.
          • Saturation and constitutional completeness are philosophical commitments whose relation to contemporary physical and biological explanations remains to be established. A model of activity can clarify these commitments, but explaining how conduct and its consequences inform learning does not yet explain realisation (anubhav).

          offers a connected theory of value in participation; its further development depends on publicly assessable warrants and worked applications of its own standards.

          6.2 Advaita Vedanta — liberation and the limits of acquisition

          Strengths.

          • The distinction between liberation and produced attainments provides a sustained account of why acquisition and pleasurable experience may fail to answer the deepest question of fulfilment.
          • Ethical preparation and action without attachment give the transformation of the evaluator a practical role. Teachings on compassion and loka-sangraha preserve responsibilities within empirical life.
          • Primary dearness of the Self and the analysis of superimposition connect what people seek with how they understand themselves. This challenges identification of fulfilment with the possessions and experiences of a presently conceived individual ego.

          Weaknesses.

          • The account’s ultimate ground depends on non-duality and the authority of the means of knowledge through which it is disclosed. The move from familiar dearness to the non-dual Self requires argument in a comparison that does not presuppose that ground.
          • The relation between empirical responsibilities and ultimate non-doership needs careful interpretation when used to guide institutions, conflict resolution, or distribution. The cited teachings give ethical direction without supplying every institutional detail.
          • The claim that produced goods cannot provide ultimate fulfilment does not alone determine their comparative importance within finite lives. Further practical reasoning is needed to assess particular relationships and material needs.

          Advaita Vedanta connects dearness, mistaken identification, and the ordering of human purposes; its account of ultimate fulfilment leaves further work in specifying the comparative claims of finite goods.

          6.3 Western value theory — distinctions and competing explanations

          Strengths.

          • The distinctions between intrinsic and final value, welfare and moral rightness, ranking and commensurability, and actual and fitting attitudes prevent several different questions from being answered by one ambiguous use of value.
          • Realist, constructivist, phenomenological, care, and capability approaches supply substantive alternatives to simple preference accounts. Evaluator development and the social conditions of fulfilment are well represented within this range.
          • Arguments concerning dignity, exploitation, recognition, and capabilities make distribution and vulnerability central concerns rather than consequences to be considered only after an abstract theory is complete.

          Weaknesses.

          • Rival accounts disagree about the grounds of value and the standards of correct response. No single position can be assessed as the settled Western answer.
          • The separation of metaethics, welfare, action, and political economy can leave difficult work in explaining how their results fit together. Conceptual clarity within one inquiry does not automatically produce a coherent account of living.
          • Each proposal must show how its standards are known and applied. Appeals to appropriate attitudes, ideal reasoning, flourishing, or intuition relocate this evidential burden rather than removing it.

          Western value theory supplies exact distinctions and substantial rival explanations that a comparative account must address individually.

          6.4 The sciences — mechanisms, outcomes and explanatory limits

          Strengths.

          • Empirical methods make claims about learning, provision, health, trust, and institutions answerable to evidence beyond an advocate’s testimony.
          • The distinction between reported experience, life evaluation, behaviour, and measured conditions enables more discriminating tests of claims about well-being.
          • Reanalysis and conflicting findings can improve an account, as the income–well-being debate illustrates. This capacity for correction is a resource for studying value education and institutional proposals.

          Weaknesses.

          • Operational measures need an explicit connection to the philosophical claim being tested. The measures reviewed here do not operationalise established relationship values or adjudicate the darshan’s usefulness-constancy claim; those comparisons require an account of what would count as evidence.
          • Associations do not establish causes, and findings in particular populations do not immediately establish universal regularities. Claims about education or prosperity need appropriate designs and limits.
          • Empirical success in predicting a choice does not settle whether it is justified; neither a neural common scale nor successful aggregation establishes moral equivalence. Selecting and weighting outcomes requires normative reasoning, and failure of an objective proxy does not by itself establish value pluralism.

          The sciences can test many consequences of a value theory while leaving the justification of its fundamental standards to a wider argument.

          7. Open Problems

          7.1 How should the ordering of values guide difficult decisions?

          The darshan’s relations of dedication, endurance, inclusion, and common or complete value provide connected grounds for ordering (§1.6). Dedication directs means towards their more comprehensive purposes; endurance concerns persistence or non-transformation; inclusion concerns comprehensiveness; commonness and completeness characterise relationship values. The four harmonies have cumulative fulfilment in living. Their combined direction gives humane and relational fulfilment priority over accumulation pursued for its own sake. The remaining task is to work out decisions within this order when legitimate needs exceed the available means.

          A gain in productive capacity alongside damaged trust is a useful test: what requires repair, how should resources support that repair, and what would count as restored fulfilment? Further cases should test whether endurance determines priority between competing needs, what inclusion entails for the fulfilment of an included value, and how love’s completeness guides a particular responsibility. These questions concern the practical consequences of the stated relations; a numerical scale of exchange or a general rule of compensation cannot be assumed from their presence.

          7.2 How can claimed awakening be assessed without circular authority?

          Self-evaluation, examination of both sides of a relationship, study, and observable conduct are available resources (MVD, p. 87; JV, pp. 98, 139). The accounts of , learning through meaningful participation, and study of meaning explain how a learner can begin to examine understanding and conduct (§1.8). The difficulty concerns how these resources establish or correct a claim of awakening.

          The unresolved question concerns contested authority. When a teacher claims established understanding and a learner finds an apparent error, what shared procedure determines whether the difficulty lies in the teaching, the learner, or the application? The distinction between knowing the more developed and measuring it by an inadequate existing measure allows inquiry; it should not make a claim of development immune to correction (KD, p. 37). Documented explanations, outcomes, counterexamples, and the learner’s freedom to question should be made part of a worked account.

          The four harmonies have definite constitutive relations within the darshan (§1.2). Uncertainty about whether an observer has established their presence in a particular person concerns evidence. It does not make those relations indeterminate. Equally, a person’s report of pleasure, calm, satisfaction, or bliss must be interpreted in context before it can establish the specified faculty harmony. A useful assessment should examine the person’s reasons, freedom from contradiction in the relevant activity, fulfilled responsibilities, and continuity across circumstances, while keeping ordinary self-description distinct from the full claim of realised understanding.

          7.3 How does mutual satisfaction include everyone affected?

          Justice requires recognised relationships, value-fulfilment, evaluation, and mutual satisfaction. It already asks more than whether two people consent. A further account should show how to assess relationships where one person cannot communicate a judgment, lacks relevant information, or depends on the evaluator for care.

          The appropriate tests include a young child, an adult requiring continuing support, and an agreement whose effects fall on absent neighbours. Actual provision and fulfilled responsibilities must matter alongside expressed assent. Satisfaction within the immediate pair cannot alone establish that obligations to other affected people have been fulfilled (§1.4).

          7.4 What warrants the identification of essentiality and value?

          The texts explicitly identify essentiality, essential nature, and value, and connect human with happiness and humane participation (MVD, pp. 47, 124–125; SB, pp. 50, 87). The question is therefore the warrant for a stated identification, rather than the recovery of a missing definition.

          A further argument should explain how essential participation is distinguished from a merely recurrent behaviour and why that participation provides a reason for action. It should also explain what would count against an asserted identification. Otherwise a dispute about the good can be redescribed as a dispute about essentiality without making the original disagreement easier to resolve.

          Effective power and persistent activity occur in both humane and inhumane conduct. The warrant for an obligation must therefore concern the character and fulfilment of participation, rather than its efficacy alone. Likewise, natural lawfulness and balance require an intervening account of relationships, knowledge, and humane purpose before they can establish a particular human obligation (§§1.1, 1.8).

          7.5 How is usefulness assessed under changing conditions?

          The connected account distinguishes definite usefulness from the temporal conditions of its fulfilment (§1.5). The wheat and sesame examples identify what material things contribute to nourishment, while also allowing interference to reduce their usefulness (JV, pp. 123, 137–138). The temporality of usefulness and beauty concerns needs, use, exchange, and deployment (MAD, reader p. 51). Read in this context, constancy need not mean that every specimen, condition, or use remains unchanged. It concerns the definite material function by which production and use can be evaluated.

          The practical questions concern the conditions under which that function is retained, improved, or impaired. How should differences of quality, storage, processing, access, and actual need enter an assessment? What distinguishes a useful improvement from a rise in symbolic valuation alone? A fuller account should explain how definite function guides these changing assessments and how comparisons are made between materially different ways of meeting a need. The conceptual distinction supplies the starting point; particular judgments require knowledge of the thing and the circumstances.

          7.6 How should the value map guide assessment?

          The thirty-value canon and its principal expression across the four dimensions provide an integrated map of human fulfilment (§1.3). The classification is explicit in PS pp. 151 and 154, and the relationship pairings in MAD reader p. 30. Need and symbolic valuation explain the purposes and representation of material provision within this account. The practical task is to show how the map guides assessment of an activity in which several values are fulfilled together.

          Care for a child, for example, calls for humane intention, adequate provision, relational fulfilment, and understanding of the whole responsibility. Generosity can express both humane disposition and care; its repeated place in the classification explains connected functions. An assessment should examine how these functions support one another and identify the particular failure when one is absent. The 122 activity members describe positions within , the thirty-value canon identifies forms of fulfilment, and the four dimensions organise their expression in living. Developing assessments that preserve these relations without counting the same evidence as several independent achievements is a substantive task (§§1.2–1.4, 5.9).

          7.7 How are rival legitimate obligations resolved?

          The claim that correct parties do not quarrel supplies the darshan’s position on harmony (SB, PDF p. 146). Recognition, mutual fulfilment, and common resolution are its proposed direction. The difficult case is a conflict between legitimate needs when existing means are insufficient: two patients need the only available treatment, or protecting one community shifts a burden to another.

          A worked account should identify relevant facts, responsibilities, available alternatives, and the reason for its allocation. It should distinguish a remediable error from a material limit that remains after misunderstanding is corrected. This would show how the harmony claim guides action and how unavoidable loss is acknowledged, rather than treating the existence of disagreement as a completed diagnosis.

          7.8 How does outward learning become establishment and continuity?

          The texts connect sanskar, understood meaning, practice, self-evaluation, and the acceptance of evidenced conduct with further expression (MVD, pp. 83, 90, 94; KD, pp. 84, 112–113). They distinguish intermittent meaningful conduct from continuity. These resources support a positive educational account across the activities of jeevan, including the learner’s visualisation, desire, understanding, and resolve (§1.8).

          The further task is to document the transition. Constitutional completeness identifies an inherent capacity; establishment concerns understood value in state; fulfilment expresses it through activity; continuity concerns its sustained evidence across circumstances. Contact with understood conduct, participation, reflection, and self-evaluation form a connected educational route (MVD, pp. 83, 94; KD, pp. 18–19, 112–113). What differentiates instructed performance, independently understood fulfilment, and such continuity? How are relapse, conflicting incentives, and mistaken self-assessment recognised? Evidence should include the learner’s explanation and self-examination as well as conduct and its effects, with each claim assessed against the faculty activity or relationship it concerns.

          The continuity from action through evaluation to revised understanding also requires discrimination between several outcomes. A learner can correct a technique, recognise a responsibility, clarify a concept, or claim anubhav; each calls for appropriate evidence. An account of qualitative refinement should explain how such changes affect subsequent participation. It should preserve the distinction between a definite constitutive relation of harmony and the process through which that harmony is achieved and evidenced in a particular person.

          7.9 How does value across the four orders guide ecological decisions?

          The darshan attributes value to every unit, directs humans towards nurture, and relates resource use to regeneration. Pranic essential nature includes vitalising and devitalising effects relative to living bodies, while animal essential nature includes cruel and uncruel conduct (MVD, pp. 51–52; SB, pp. 179–182). Essentiality therefore does not imply that every interaction benefits its immediate recipient. Human responsibilities for nurture and regeneration require an account of these different effects and their place in coexistence (SB, pp. 141, 200–201).

          Further work should examine habitat loss, competing forms of life, restoration times, and effects on future people. The account must explain how participation and regeneration are assessed when there is no single immediate beneficiary and when one useful activity disrupts another form of coexistence.

          7.10 What evidence supports the educational and institutional claims?

          Self-examination, fulfilled relationships, useful work, and reported educational results supply different kinds of evidence. Nagraj’s accounts include learning and self-experimentation, while the discussion of exchange in Adhyatmvad distinguishes reported achievements in production, prosperity, and justice from the wider exchange order still awaited at the time of writing (JV, pp. 98, 139; SB, p. 294; AVD, p. 52).

          These historical reports should not be presented as proof that the full institutional order was implemented or as a statement of its present reach. Longitudinal studies could examine learning, sustained conduct, provision, and relationships, with explicit measures and comparison conditions. Institutional evaluation should separately examine distribution, ecological effects, dependency, and the ability to correct failures. Such research would test specified claims without assuming that a questionnaire exhausts jeevan or that inward evaluation forbids external investigation.

          7.11 How should labour be evaluated and provision distributed?

          Labour deployment, labour-value assessment, protection from exploitation, and profit-and-loss-free exchange are explicit commitments (MVD, pp. 260, 268–269; SB, p. 237). The next questions concern their implementation: how should differences of skill, effort, training, risk, tools, time, quality, and productivity enter an assessment? What happens when socially necessary care yields no exchangeable product, or when a useful product depends on accumulated knowledge contributed by many people?

          Remuneration, gift, and award already widen the account of rightful wealth beyond an individual’s immediate production (AVD, p. 170). A developed allocation procedure should explain provision for children, older people, people with disabilities, and anyone temporarily unable to work. It should also distinguish the evaluation of labour from the urgency of need and from the basic standing of the person who receives support.

          7.12 How are equal human standing and unequal development related?

          The account recognises common human purpose and capacity while distinguishing levels of understanding and conduct. Developmental differences can matter when assigning a task or assessing competence. They do not by themselves justify differences in basic worth, protection, or the right to question a judgment.

          A full treatment should show how conferred rights and duties are applied to less experienced learners and people whose capacities differ. It should make clear how recognition and care protect a person’s standing while their understanding or productive ability is still developing. This is especially important wherever claims of higher value or awakening carry authority within a family, educational setting, or institution.

          8. Conclusion

          identifies value with essentiality in coexistence. Units are activity, and their form, properties, essential nature, and concern aspects of that participation. A unit’s value is present through its essentiality; human recognition can understand it correctly or mistake it. Awakening concerns the development of understanding and fulfilment in the active conscious unit, .

          The structural account specifies what this fulfilment comprises. Happiness, peace, contentment, and bliss are harmonies between defined faculty activities. Established values in state have corresponding expressions in motion, while justice joins recognition of relationships, value-fulfilment, evaluation, and mutual satisfaction. Constitutional capacity, achieved harmony, and evidence of attainment remain distinct. Their distinction allows education to acknowledge meaningful participation while examining the understanding and continuity it demonstrates.

          The thirty-value canon gives this account a coherent articulation across realisation, thought, behaviour, and work. Jeevan values express experiential harmony, human values guide humane thought, relationship values take form in behaviour, and object values are established and fulfilled through work. Dedication orders these connected purposes, while endurance, inclusion, and completeness explain further aspects of their relation. Definite usefulness guides material provision whose conditions and fulfilment are temporal. Evaluation therefore considers the activity as a whole: its understood purpose, the values fulfilled, the means employed, and the continuity of their contribution.

          Advaita locates dearness in the Self, identical with , while Madhyasth Darshan locates value in units’ essentiality. Western theories distinguish grounds, welfare, and exchange; the scientific studies reviewed here test mechanisms and outcomes without supplying the darshan’s classes of mulya. These comparisons sharpen the questions of normative warrant, disputed authority, dependency, allocation, and public evidence. The darshan’s contribution is a connected account of value’s ground, its constitution in activity, and its fulfilment in living. Further work must show how its distinct ordering relations guide difficult decisions and how claims of established understanding are assessed through explanation, conduct, and sustained participation.

          Evaluation can clarify the basis of subsequent participation. These changes concern understanding and conduct within the constitutionally complete jeevan. The notation specifies the content that development must fulfil. Explaining how learning proceeds, how long it takes, and how realisation occurs requires a further account of development (§7.8).

A record can mark a claim as supported, contradicted, or not yet established; these describe the assessment of a claim.           Essentiality, activity and the enumeration

          The identification of essentiality with value and its connection with essential nature and are explicit in MVD pp. 47, 124–125, SB pp. 50, 87, and AVD p. 184. The four aspects jointly describe the active unit whose characteristic participation has this essentiality. PS p. 154, under “मौलिक पद्धति”, explicitly enumerates thirty values as four , six human, nine established, nine expressed, and two object values. The entry “मानवीयतापूर्ण आचरण” on p. 151 repeats the classification and identifies the two object values as usefulness and aesthetic value. The canon in §1.3 retains this enumeration. It counts places in the classification: udarta occurs as both a human value and the expression paired with care.

          The mapping of the canon to the four dimensions is a systematic exposition of their connected functions. MVD p. 18 names the four dimensions; PS p. 149 joins the five value groups with experiential authenticity, resolved thought, social behaviour, and self-reliant vocation. JV p. 138 connects the four jeevan harmonies with realisation-based living. The humane dispositions in MVD p. 125 and PS p. 151 give the thought–dharma relation in MVD pp. 137 and 156 its humane content; MVD p. 94 explicitly relates wholesome tendencies to both thought and conduct. MVD pp. 331–340 develop the established–expressed relationship pairs, while PS pp. 93 and 179 identify vocational values as usefulness and artistic value. Taken together, these support jeevan values with anubhav, human values with vichar, relationship values with vyavahar, and object values with karya.

          The dimensional map describes principal roles in human living; the faculty tables describe the activities through which those roles are understood, felt, and fulfilled. Realisation-based harmony belongs to the coordinated jeevan, and PS p. 78 describes its expression through authenticity, resolution, and communicated justice. This interrelation explains the map’s unity. Need value in MAD reader p. 34 and symbolic value in MVD p. 183 specify the purposes and representation of provision within the wider account (§§1.5–1.6, 7.6).

          MVD p. 203 uses bhav- broadly for values and evaluation. MVD p. 338 and AVD p. 92 also identify a particular bhav/samveg pair at . Bhav is defined through essentiality, value, responsibility, and participation; samveg is the impetus associated with meaningful union, whose direction can be towards advancement or decline (MVD, pp. 43, 338). The shared term does not make all value-activity one position at vritti, nor does the pairing establish a universal causal sequence from sentiment to every outward action.

          The awakened enumeration has 122 activity members in 61 pairs: two at atma, four at , sixteen at , thirty-six at vritti, and sixty-four at (MVD, p. 323; AVD, p. 91). This counts activity positions, not 122 distinct names or 122 relationship values. The nine established–expressed pairs in §1.3 are a specified part of the account; the enumeration does not classify every activity pair as such a relationship-value pair.

          Established and expressed values

          The exposition adopts the one-to-one correspondence of established values in bal/sthiti and expressed, civic, or shishta values in gati. The table retains the paired positions in AVD pp. 91–94 and the definitions in MVD pp. 328–348. The educational account connects contact with understood conduct, participation, examination of meaning, and establishment. MVD p. 94 describes awakening initiated through contact with an awakened person and relates wholesome tendencies to thought, practice, and eventual evidence. MVD p. 83 and KD pp. 112–113 connect apprehension, acceptance, and renewed expression. Together with KD pp. 18–19 and 84 and SB pp. 237 and 294, these support outward participation as an entry into developing independent understanding. The distinction between imitation and understood fulfilment remains necessary when assessing a particular learner.

          MAD reader p. 30 explicitly identifies the principal nine established values, their corresponding nine expressions, trust as common value, and love as complete value. This supplies direct classification evidence beyond JV p. 108, which introduces the nine relationship values as examples. KD p. 26 uses an illustrative list including daya, sneha, udarta, gaurav, adar, vatsalya, shraddha, prem, and kritagyata. Six Hindi terms coincide directly with the JV list; adar and samman should not silently be counted as identical. The principal nine-value classification is retained; the illustrative KD list is not silently substituted for it or treated as proof that the principal taxonomy is uncertain. This does not assert that every occurrence of mulya throughout the corpus belongs to those nine values. Naturalness, concordance, humility, and devoutness are running English terms for the relevant expressed values; ordinary English associations do not exhaust their source definitions.

          The table’s meanings follow the technical definitions in MVD pp. 331–340. In particular, sahajta involves clarity, authenticity, and coherence of behaviour, norm, thought, and realisation (p. 332); saumyata involves voluntary self-restraint and civility (p. 335); and sauhardta involves faithful presentation of the conception, realisation, memory, and recognition accepted by the person (p. 340). Their behavioural illustrations should retain these meanings rather than substitute spontaneity, gentleness, or warmth alone.

          Activity, saturation and the four aspects

          The effort–motion–result alignment follows SB pp. 60–62. The text places effort/strength with dharma and svabhava, motion with svabhava and guna, and result with roop and amaratva. The compressed account in §1.1 concerns aspects of the active unit; amaratva is retained here with its surrounding constitutional-completeness context, rather than generalised into the immortality of every present material configuration. The overlap of svabhava means this is not a one-to-one derivation or a three-stage chronology. No physical potential, numerical force law, or transition equation is inferred from it.

          The account of relative power and characteristic participation draws on Technical Note: Four Aspects of the Active Unit, in its discussions of unit activity, the four aspects, order-wise examples, and the limits of a process interpretation. Its rendering of usefulness as functional significance is analytical and does not make every actual effect morally good. The interpretation of dharma as order-specific innateness must distinguish change within an order from a transition between orders. The human case also distinguishes the constitution of jeevan from its current understanding and conduct.

          Chetana, chaitanya and knowledge

          MVD p. 35 names knowledge as chetana in relation to chaitanya; pp. 74–75 distinguish actionless, non-transforming consciousness/knowledge from transformations in nature and identify chaitanya as constitutionally complete jeevan. The exposition does not treat as becoming a unit, performing evaluation, or receiving anubhav. Knowledge as omnipresent ground and knowledge realised and evidenced in human living are connected uses, not an additional substance and its transmission. Sentience includes evaluation but is not exhaustively defined by it, and constitutional completeness does not guarantee awakened evaluation. SB pp. 63–64 and 249 supply the distinctions between natural mutuality, sentient capacities, and correct human recognition and fulfilment.

          The faculties, qualitative refinement and evidence

          The five-faculty table follows KD §3.8, p. 84’s Hindi account of sthiti/gati. In AVD pp. 91–94 the English strength/power headings are reversed relative to the Hindi; §1.2 follows the checked Hindi relation. Chitta names the faculty, chitran its visualising activity, and chintan its contemplative activity. The integrated exposition describes dependencies of meaning and expression; it does not posit a neural mechanism or a fixed temporal passage through five successive compartments.

          MVD p. 83 presents interdependent relations among language, meaning, evaluation, worldview, and the perceiver’s capacities, returning to language. Words communicate meanings concerning existing realities; what the person apprehends depends on view, evaluation, capacity, ability, and receptivity. These relations concern activities of chitta, whose adequacy depends on understanding. Study of meaning can reach understanding and realisation and become available for renewed expression (MVD, p. 83; KD, pp. 112–113). This is a cycle of apprehension and communication, not a linear causal sequence that generates the value of the indicated unit. Constitutional completeness identifies the capacity of jeevan; it does not establish that a present evaluation is correct.

          The realisation–behaviour–application evidences (MVD, p. 12) and the knowledge–law–regulation–balance–justice–dharma–truth passage (p. 174) connect understanding and living without making their terms interchangeable outside that argument. MAD reader p. 45 grounds the account of aapyayan and parimarjan; neither is equated with memorisation or habit formation alone.

          MVD pp. 99–100 distinguish realisation-oriented understanding in study from realisation-based understanding and its expression. Page 156 connects atma with realisation, buddhi–chitta with resolution, and vritti–mun with just behaviour. These relations are not exclusive ownership of justice, dharma, or truth by individual faculties: all three are accepted in buddhi and participate in contemplation and deliberation. The eight dimensions of visualisation on p. 327 describe an activity of chitta, not the production of the imaged unit by a perceiver.

          Sixfold knowledge and continuing evaluation

          The continuity of knowledge, law, regulation, balance, justice, dharma, and truth is directly stated in MVD p. 174, with evidence of all six through realisation on p. 80. The natural-order conformance descriptions follow SB pp. 236–237. Their particular illustrations do not allocate each term exclusively to one order. Dharma as innateness and dharma as the humane perspective of resolution in thought are related uses whose distinctions remain necessary (MVD, pp. 125, 137, 156, 174).

          A State-Dynamic Model of Coexistence, §§1–3, 7–8, and 10–11, develops the analytical connection between unit activity, human orientation, consequences, and later evaluation.

          The present paper uses that connection to clarify continuing evaluation and learning in §§1.4 and 1.8. The model’s distinction of actual and operatively recognised mutuality and its grouping of consequences into internal, bodily, relational, and natural domains are analytical reconstructions. They do not enlarge configurational parinam into a source-stated definition of every human consequence, or make feedback a sufficient cause of anubhav. The applications in §5.9 use the forms of value, dedication, and temporal distinctions developed in this study.

          The model’s grouping into two coupled triads and its projections of niyam–, niyantran–dharma, and santulan– are likewise analytical, non-exclusive mappings. They are not adopted as primary-text identities or faculty assignments. A family or institution is an arrangement of persons, relationships, and work; the evaluators are the participating human beings. No collective jeevan or new ontological unit is inferred from social orderliness.

          Harmonies and contextual uses of jeevan values

          The faculty-harmony account in JV p. 138 and MVD p. 327, and the cumulative fulfilment in KD §3.12, p. 112, supply the faculty and fulfilment columns of the harmony table. The fulfilment column preserves the cumulative sequence; it does not assign fearlessness or coexistence alone as an exclusive outward equivalent. Their distinctness supports separate examination, but does not alone prove the impossibility of every aggregate assessment. The formal appendix keeps the named relations distinct because the cited texts supply no numerical scale or exchange rates.

          The scope of fulfilment described in §§1.2 and A.3 connects inner satisfaction with self-governed conduct in relationships and wider orderliness (JV, pp. 53, 62, 140; AVD, p. 228; KD, pp. 112–113). This connection does not replace the cumulative fulfilment table with four exclusive correspondences or make a family or society the bearer of a collective jeevan.

          MVD p. 328 locates the named anand activity at buddhi, while p. 323 also uses bliss language at atma. The table follows the specific named positions rather than asserting an exclusive location for every use of bliss. KD p. 37 also speaks of resolution and prosperity as jeevan values in its context. The fourfold list is retained where the four harmonies are the subject; contextual lists are not silently expanded or reduced to force one enumeration. Contentment in MVD p. 331 includes readiness for production beyond need as well as absence of deficiency-sentiment.

          MAD reader p. 47 explicitly equates absence of opposition in each adjacent faculty pair with a named value and with a quality of behaviour, thought, desire, or understanding. This strengthens the constitutive reading adopted in §§1.2 and A.1. Its aparadh-hin is rendered behaviour free of wrongdoing; it is not confined to compliance with a prevailing penal code. Page 51 connects the four harmonies with the experience of established values and describes established values as enduring across time and place. The study’s term structural names these definite relations of activity, rather than a claim about measured anatomy or a new spatial arrangement of the jeevan atom.

          MVD pp. 343 and 346 distinguish sensory pleasantness from happiness defined as resolution. Read with JV pp. 138–140 and the cumulative fulfilment in KD p. 112, the faculty relations explain how enjoyment is integrated into more comprehensive understanding and participation. The four harmonies express connected fulfilment across jeevan. Their specified content matters: coherent beliefs can still misrecognise a relationship, and a successful plan alone does not establish the understanding and freedom from lack involved in santosh. The eight dimensions of visualisation concern an activity through which this understanding can guide planning. Feeling the harmonies belongs to jeevan; their named faculty positions identify particular relations within that whole.

          PS pp. 40–41 defines asvadan through reception, satisfaction or its expectation, and the distinction between sensations under delusion and values upon awakening. It expressly names ruchi-mulak, mulya-mulak, and lakshya-mulak asvadan. Read with MVD pp. 124 and 203 and JV pp. 138–140, these forms support the developmental movement from sensory dominance towards relational value and the comprehensive human goal. The exposition preserves that direction while treating the forms as orientations that can be integrated in living. The four faculty harmonies describe the constitution of fulfilment; the tasting forms describe its orientation in mun.

          The appendix’s harmony predicates, relationship requirements, and conjunction for justice are analytical notation. Their substantive content comes from the cited definitions, not from the symbols themselves. They distinguish constitutive definiteness, an actual person’s state, and an observer’s evidence. No stochastic rule, numerical utility function, automatic passage from logical consistency to fulfilment, or deterministic law of awakening is attributed to the primary texts.

          The Hindi sentence on correct evaluation in MVD p. 203 names under- and over-evaluation, whereas its English adds mis-evaluation. The triadic account in §1.7 is therefore cited to MVD pp. 152, 306 and SB p. 146. The illustrations in §1.7 come from SB PDF p. 146, which gives both the rope–snake and iron–gold comparisons in Hindi and English. MVD p. 306 prints nirmulyan (निर्मूल्यन); occurrences of amulyan elsewhere should retain their own source wording.           Human development and relational fulfilment

          MVD pp. 60–61 fixes the humane and higher-humane triads and the conditions distinguishing kindness, grace, and compassion. The relevant distinction is between available content or means (vastu) and humane receptivity (patrata); it should not be confined to money and occupational skill. SB p. 180 reverses kindness and compassion in the English ordering relative to its Hindi; the exposition follows the checked definitions. SB p. 189 defines heenta through deceit, fraud, duplicity, and hypocrisy. That evaluative term should not be read as a person’s disability or lower productive capacity.

MVD pp. 60, 65, and 304 places them in humane living; p. 304 explicitly describes the humane human with these motives as awakened, while retaining the need for further participation as deific-human and divine-human. Page 321 discusses loka-eshana in deific-human living. No separate pre-human stage called “Awakening Consciousness” is introduced here. MVD p. 153 distinguishes partial humane-human from complete divine-human nirakarshan. The exposition retains those distinctions without forcing every contextual use of awakening or freedom from delusion into a single interchangeable label. MVD p. 250’s account of sibling evaluation concerns a pre-adolescent child in an awakened family; it does not establish a universal first onset of evaluation.

          Ordering, production and temporal claims

          The relations in §1.6 contribute to a connected qualitative ordering. MAD reader p. 34 supplies the dedication of usefulness to need, need to expressed value, expressed to established value, established to human value, and human to jeevan value. This gives material means their purpose in humane fulfilment. MVD p. 56 connects sociality, need, production, use, humaneness, and undivided sociality in a returning sequence. MVD p. 43 supplies endurance as a criterion of higher value, p. 47 supplies inclusion across the four orders, and pp. 219–222 develop higher and lower value in social participation. MAD reader pp. 30 and 43 identify trust as common value and love as complete value. Together these passages support a hierarchy of mutually supporting purposes, with distinguishable criteria of endurance, comprehensiveness, and relational fulfilment. Quantitative exchange rates would require a further construction. MVD pp. 58 and 67 define acquisitive profit through higher-for-lower exchange; that practice reverses the service of useful means to just participation.

          JV p. 123 calls material usefulness definite through wheat’s enduring role in nourishment and immediately allows human interference to reduce it. The sesame example in PDF pp. 137–138 appears within the account of definite needs, production, and right-use; it distinguishes useful function from the pursuit of accumulation. MAD reader p. 51 calls usefulness and beauty temporal because needs, use, exchange, and deployment are temporal. Read with the account of production in MVD pp. 56–57 and symbolic valuation in SB pp. 211–212, these passages support a distinction between definite material function and its changing embodiment, availability, and fulfilment. This is the connected reading adopted in §§1.5 and 7.5. It explains why changing a price does not itself change nourishment, while changing the thing or the conditions of use can change what is actually fulfilled. It does not assert a conserved physical quantity of usefulness. PS p. 151 identifies two object values; the local use of vastu mulya for usefulness in the sesame passage retains that context.

          MVD p. 56 defines prayog as endeavour until success and utpadan as labour deployed in a developed process that generalises that application and seeks abundant production. The exposition retains both the exploratory and productive phases. MVD p. 57 defines upyog through deployment of means for production and service. AVD p. 170 specifies pratiphal, paritosh, and puraskar as the three modes of receiving rightful wealth: the fruit of labour, freely conferred gift, and award. SB p. 237’s Hindi differentiates evaluation in justice, labour-value assessment in exchange, and labour deployment in production; its English does not fully reproduce that paragraph. The shared attention to labour supports comparison with Marx, whose specific criterion of socially necessary labour time remains distinct (§§3.8, 5.7).

          The extended fear-and-temptation discussion is SB chapter 9, item 11, PDF pp. 290–295, ending before item 12 on p. 295. It is not chapter 11 beginning at PDF p. 199. Its political and educational claims, like MVD p. 199’s account of sentient atoms, are treated as the darshan’s positions rather than independently verified scientific or historical facts. AVD p. 52 is a dated report distinguishing experienced production and justice from the wider exchange system then still awaited.

          Comparative terms and verification

          Value in a theory of normative properties, value as essentiality, economic commodity value, and a neural decision variable are not interchangeable senses. The comparisons examine relations between the accounts without treating a shared English word as proof of a shared concept.

          The Advaita account brings dearness, human purposes, discrimination, and superimposition into an axiological comparison; these are related concepts, not translations of one technical term equivalent to mulya. Primary and secondary love have direct support in Shankara’s commentary on BU 2.4.5. Reading this as an account of value’s ground is the study’s comparative articulation. Neither derivative dearness nor dependence on is silently equated with the modern denial of intrinsic empirical value. TU 2.1.1 gives satyam jnanam anantam; the account of being, consciousness, and bliss combines that teaching with 2.7–2.9. Conditioned pleasure does not add to Brahman’s fullness, and the anandamaya sheath in the inward inquiry is not identified with the absolute without qualification.

          The four purusharthas are an inherited Indian framework. BG 18.34 groups dharma, kama, and artha in its account of attachment to results; BSB 1.1.1 identifies liberation as the highest human objective. These passages support the Advaita ordering without being presented as one textual enumeration of all four. BG 7.11’s commentary illustrates permissible desire through eating and drinking for bodily maintenance; BG 16.12 condemns unjust acquisition. The governance of finite pursuits by dharma is stated through those constraints. The Vivekachudamani supplies a pedagogical account of discrimination and qualification, with its disputed authorship retained.

          BSB’s Introduction supplies mutual superimposition; its 1.1.4 supplies Shankara’s own rope–snake comparison with liberating knowledge. Ordinary correction identifies the rope in both traditions; the philosophical extension of corrected error is what differs in §5.3. BG 3.8 concerns prescribed action and bodily maintenance, while 3.19 supplies the explicit instruction to act without attachment. BG 3.17–3.18 distinguishes the realised knower’s freedom from personal obligation, and 3.25–3.26 permits action for others’ guidance. The continued significance of empirical conduct does not reinstate obligatory individual doership after knowledge. BSB 2.1.13–2.1.14 preserves empirical distinctions while denying objects’ independent reality. The limits concerning usefulness, labour, and price in §5.7 refer to the examined passages, not the entire Advaita tradition.

          The scientific discussion separates reward prediction error from decision valuation, cross-cultural regularity from exceptionless moral agreement, association from causation, and proxy failure from a proof of irreducible plurality. The 2010 income finding is read with the 2021 study and 2023 reanalysis. The critical questions concern the passages and proposals examined here; they do not assert that the wider literature contains no further treatment of education, constitution, economics, or institutional practice.
