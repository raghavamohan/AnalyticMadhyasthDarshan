## Book proposal

This primer is a book extension of the project, maintained in `Primer/` separately from the site's catalog studies. Its development is tracked as `BOOK-01` in [PENDING.md](../PENDING.md#extensions).

This book introduces Madhyasth Darshan through the questions and responsibilities of ordinary human life. Its central question is: **What becomes possible in human living through understanding, and what does its fulfilment require?** The title, *The Human Possibility*, names the capacity to understand ourselves and existence, fulfil our relationships, and participate consciously in humane living. The book develops the philosophy’s account of this possibility through inner life, relationships, work, society and the rest of nature. Babaji’s literal endeavour that every human being live as *dev-manav* gives this inquiry its full direction; the opening wish introduces that purpose and Chapter 20 explains its meaning in the account of awakening.

Each chapter begins with something the reader can recognise, opens a question worth staying with, and develops the philosophy through an explanation that returns to living. A shared meal, dependable care, learning a skill or purposeful work can show a human aspiration already finding some fulfilment. Questions also emerge from misunderstanding and unmet need. Develop what understanding makes possible and the conditions of its fulfilment: competence, dependable conduct, actual material provision and shared arrangements. Familiar experience opens the inquiry; it does not settle every claim in advance.

The intended reader is an interested adult or older teenager with no background in philosophy. A working length of about 40,000–50,000 words allows twenty relatively short chapters, an opening portrait and wish, a brief preface, a small glossary, chapter source notes, and a closing Hindi song of gratitude to Babaji. The length is a planning estimate, not a reason to compress an explanation that needs space.

The book presents the philosophy affirmatively and persuasively. Its persuasion comes from connected reasoning, recognisable situations, and the possibility of examining the account in life. Philosophical comparisons, academic debates, formal models, and extensive terminology are outside this volume's purpose.

The six parts develop one inquiry: what human beings seek; the reality we belong to and our capacity to understand it; how understanding finds expression in values and relationships; the care, provision and skills through which we participate; arrangements that sustain humane life beyond familiar people; and the continuity and fulfilment of understanding. Each part widens the question while keeping earlier responsibilities in view. Material from the papers is redistributed according to this learning sequence. A chapter is a fresh explanation for the general reader, rather than an abridged paper.

Four connected aspirations give this movement a recurring thread: resolution within the person (*samadhan*), prosperity in the family (*samriddhi*), fearlessness and trust in society (*abhay*), and coexistence with nature (*sah-astitva*). Introduce them in Chapter 1 and return to them as the reader's understanding grows. The six parts give room to develop their connections: family life also needs resolution and trust, work connects provision with nature, and awakening is expressed across all these relationships. Coexistence receives its full account as the nature of existence in Chapter 4, alongside its expression in responsible human participation.

The method of inquiry begins with the opening chapters, before the fuller account of existence. Chapter 1 invites the reader to consider a proposal; Chapter 3 examines desires, assumptions, and the wish for harmony; Chapter 6 develops the relationship between study, understanding, and evidence in living. The distinction between the body's requirements and the conscious self's fulfilment also recurs through relationships, prosperity, education, and technology.

The account of development also becomes visible early. Chapter 5 distinguishes changing material composition, the constitutional completeness of *jeevan*, and the possibility of awakening. Chapters 6 and 19 deepen the questions of understanding and continuity; Chapter 20 brings conscious activity and conduct to their fulfilment. This is the philosophy's account of development, with its own definitions; the book does not present it as a scientific history of the universe or a guaranteed climb from one species or lifetime to another.

*Dharma* connects these stages without requiring a separate chapter. Chapter 1 raises the ordinary question of continuing happiness without adding the term. Chapter 5 first explains *dharma* as a unit's inseparable innateness across the four orders of nature. Chapter 7 returns to happiness as human *dharma* and asks how it becomes fulfilled; Chapter 10 introduces *dharma–adharma* as a perspective for evaluating thought and resolution alongside justice in behaviour and truth in understanding. Chapter 20 brings these senses back into the account of awakened living. The book must distinguish this use of *dharma* from a religious affiliation, a prescribed social role, or an external list of duties.

## Chapter objectives and learning sequence

The order moves from a question the reader can recognise, to a proposal about the human being, to an account of existence, and then to values expressed in relationships, work, society and nature. It ends by distinguishing kinds of continuity and returning to the fulfilment of the human possibility. Each chapter has one main learning task: a particular possibility to understand and the basis on which it can be fulfilled. The table states that contribution; each chapter plan below gives its guiding question and develops the necessary conditions. The fuller classifications in the concept map and source notes need not all be mastered at once.

Inquiry has two passes. Chapters 1 and 3 establish how to approach a proposal and distinguish a wish for harmony from a present assumption. Chapters 4 and 5 state the larger account of existence and nature as proposals to examine. Chapter 6 then gives the fuller account of understanding, realisation, and evidence in living, using a study dialogue in which learners examine borrowed assumptions against lived reality. This is why Chapter 6 follows the account of nature rather than serving as the first permission to question it.

## Connected concept groups and their chapter treatment

Readers should encounter the groups in which Madhyasth Darshan develops its account, as well as the individual concepts. Introduce each central group together in plain English, give its source names together, explain the relationship among its members through a shared situation, and return to the complete group after developing a particular member. A chapter may prepare a group before explaining it fully, but its connections must be intelligible in the main text. Complete lists and precise source wording remain available in the notes.

The familiar order of a list helps readers recognise it across chapters. That order can express a set of related aspects, a correspondence, a developmental distinction or a process. Explain which relationship is meant. Use arrows only when the explanation establishes a direction or succession; use a joined list or table for coordinated aspects. In particular, *asha–vichar–ichha* are connected powers, and *priya–hita–labha* and *nyaya–dharma–satya* are connected evaluative perspectives. Their printed order does not make them three compulsory moments of every decision or establish a one-to-one pairing between the triplets.

## Three motives across forms of human living

Concern for children and continuity, material provision, and recognition gives readers a familiar entrance to the three eshanas. Madhyasth Darshan gives these motives specific humane meanings: *putreshana–vitteshana–lokeshana* belong together with *nyaya–dharma–satya* and *dhirata–virata–udarata*. Explain the connections among what a person seeks, the perspective guiding it and the conduct that expresses it. The motives are more than a neutral list of things everybody wants.

### What the three eshanas mean

| Motive | Meaning and connection to living | Scene and chapter path |
|---|---|---|
| **Putreshana — progeny and human continuity** | The wish for people's participation in family and social order, together with confidence in the continuation of the lineage. Keep children and generational continuity visible while explaining the larger purpose of preparing people to live responsibly. It is not fulfilled merely by having offspring, obtaining an heir or securing a child's prestige. | Introduce with the other two in 3. Return in 20. |
| **Vitteshana — resources for right use** | The wish for material means acquired through justice and directed to right use. Relate it to actual provision, prosperity and participation. Accumulation for comparison or unlimited gain does not become humane vitteshana simply because it concerns money. Rightful remuneration, gifts and awards retain their earlier definitions. | Principal explanation in 12 through **E13**'s food, tools and reserves. ### How the governing motive changes

The comparison should remain visible in the main explanation, with fuller classifications in the notes. Beneficence (*upkar*) names the positive contribution to others' fulfilment that the later rows develop through understanding, teaching and participation. Here *manav* means humane living in the source's technical sense, not simply an ordinary or statistically typical person. *Jeev-chetna* describes a human orientation organised around the body and sensory concerns. Its two classified forms, *pashu-manav* and *rakshas-manav*, together with manav, dev-manav and divya-manav make the five-category account. Use the terms to explain the philosophical distinctions; a person's income, family size, job, illness or a single good or bad action cannot establish their category.

The familiar concerns recur, but the source does not preserve the same three motives as an unchanged classification at every level. Putreshana, vitteshana and lokeshana are stated together for manav; lokeshana receives the governing role for dev-manav; divya-manav is described through coexistence and freedom from eshana. The comparative movement from bodily preoccupations through the three eshanas and lokeshana to universal well-being is also explicit in Paribhasha Samhita, printed p. 2. Explain this direction without making it a guaranteed biography or a scale measured by possessions, children or reputation.

Eshana-mukti must not be translated as the loss of all desire, thought, expectation or action. The source continues to describe asha and all the activities of jeevan in awakened living. Dev-manav's use of people and material resources also makes clear that a changed governing motive is compatible with practical participation. Keep the source's distinctions of understanding, its expression and its scope; do not repeat the mistaken ladder in which only divya-manav has all ten activities.

### Developing the recurring examples

Use the linked household, learning and water-maintenance scenes rather than introducing a new set of exemplary people. In **E14**, ask what the adult wants for the child and why: comparison and status can be contrasted with capability, justice and responsible participation. In **E13**, ask what the household seeks resources for and how it obtains and uses them. In **E17–E18**, ask what recognition is doing: concentrating personal prestige, making dependable contribution visible, or enabling understanding and humane arrangements to continue through others. At each return, identify the perspective, motive, action and actual fulfilment together.

Chapter 20 can revisit these situations side by side to explain the four orientations. Give the source account first and make the proposed application explicit. The same outward act, such as providing a tool or teaching a child, does not by itself reveal the governing motive or certify manav, dev-manav or divya-manav. Avoid turning the household into a staged ascent through categories; its everyday questions help readers understand the distinctions and pursue study.

## Chapter plans

The guiding questions below direct chapter writing; they need not appear as repeated headings in the finished book. Each question should govern the selection and order of explanation. The opening scenes (`E01`–`E20`) are proposed illustrations designed at the natural scale of each chapter's concepts, not reports of historical events or examples attributed to Shri A. Nagraj. Explain the relationship between everyday illustrations and completed realisation clearly where needed, avoiding repetitive paragraph-by-paragraph disclaimers.

### 1. What Are We Really Looking For?

**Guiding question:** What kind of continuing fulfilment can human life hold?

Begin with Meera finishing a handcrafted wooden table on a Saturday afternoon, enjoying the satisfaction of useful making (`E01`). Let that moment open a wider inquiry when she contrasts three distinct kinds of satisfaction in the same afternoon: physical craft and bodily comfort, clearing up a lingering misunderstanding with her niece Anu about her college path (true resolution, *samadhan*), and reviewing the household's seasonal food and repair supplies (sufficiency, *samriddhi*). Explore the wish for continuing happiness, the place of bodily comfort and passing sensory pleasure, the need to understand, and the importance of dependable relationships. Introduce four connected aspirations in ordinary language: clarity and resolution within ourselves (*samadhan*), assured provision in the family (*samriddhi*), fearlessness and trust in society (*abhay*), and coexistence with nature (*sah-astitva*). Give their source terms once, with their meanings, so later chapters can return to a familiar thread. Establish the book's method here: consider the account as a proposal, ask what its words mean, examine its reasoning, and investigate its implications in living.

### 2. The Body and the Conscious Self

**Guiding question:** How can we understand and fulfil the different needs within one human life?

Use the book's single broken-timing and dinner scene (`E02`): Ravi returns late at 9 p.m. after promising to relieve Meera with Kamala's evening care at 7 p.m. Contrast feeding a hungry person a hot meal with repairing the broken trust and exhaustion caused by a missed commitment. Present Madhyasth Darshan's account of the human being as the joint life of a material body (*sharir*) and an enduring conscious self (*jeevan*). Explain their different requirements and their close cooperation in sensing, acting, and living. Bodily requirements such as food, rest, and shelter call for appropriate material provision in finite, assessable quantities—each sense organ and the body as a whole have a definite natural capacity (*KD* p. 114). The wish for understanding, trust, and continuing happiness calls for a qualitative fulfilment in *jeevan*. Explain from *Jeevan Vidya* (*JV* p. 163) that the transience of sensory pleasure is not a design flaw in the body, but an alarm and an inspiration for awakening toward continuous happiness. Contrast attempts to seek relief after work through sensory indulgence or intoxication with true restfulness (*vishram*), which arises from family harmony and inner resolution (*KD* pp. 12–13; *AA* p. 23).

### 3. Our Inner Life: Expectation, Thought, and Desire

**Guiding question:** Can expectations, thoughts and desires find a coherent direction through understanding?

Control the chapter's density through an authentic ethical and economic dilemma (`E03`) rather than repeating the late-arrival scene: Meera's woodworking cooperative is offered a lucrative rush contract that would require using unseasoned timber under dark varnish and delaying promised benches for a local school, while at home Anu faces peer pressure to spend her stipend on status-signalling clothes and gadgets. Follow what each person expects (*asha*), considers (*vichar*), and desires or visualises (*ichha*). Introduce imagination (*kalpanasheelta*) and freedom of action (*karm-swatantrata*). Explain from *Avartansheel Arthshastra* (*AA* pp. 17–18, 95) why, when the higher faculties of comprehension (*buddhi*) and realisation (*atma*) are not yet awakened, *jeevan* operates through four-and-a-half activities and treats sensory tasting and selection (*aswadan–chayan*) as its main business—making the loop of new tastes and status forms appear inexhaustible. Show how inner discontent in *jeevan* is misread as a shortage of material goods, causing the expected satiation point (*tripti bindu*) to recede like a mirage whenever income rises (*AA* p. 78; *KD* p. 115).

Introduce natural acceptance (*sahaj swikriti*) through the source's affirmative account: *jeevan* naturally accepts justice, dharma, and truth even before their meanings are fully understood (*AVD* p. 156). Present both complete evaluative triplets together: pleasantness, bodily benefit, and gain (*priya–hita–labha*); and justice, dharma, and truth (*nyaya–dharma–satya*). Apply each whole basis of evaluation to the workshop contract decision, distinguishing rightful remuneration for useful labour from exploitative profit (*labha*). Once the contrast is clear, introduce *jeev-chetna* and *manav-chetna*, along with the four bodily preoccupations (*ahar, nidra, bhaya, maithun*), the three obsessions (*kam-unmad, bhog-unmad, labha-unmad*), and the three humane motives (*putreshana, vitteshana, lokeshana*). Close by tracing the four aspects of *karma* (*kayik, vachik, manasik, asha-vichar-ichha*) directly through the cooperative's refusal of the deceptive order and its counter-proposal for honest seasoned-wood delivery. Keep the complete five-faculty, ten-activity table in the chapter notes.

### 4. Existence Is Coexistence

**Guiding question:** What reality do we belong to, and how can we understand it?

Begin in the quiet courtyard reading corner (`E04`): Anu's river stone paperweight, the wooden chair, cooling tea in a clay cup, a potted basil plant, a sparrow, and family members conversing. Widen attention from these bounded, active units (*ikai*) to the all-pervading, activity-free reality (*satta*) present within, around, and between them. Make the ontological distinction clear: *satta* is not empty air or the gap left between objects, but the pervasive ground in which every material and conscious unit is submerged (*dooba*), surrounded (*ghira*), and soaked (*bheega*)—the state of *sampriktata*. Build an experiential bridge by contrasting everything that has boundaries, changes, and acts with the boundless, activity-free expanse that neither resists motion nor runs out of room, yet renders every unit energised (*urjasampanna*), self-regulated (*niyantrit*), and active (*kriyasheel*). Explain the name *Madhyasth Darshan* through mediation, balance, and direct understanding of reality, and introduce law (*niyam*) in activity.

### 5. Our Place in Nature

**Guiding question:** What becomes possible when human participation is guided by understanding?

Remain in the courtyard garden (`E05`) with soil and stones, water, the climbing bean vine, the neighbourhood dog, and the household members. Introduce the four orders of nature (*padarth, pran, jeev, gyan avastha*) and examine their four inseparable aspects: form (*rupa*), properties or relational effects (*guna*), characteristic nature (*swabhava*), and cumulative innateness (*dharma*: existence, existence + growth, + the will to live, + happiness through understanding). Explain law, regulation, and balance (*niyam, niyantran, santulan*), and introduce material (*jada*) and conscious (*chaitanya*) nature alongside development toward constitutional completeness (*vikas-kram* and *vikas*) and awakening (*jagriti-kram* and *jagriti*). Highlight the foundational insight from *Avartansheel Arthshastra* (*AA* pp. 41–42, 117): the first three orders of nature are already in self-regulated balance and mutually complementary with one another and with human beings, whereas the fourth order (humanity), endowed with imagination and freedom of action, is not automatically in balance by birth and achieves complementarity only through knowledge (*janna*) and cultured disposition (*sanskar*). Reserve the meal/birthday celebration scene for Chapter 7 so each chapter has a distinct setting.

### 6. From Assumption to Understanding

**Guiding question:** How does something we can repeat become something we understand and live?

Set `E06` at a weekend study dialogue with Ravi, Meera, and Anu where Anu brings two unexamined assumptions (*manna*): a cynical college commonplace ("everyone ultimately acts out of self-interest, and human wants are infinite") and a memorised philosophical slogan ("existence is coexistence"). Show how neither a borrowed cynicism nor a repeated teacher's phrase is yet knowledge (*janna*). Use the subsection **Knowing, Believing, Recognising, and Fulfilling** (*janna, manna, pahchanna, nirvah karna*) to show how unexamined beliefs (*manyata*) that diverge from reality constitute delusion (*bhram*), whereas understanding reality (*satya*) grounds recognition and fulfilment across the three contents of knowledge: knowledge of coexistence (*astitva-darshan-gyan*), knowledge of the conscious self (*jeevan-gyan*), and knowledge of humane conduct (*manaviyata-poorna-acharan-gyan*). Trace the learning path of listening (*shravan*), rational reflection (*manan*), and grasping meaning (*arth-bodh*), alongside study (*adhyayan*), experiment and practice (*prayog aur abhyas*), direct recognition (*sakshatkar*), comprehension (*bodh*), and realisation with evidence in living (*anubhav aur praman*).

### 7. What Makes Life Valuable?

**Guiding question:** What constitutes fulfilment within ourselves, between people and through useful things?

Split `E07` across two natural settings suited to the scale of the thirty values. First, during Kamala's seventieth birthday meal, Anu and Kamala reflect on the difference between fleeting sensory excitement and the four *jeevan* values—happiness (*sukh*), peace (*shanti*), contentment (*santosh*), and bliss (*anand*)—explained from *Avartansheel Arthshastra* (*AA* pp. 20–21, 92) as the specific harmonies achieved when each inner faculty aligns with the higher faculty adjacent to it (*mun* with *vritti*, *vritti* with *chitta*, *chitta* with *buddhi*, and *buddhi* with *atma*). Second, follow Iqbal mentoring his apprentice Leela in the workshop to illustrate the six human values: steadfastness (*dhirata*), courage (*virata*), and generosity (*udarata*), together with kindness (*daya*), grace (*kripa*), and compassion (*karuna*) according to a learner's capacities and means. Present the complete thirty-value architecture and its dedication chain (*AA* pp. 20–24, 92): two object values (usefulness and artistic value) produced for family use are dedicated to expressing the nine expressed values (*shisht mulya*) and fulfilling the nine established values (*sthapit mulya*), which cultivate the six human values and culminate in the four *jeevan* values.

### 8. Trust, Respect, and Justice

**Guiding question:** What makes dependable and mutually fulfilling relationships possible?

Use `E08`—the family's Care Review for Kamala—as the single place in the book where Meera's unseen coordination load is surfaced, evaluated, and rebalanced. Walk through the four elements of justice (*nyaya*): recognising relationship (*sambandh ki pahchan*), fulfilling its values (*mulyon ka nirvah*), right evaluation (*mulyankan*), and mutual satisfaction (*ubhay-tripti*). Distinguish intention, competence, and actual fulfilment, and explain respect (*samman*) as right evaluation (*sahi mulyankan*) rather than over-, under-, or mis-evaluation (*adhik, kam, anyatha mulyankan*). Rather than marching all nine value-pairs through one small errand, group the nine established values and their nine expressed counterparts into three natural relational horizons: (1) the everyday foundation of dependability (*vishwas–saujanyata, samman–sauhardrata, sneh–nishtha*), (2) intergenerational nurture and gratitude (*mamata–udarata, vatsalya–sahajata, gaurav–saralata, kritagyata–saumyata*), and (3) orientation toward awakening (*shraddha–pujyata* and *prem–ananyata*, illustrated through the household's relation to their guiding teacher and undivided belonging).

### 9. Family: Our First Place of Shared Living

**Guiding question:** How can humane living become something another generation learns and continues?

Follow a Saturday across the wider household and workshop web (`E09`): Kamala advising a former student who now teaches (*guru–shishya*), Iqbal and his wife fostering Leela's orphaned younger brother (illustrating how *mata–pita / putra–putri* rests on continuing nurture, protection, and education alongside biological birth, *MVD* pp. 244–246), Ravi and Meera as partners (*pati–patni*), siblings and accepted sibling-like peers (*bhai–bahin*, *MVD* pp. 344–345), colleagues in the workshop (*sathi–sahyogi*), friends supporting one another's resolution and prosperity (*mitra*), and Ravi assisting an injured delivery rider in the street to distinguish voluntary contact (*sampark*) from definite relationship (*sambandh*, *MVD* pp. 61–62, 235–236) within the wider order (*vyavastha*). Develop *putreshana* as the humane aspiration for continuity—raising and educating capable participants in orderliness rather than seeking dynastic status or possessive security.

### 10. Freedom, Responsibility, and Character

**Guiding question:** What makes our freedom dependable in practice?

Combine Iqbal's honest pump-valve repair and an overpaid bank transfer (`E10`) with a contrast between Iqbal's remunerated repair work and Kamala's and Ravi's fee-free teaching and community health support (`E11`). Under **Justice, Resolution, and Truth**, bring together *nyaya–dharma–satya* as a whole evaluative triad governing behaviour, thought, and realisation. Under **Values, Character, and Ethics**, present the three pillars of humane character (*manaviya charitra*):
1. **Rightful wealth (*swa-dhana*):** resources acquired through one's own physical labour on natural abundance, willing gift (*daan/bhent*) from a prosperous giver, or public award (*puraskar*) for an original contribution (*AA* p. 140; *AVD* p. 170). Enrich this with the source distinction between the **two forms of service (*seva*)** (*AA* pp. 24, 29, 45–46, 130–140): remuneration-primary service (*pratiphal-pradhan seva*, such as repair and maintenance earning *parishramik* as *swa-dhana*) and benevolence-primary service (*upkar-pradhan seva*, such as teaching, health care, elder care, and council service, offered without commercial fee because the practitioner is sustained by a prosperous household, in contrast to the "seven kinds of trade" that commodify education, medicine, and justice).
2. **Chastity in relationship (*swa-nari / swa-purusha*):** faithfulness within marriage and dignified, trustworthy conduct with all men and women.
3. **Kindness (*daya*):** supporting others' capacity and receptivity without interference.

Explain ethics (*niti*) across the three source policies (*KD* p. 67): *dharm-neeti* (right use of body, mind, and wealth), *arth-neeti* (production beyond need), and *raj-neeti* (protection of humanity and natural assets). Integrate the threefold moral responsibility of action from *Karm Darshan* (*KD* pp. 1–2, 117): doing (*krit*), commissioning (*karit*), and consenting or endorsing (*anumodit*).

### 11. Caring for the Body

**Guiding question:** How can bodily care support meaningful participation across changing capacities?

Follow Kamala's adapted morning routine (`E12`—slicing vegetables at a seated table with an easy-grip knife so she remains an active participant in the household) alongside Ravi correcting a cycle of weekday overwork and festive over-indulgence. Explain bodily health (*swasthya*) through the body's assimilation and expression of needed elements, restraint (*sanyam*) as a regulated programme of nourishment (*poshan*), protection (*sanrakshan*), and right use (*sadupyog*) of the body as an instrument of *jeevan*, and *sanyamata* as integrating *priya–hita–labha* within *nyaya–dharma–satya*. Keep bodily vulnerability, ageing, and disability within dignified family care without treating health as a measure of human worth or level of awakening.

### 12. Work, Wealth, and Having Enough

**Guiding question:** What would assured provision make possible for a family and its wider contribution?

Anchor `E13` in Ravi, Meera, and Kamala conducting their seasonal household assessment of food, tools, repair reserves, and sharing, contrasted with a cousin whose rising corporate income leaves him perpetually anxious because his desires are driven by status comparison. Ground the chapter thoroughly in *Avartansheel Arthshastra* and *Karm Darshan*:
- **Resources (*artha*):** introduce the full triad of body, mind, and wealth (*tan, man, dhan*, *JV* p. 151) and explain why **human labour (*shrama*) on natural abundance is primary capital** (*AA* p. 73).
- **Real wealth (*dhan*) versus symbolic currency (*prateek mudra*):** define real wealth as tangible goods possessing usefulness and artistic value (*AA* pp. 25, 45, 52, 105). Explain why natural goods have a finite shelf life that invites timely use and sharing, whereas symbolic currency never rots, becoming a vehicle for the three allurements (*pralobhan*) of hoarding, black-marketing, and tax evasion.
- **Assessing family need:** distinguish **common aspirations (*samanya akanksha*)**—nourishing food (*ahar*), dwelling (*avas*), adornment/apparel (*alankar*), health, and education—from **special aspirations (*mahatvakanksha*)**—shared communication, broadcasting, and transport facilities (*suvidha*) for civic and cultural participation (*AA* pp. 26, 93, 112).
- **The structure of prosperity (*samriddhi*) and benevolence (*upkar*):** show how a household in which two to four members engage in skilled production easily produces more than the family consumes (*KD* p. 115), and how **benevolence (*upkar*)** serves as the bridge that channels surplus into family **use (*upyog*)**, societal **right use (*sadupyog*)**, and **purposeful use (*prayojansheelta*)** for universal order (*AA* pp. 72, 94, 116; *KD* pp. 115–117), fulfilling *vitteshana* and freeing the household from both deprivation (*daridrata*) and profit-mania (*labhonmad*).

### 13. Education for a Whole Life

**Guiding question:** How can understanding become another person's own capacity to live and teach?

Replace minor domestic reminders with a substantial educational setting (`E14`): a weekend community learning workshop hosted by Ravi, Meera, and Iqbal for students (Anu and her younger cousin) and young apprentices (Leela). Contrast exam-cramming and competitive ranking (*ku-sanskar*) with holistic education and cultured formation (*shiksha–sanskar*). Under **Knowledge, Wisdom, and Science**, unite *gyan* (knowledge of why and what—coexistence, *jeevan*, and humane conduct), *vivek* (wisdom discerning human purpose and the laws of living), and *vigyan* (directional science of how to accomplish those purposes in material work and time). Integrate the *Avartansheel Arthshastra* account of how *jeevan* powers project outward (*paravartan*) into the hand's dexterity (*hastalaghav*) and reflect inward (*pratyavartan*) to strengthen the self's faculties (*AA* pp. 18–19, 131), developing across **skill (*kushalata*)**, **proficiency (*nipunata*)**, and **scholarliness (*panditya*)** (*AA* pp. 49–50). Explain why every young person in a humane curriculum learns **more than one occupation** across agriculture, cow-care, and craft/industry (*AA* p. 48), eliminating occupational fear and dependency.

### 14. Beauty in What We Make and How We Live

**Guiding question:** How does understanding find expression in useful, thoughtful and beautiful making?

Set `E15` in Meera and Anu's design of reading benches, shelving, and a printed opening invitation for a neighbourhood reading room. Explain the two object values—usefulness (*upayogita mulya*) and artistic value (*kala mulya*)—showing how durability, ergonomics, proportion, surface finish, and welcoming spatial arrangement complete an object's human purpose without wasteful ornament or status display. Extend the account to thoughtful speech, writing, and celebration that make humane values tangible in shared life.

### 15. Science and Technology with a Human Purpose

**Guiding question:** How can technical capability serve clearly understood human purposes?

Unite two concrete technical decisions in `E16`: (1) neighbours choosing between a high-extraction borewell pump and a repairable, solar-assisted system matched to aquifer recharge; and (2) Iqbal and Meera designing an original wood-seasoning and joinery jig (*maulikta*) in their workshop. Use the workshop invention to explain why, in Madhyasth Darshan, scientific research (*anusandhan*) and public dissemination (*prasar*) are inseparable—like a farmer sharing improved seed rather than locking it behind a monopoly patent (*AA* pp. 56–58; *JV* pp. 88–89). Show how the local council honours their innovation with public recognition and a material award (*puraskar*, a legitimate form of *swa-dhana*, *AA* pp. 140, 144), while open sharing lowers unit labour time (*shram mulya*) for every workshop (*AA* pp. 118, 145). Formulate the **twofold criterion for machines and automation** (*AA* pp. 107, 118; *JVD* p. 35): technology succeeds when it reduces drudgery and required hours while keeping every healthy adult an active producer, avoiding the cultural error of shifting from a *working mindset* to a *managing mindset*. Introduce the foundational principle from *Karm Darshan* (*KD* p. 139) on **trusting instruments (*yantra*)**: a machine is trusted only to the extent its law-governed operation is understood by a human being who remains responsible for its maintenance and right use.

### 16. Working Together with a Shared Purpose

**Guiding question:** What becomes possible when competence and responsibility are shared?

Set `E17` inside Meera and Iqbal's six-person Craft and Repair Cooperative during a busy month when a senior coordinator falls ill. Show how the team diagnoses bottlenecks, redistributes quality checks, pricing, and apprentice guidance through complementarity (*purakta*) and collegial partnership (*sathi–sahyogi*), and sees Leela step forward to coordinate deliveries and mentor a newer trainee. Develop self-governance (*swa-shasan*) as orderliness grounded in shared understanding, transparent responsibilities, and mutual evaluation rather than boss-subordinate coercion.

### 17. An Undivided Human Society

**Guiding question:** How can humane belonging and dependable provision extend beyond familiar people?

Anchor `E18` in a multi-village (*Gram-Samuh*) seasonal survey and cooperative **exchange-reserve (*vinimay kosh*)** meeting where Ravi, Meera, and neighbouring village delegates coordinate grain, oilseeds, textiles, and repair tools. Ground the chapter's economic and institutional architecture in *Avartansheel Arthshastra* and *Vyavaharatmak Janvad*:
- **Family-based village survey:** show how coordination begins from each household's assessed annual needs and production capacities (*AA* pp. 122–125).
- **Need-based sharing within the household and equal labour-value exchange between villages:** explain why sharing inside the family is unconditional and need-based, whereas exchange between families and across village exchange-reserves takes place on the basis of equal **value of labour (*shram mulya*)**—evaluated across skill, proficiency, work pace (*karya-gati*), time, and means (*AA* pp. 66, 76, 119, 136–138, 145)—turning barter into an exact, hoarding-free clearing ledger without speculative middlemen.
- **Five social committees and nine village production branches:** present the five functions in source order (*shiksha–sanskar, nyaya–suraksha, utpadan–karya, vinimay–kosh, swasthya–sanyam*) alongside the nine practical branches of village production (*AA* pp. 133–136) and the division of industrial scale across the ten council tiers (*Parivar Sabha* to *Vishva-Parivar-Rajya Sabha*, where heavy transport, telecommunications, and solar equipment are stewarded by regional and national councils, *AA* pp. 29–30, 115).
- **The three-stage transition:** explain the practical pathway from present market conditions (*AA* pp. 142–143)—Stage 1: cooperative collection and direct distribution at wholesale reference prices without middleman markup or hoarding; Stage 2: transparent cost-and-labour accounting across federated reserves; Stage 3: full labour-hour (*shram-mulya*) parity.
- **Connected computing and intelligent coordination instruments:** explain (following *AA* pp. 132, 138 and *Prosperity Economics* §8) how networked ledgers and AI-assisted analytical tools help part-time producer-representatives aggregate household surveys, maintain dual transition ledgers, and match multi-tier surpluses and deficits without creating a permanent technocratic bureaucracy—while keeping all normative judgements of need, justice, and purpose strictly in awakened human hands.

### 18. Living Responsibly with the Earth

**Guiding question:** How can our participation maintain the natural conditions that support life?

Follow `E19` as Ravi and Meera visit a neighbouring mixed farm and biogas-linked craft unit that supplies their household and workshop. Unite both complementary meanings of cyclicality (*avartansheelta*): (1) the **human provision cycle**, in which natural raw materials are transformed by human labour into useful goods and returned through right use to nourish the next generation of producers (*JV* p. 154; *AA* pp. 26, 106); and (2) **cyclical resource use in nature**, using material assets strictly in proportion to Earth's regenerative cycle (*avartansheel prakriya*, *AA* pp. 142, 146; *KD* p. 7). Contrast **cyclical production (*utpadan*)** with **non-cyclical extraction (*dohana*)** of coal, petroleum, and deep geological ores, drawing on Nagraj's warning that stripping subterranean strata disrupts Earth's internal balance and radiation/heat regulation (*AA* pp. 30–31, 77–78). Present the source blueprint for **cyclical energy**—flowing-water hydroelectricity, solar energy, wind, livestock biogas, and closed-loop plant oils (*AA* pp. 31–32)—and **integrated agriculture, forestry, and animal husbandry** (*AA* pp. 70–71, 134–135) where soil fertility, water tables, trees, animals, and human craft replenish one another.

### 19. Life, Death, and What Continues

**Guiding question:** What continues, and what significance does understanding have within that account?

Anchor `E20` in the family's annual remembrance of Ravi's late father, Master Keshav, whose workbench Meera now uses and whose notebook of reflections Anu reads. Use this concrete intergenerational setting to distinguish three distinct continuities: (1) bodily composition and biological lineage returning to the material and vital orders; (2) the humane tradition (*manaviya parampara*) of knowledge, skills, and institutions passed from person to person through education and example; and (3) the continuity of the individual conscious self (*jeevan*) and its accumulated *sanskar* across bodily death. Explain *kaivalya* first through realised understanding, comprehensive resolution, and active participation in universal order, and include the *Anubhavatmak Adhyatmvad* (*AVD* pp. 260–262) qualification that upon completed awakening further bodily journeys are no longer necessary while awakened *jeevan* activity continues.

### 20. Awakening in Everyday Life

**Guiding question:** What does fulfilment of the human possibility mean throughout living?

Return to an ordinary day across the household, workshop, learning forum, and village exchange-reserve to synthesise the book's journey. Include the substantive comparison **What Gives Our Motives Their Direction?**, contrasting the five categories (*pashu-manav, rakshas-manav, manav, dev-manav, divya-manav*) across perspective (*drishti*), governing propensity (*pravritti*—from taste-driven *ruchimulak* consumption in *jeev-chetna* to value-oriented *mulyamulak* and goal-oriented *lakshyamulak* living and the beneficence tendency *upkar-pravritti* in *manav-chetna*, *AA* pp. 23–24, 101–102), and the three *eshanas* (*putreshana, vitteshana, lokeshana*, culminating in *eshana-mukti* in *divya-manav*). Present the **chain of self-regulation** from *Avartansheel Arthshastra* (*AA* p. 95): sensory tasting regulated by deliberation, deliberation by contemplation, contemplation by comprehension/enlightenment, comprehension by realisation, and realisation saturated in coexistence—bringing all ten *jeevan* activities into harmony. Connect the three completions (*gathan-poornata, kriya-poornata, acharan-poornata*), freedom from delusion (*bhram-mukti*), and *adhyatma* back to Babaji's opening wish that all human beings live as *dev-manav*.

## Writing approach

Use the connected-group map alongside the concept-coverage map and example catalogue. For each principal explanation, the writer should be able to identify the complete group, state what relates its members, show that relationship through one scene, and explain what a later chapter develops. Let a short recap or small table gather the group after its members have been explained. The glossary should cross-reference the group and its main chapter, so finding asha also leads to vichar and ichha, and finding nyaya also leads to dharma and satya in their shared evaluative context. Use the same order when recalling a group unless the source context requires a different order.

Each chapter should move naturally from a familiar situation or an unanswered question into an explanation and its consequences in living. Begin by recalling the distinction the previous chapter established, then name the question that remains. The final prose should read as a connected book. Use the recurring setting where it clarifies a new point, and make independent examples recognisably separate. Illustrative scenes remain clearly fictional or hypothetical.

Use a flexible rhythm: **recognisable situation, open inquiry, philosophical explanation returned to living**. First let the reader notice something; then ask what it means and what would fulfil the need; then offer the concept and revisit the situation. Four questions guide the writing: What human aspiration is present? What understanding is needed, including the complete connected concepts? What possibility becomes intelligible? What would its fulfilment require in competence, conduct, material provision and shared arrangements? Emphasise the conditions relevant to that chapter. These are authoring questions, not a mandatory sequence of printed subsections. Reflection opens study; it does not turn the reader’s preferred answer into proof of the doctrine.

For example, Chapter 8 can begin with two partners and an elderly parent already sharing dependable care, then responding to changed needs. Ask what their relationships call for, what each person needs and can contribute, and how they will know whether the arrangement fulfils care and dignity. Introduce justice through recognition, value fulfilment, evaluation and mutual satisfaction, then return to what they actually do and any adjustment needed. This supplies a recognisable situation and a philosophical distinction without making any character merely a mouthpiece.

Use ordinary English first. Retain *jeevan* and *satta* where their meanings cannot be carried adequately by a casual synonym; explain each on first use. Use the essential-concepts map to introduce other source terms after their meanings become useful. Keep the glossary concise and cross-reference related meanings rather than repeating whole explanations. Detailed classifications and primary-text pointers belong in chapter notes; an index of terms can make them easy to find without becoming a prerequisite for following the argument.

Before expanding a catalogue scene, identify its main question, the aspiration it reveals, the concept it introduces, the assumptions open to examination and the fulfilment to be evaluated. Let positive accomplishment, care, learning and making have weight in their own right. A satisfying achievement can open further inquiry without first becoming a disappointment. A later appearance should add a distinction, change a responsibility or widen participation. Let work, care and resource constraints have real consequences; characters need not reach easy agreement. Include different ages, abilities and circumstances naturally in the recurring setting. Read sample scenes aloud and ask readers to explain both the concept and what the example establishes. If they retain only “repair immediately,” “space is empty air,” “justice is agreement” or “liberation means spending less,” revise the explanation even if a qualification elsewhere is correct.

With twenty chapters in a working length of 40,000–50,000 words, draft around one main reader objective per chapter. Chapters 3–6, 17, and 19 carry especially dense material; use the objectives above to decide what belongs in the main explanation and what belongs in source notes. Test the length and clarity with sample chapters before treating the word estimate as a fixed limit.

State the philosophy's positions clearly within the book's established frame. Preserve the difference between a philosophical claim, an observation available in everyday life, and an example constructed to illuminate it. The reader should be invited to understand and examine the account, with no suggestion that agreeing with a teacher or performing a brief exercise proves the whole philosophy.

Short reflection prompts may close selected chapters. They should invite observation, conversation, and examination of action. They are aids to learning, not tests that certify realisation. A few simple figures can show the human being's joint life, the four orders, the process of justice, and the five social functions.

Plan four principal figures: body and *jeevan* with their distinct requirements and joint participation in Chapter 2; the four orders with their cumulative *dharmas* and examples of complementarity in Chapter 5; recognition, fulfilment, evaluation, and mutual satisfaction in Chapter 8; and the five coordinated social functions in source order in Chapter 17. The last figure should show shared purposes and coordination across council levels, with the ten-level enumeration in a separate compact table. Use ordinary labels, give each figure a short explanatory caption, and distinguish an illustrative process from a claimed causal mechanism. Chapter openings and transitions can recall the four aspirations without adding another technical diagram.

Reader testing belongs after sample prose exists. Use Chapters 3, 8 and 17 as an initial range of inner activity, relationship and social organisation, then test the denser continuity explanation in Chapter 19. Ask readers to explain the chapter’s particular human possibility and its conditions, explain a connected group in their own words, apply it to a changed situation, identify what the scene demonstrates and what the philosophy separately proposes, and point to wording they could not follow. Record misunderstanding and revise the relevant explanation; a reader's agreement with the philosophy is not evidence of comprehension. Check that revised scenes retain practical weight through care, work, provision and learning rather than relying on exceptional moral tests. When the relevant sample chapters are drafted, also test for confusion between satta and empty air (4), relationship fulfilment and a role label (9), prosperity and a bank balance (12), or kaivalya and withdrawal from present responsibility (19). In Chapter 3, check that readers can follow expectation, thought and desire before asking them to recall any fuller classification.

The back matter should reconnect readers with the book: glossary entries give a short contextual meaning, related groups and principal chapters; chapter notes give editions, precise page conventions, translation decisions and sources for claims; a term index, if included, directs readers to the substantive explanations. Acknowledgements carry the portrait credit/licence, translation attribution and the supplied song's provenance. Final layout should preserve the opening portrait/wish before the preface and the Hindi song after all back matter. These production elements use the design and evidence already specified here.

## Brief preface

We spend much of our lives learning how to earn, manage a household, care for others, and find our place in the world. Alongside these efforts, certain questions keep returning. What makes happiness dependable? What does it mean to understand another person? How much is enough? How can we live in ways that support both our own fulfilment and the lives around us?

This book introduces Madhyasth Darshan, the philosophy presented by Shri A. Nagraj, through these questions. *The Human Possibility* names the capacity to understand ourselves and the reality we belong to, fulfil our relationships, and participate consciously in humane living. What becomes possible through understanding, and what does its fulfilment require? That question connects the chapters that follow. The name Madhyasth Darshan leads into the study of mediation and orderliness in existence, and of understanding reality. Chapter 4 develops these meanings of *madhyasth* and *darshan* as part of the account itself.

Babaji wanted to see every human being live as *dev-manav*: with awakened understanding, expressed in helping others understand and in nurturing humane living together. His endeavour was to make this understanding and way of living available to everyone. The wish on the preceding page expresses this purpose. This book introduces the account of human awakening through which he proposed its fulfilment.

At its heart is the proposal that existence is coexistence. Things and beings are distinct, yet their existence is inseparable from the reality in which they are present. Human beings have the capacity to understand this and to give their participation a conscious direction. Our desire for happiness, our need for trust, and our responsibility towards nature can then be understood as parts of one life.

The pages that follow begin with familiar experiences: satisfaction in useful work, bodily needs, affection, learning, disagreement and the wish to provide for those we care about. From there, they develop a wider picture of understanding within the person, prosperity in the family, trust in society, and responsible participation with the Earth. Spiritual fulfilment belongs within this whole, finding expression in how we think, relate, work and help understanding continue. This participation also calls for practical ability, dependable conduct, material means and arrangements we sustain together.

No prior study of philosophy is assumed. Unfamiliar words are explained when they become useful. The reader is invited to consider each proposal carefully, discuss it, and examine what it would mean in living.

The hope behind this book is that a clearer understanding of life can make our everyday responsibilities more intelligible and their fulfilment more joyful. The places where this understanding matters are already before us: the people we live with, the work we undertake, and the world we share.

### Verified decisions for chapter sources

The five source-analysis questions previously tracked under BOOK-01 were resolved for this book's planning scope on 2 October 2026. The decisions below govern the concept maps, scenes and chapter instructions. They specify what the eventual chapter may explain and where its source support lies; new claims introduced while drafting will still need their own passage checks.

#### Natural acceptance and understanding

AVD PDF p. 156 explicitly identifies justice, dharma and truth as naturally accepted by jeevan even without clear understanding. In that passage, dharma includes universal orderliness and undivided society. Page 70 distinguishes accepting orderliness from being able to explain it; p. 79 explains how study, recognition, comprehension and realisation establish acceptance as sanskar. These passages support a positive account more specific than a general wish to live harmoniously.

Chapters 3 and 6 should therefore keep three distinctions together: what is naturally accepted, what a person presently assumes or prefers, and what the person has come to understand and fulfil. In E03, each person can seek justice and clarity while evaluating a workshop or personal decision. Their shared orientation makes inquiry meaningful; it does not make every immediate assumption correct in advance. The return in E08 should show learning changing recognition and fulfilment. Avoid treating natural acceptance as either an arbitrary preference or a guarantee that every immediate judgement is true.

#### Health and restraint

Paribhasha Samhita printed p. 216 / PDF p. 224 defines swasthya through the body's assimilation and expression of necessary elements. Its swasthya–sanyam entry joins a body capable of expressing awakening with jeevan as the body's seer. Printed p. 207 / PDF p. 215 defines sanyam as a regulated programme, sanyamata through humane thought, behaviour and work, and sanyat through the integration of priya–hita–labha within nyaya–dharma–satya. MVD pp. 260–261 locates health–restraint among the five social functions.

Chapter 11 should explain bodily health, understanding-guided regulation and their joint contribution to participation. Nourishment, protection and right use give E12 its practical content; they do not replace the fuller definitions. The scene should show changed expectations, thoughtful work and dependable help as well as physical care. This account supplies no clinical regimen and does not establish that an ill or disabled person lacks understanding. Connect the chapter back to Chapter 3's complete evaluative groups and forward to collective provision in Chapter 17.

#### Sanskar, individual continuity and humane tradition

JV printed pp. 19 and 53–54 / PDF pp. 20 and 54–55 affirms continuing jeevan and further embodiment in its discussion of delusion. Printed pp. 48–49 / PDF pp. 49–50 distinguishes changing bodily composition and learning that another person can understand and convey further. MVD pp. 90 and 94 gives both the positive completeness-oriented use of sanskar and the broader su-sanskar/ku-sanskar distinction; pp. 179, 187 and 217–218 explains its formation through prior activity, study, behaviour and work, and its establishment in jeevan. SB PDF pp. 236–237 distinguishes bodily lineage from jeevan-centred sanskar and family tradition.

Paribhasha Samhita printed p. 37 / PDF p. 45, under amushmik, explicitly affirms retention of awakened principles after bodily death. AVD PDF pp. 260–262 explains kaivalya through comprehensive resolution, participation in wider orderliness, expression and communication, and continuing awakened activity. Within this account, p. 261 adds that bodily journeys are no longer needed, while awakened activities continue after bodily dissolution. Read together, these support enduring individual jeevan and retained awakening, distinct from the teaching transmitted between different people. Chapters 19–20 must preserve the kaivalya qualification rather than describing re-embodiment as endlessly necessary for every jeevan.

The book should explain these three continuities separately: bodily composition and lineage; individual jeevan and awakened attainment; and understanding made available through human tradition. E09, E14 and E17 give the third a concrete development when a learner independently understands, acts and teaches. The sources checked do not establish intact transfer of every memory, craft skill or habit into a specified next life. MVD p. 90's working English adds a carried-forward gloss that the Hindi does not itself specify as transfer between births; do not make that phrase the basis of a stronger claim. Nor does another birth guarantee progress: MVD pp. 134 and 289–291 keeps study, formation and environment relevant to awakening or decline. These limits define the book's claim; speculative mechanisms of memory transfer are outside its scope. JV’s discussion of further embodiment in delusion and AVD’s kaivalya qualification should be explained in their respective contexts. Do not turn them into a mechanical equation of sanskar with incomplete awakening, a rule that bodily life itself proves incompleteness, or a claim that another birth automatically brings completion.

#### Ten council levels and five social functions

JVD PDF pp. 133–134 gives the general council account and complete five-function group; MVD p. 161 confirms family as the beginning of ten levels. The detailed JVD passages establish the following sequence. The English labels explain relative reach; source names at the wider levels are retained because working translations vary.

| 2. Parivar-samuh sabha | Family-group council, with one representative from each of ten families. | 179–181 |
| 3. Gram/mohalla-parivar sabha | Village or neighbourhood council, represented through family-group councils. | 180–182, 189 |

Teach this sequence together with the complete function group, in source order: shiksha–sanskar, nyaya–suraksha, utpadan–karya, vinimay–kosh, swasthya–sanyam. The ten levels express the reach of participation; the five functions express coordinated kinds of work. JVD PDF p. 189 describes five village committees with locally determined membership, so the ten-person council pattern must not be copied into every committee. Pages 238–239 describes communication and response between local and world-family levels. The Chapter 17 figure should make these connected purposes visible; the full council enumeration can remain a compact table.

Preserve the qualifications in the chapter notes. The tenth-level account permits representatives from mandal-group or mukhya-rajya councils to complete its ten members if ten pradhan-rajya councils are unavailable. The village account allows at least ten family-groups; each village council determines its representatives' tenure with regard to local and shared suitability. These details prevent a rigid population formula or a mathematical fractal claim. Explain recognised understanding, representation and fulfilled responsibility without importing present-day electoral rules or administrative boundaries. In the working translation, pp. 211 and 217 sometimes flatten mukhya-rajya and pradhan-rajya into the same English name; the Hindi distinguishes them. The summary at pp. 151–152 repeats village/neighbourhood at the fourth position; the enumeration at p. 134 and the detailed fourth-level passage at pp. 189–190 establish gram-samuh. Use the detailed account. The village survey and exchange-reserve scene illustrates the responsibilities proposed, not demonstrated success of a universal institutional implementation.

#### Evaluative perspectives and the Ethics study

MVD p. 58 defines priya, hita and labha; p. 67 gives the six perspectives in two complete groups; pp. 102 and 126 treats nyaya–dharma–satya together as the humane basis of evaluation. These passages do not establish a one-to-one conversion of pleasantness into justice, health into dharma and profit into truth. MVD pp. 67 and 137 does support a different relationship: justice in behaviour, dharma in thought, and truth in realisation. Keep those functional distinctions while applying the whole humane group to a decision.

The existing Ethics study, §3.2, contains a three-line diagram pairing the two triads term by term. That diagram is not supported by the cited MVD passage and is not a source for the book's mapping. Its surrounding whole-group contrast and §3.3's behaviour/thought/realisation distinction remain useful when checked against MVD. Chapters 3 and 10, the glossary and the figures should use the verified whole-group relationship: bodily benefit and gain are evaluated through justice, dharma and truth together. E03 and E10 make this visible by returning to the same workshop or repair decision through all three humane perspectives. This resolves the Primer's source-use question. Applying the finding to the related study is recorded separately as ST-FIX-02 in [PENDING.md](../PENDING.md#studies); that study has not been revised by this planning review.
